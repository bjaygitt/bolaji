## 24.1 Thinking in systems

A cryptographic system is more than its algorithms. It includes:

- The **keys** and where they live (KMS, HSM, files, devices).
- The **identities** that may use each key (people, workloads, services).
- The **protocols** that carry protected data.
- The **trust anchors** that decide what is believed (root certificates, JWKS endpoints, pinned keys).
- The **processes** around them: issuance, rotation, approval, backup, monitoring and recovery.

Attackers look for the weakest link across all of these. In practice it is almost never the algorithm.

## 24.2 Threat modelling cryptographic systems

Use a structured method. A simple, effective one has five steps:

1. **Draw the data flow.** Where is sensitive data created, stored, transmitted and processed? Mark each **trust boundary**: the points where data crosses between systems, networks or organisations with different levels of trust.
2. **List the keys.** For each protection on the diagram, identify the key, where it lives, who can use it, and how it is rotated.
3. **Ask the key questions** at every boundary:
    - Who can decrypt this data, in practice, including administrators, cloud providers and backups?
    - Who can sign or issue something others will trust?
    - What happens if this key leaks? How would we know? How fast could we replace it?
    - What does this trust anchor accept, and who can change it?
4. **Apply a threat catalogue.** STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege) is a good checklist: spoofing maps to authentication and certificates, tampering to integrity and signatures, repudiation to signing and audit logs, and so on.
5. **Prioritise by blast radius.** A key that protects one record is less important than one that can mint every identity. Spend protection where a single failure has the widest effect.

### Blast radius as a design principle

**Blast radius** is how much is affected if one thing fails. Many of this book's recommendations simply reduce it:

| Design choice | Blast radius reduced |
| --- | --- |
| Per-object DEKs (envelope encryption) | One leaked key exposes one object |
| Per-tenant or per-application KEKs | One misconfigured policy exposes one tenant |
| Separate issuing CAs by purpose | One compromised CA affects one certificate type |
| Name constraints on CAs | A stolen CA key cannot issue for other domains |
| Short-lived credentials and certificates | A leaked credential works for hours, not years |
| Ephemeral key exchange | A stolen server key does not expose past sessions |

## 24.3 Crypto agility

**Crypto agility** is the ability to change algorithms, keys, certificates or providers quickly and safely, without redesigning systems. Every major event in recent cryptographic history has tested it: SHA-1's retirement, the removal of TLS 1.0, ROCA, mass revocations, CA distrusts, and now the post-quantum transition, which requires replacing nearly all public-key cryptography.

Agility has three layers.

### Protocol and format agility

Protocols should negotiate algorithms, carry algorithm identifiers with every key, signature and ciphertext, and allow new options without breaking old clients. TLS 1.3 is a good example: adding the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 group needed no protocol change. Formats should record which algorithm and key version protected each object, so data can be re-protected later.

Agility has a cost: every negotiable option is attack surface, as two decades of downgrade attacks showed. The modern balance is **agile, but with few, strong choices at any time**, and deliberate retirement of old ones.

### Application agility

Applications become rigid when algorithm choices are hard-coded throughout the code. Make them agile by:

- Calling cryptography through a small number of internal libraries or services, not directly throughout the code base.
- Keeping algorithm, key size and key location in configuration.
- Avoiding assumptions about sizes. Post-quantum keys and signatures are much larger than today's (Chapter 26); fixed-length database columns, protocol fields and buffers will break.
- Testing with alternative algorithms in continuous integration.

### Organisational agility

The hardest layer is organisational:

- An accurate **inventory** of keys, certificates and cryptographic usage (a CBOM, Chapter 27).
- **Owners** for every cryptographic asset.
- **Automation** for issuance, rotation and deployment.
- **Rehearsed procedures** for mass replacement.
- **Procurement rules** that require vendors to support algorithm upgrades and publish timelines.

## 24.4 Compliance landscape

Most organisations must satisfy one or more regimes with cryptographic requirements. The table is a map, not legal advice.

| Framework | Scope | Key cryptographic requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PCI DSS v4.0.1** | Payment card data | Strong cryptography for stored and transmitted card data; documented key management with dual control and split knowledge; an inventory of cryptographic suites and protocols; annual review of cryptographic choices |
| **FIPS 140-3 and FIPS-approved algorithms** | US federal systems and many regulated buyers | Validated modules; approved algorithms only |
| **NIST SP 800-57, 800-131A** | Guidance used widely beyond government | Key management practice; algorithm transitions and deadlines |
| **SOC 2** | Service organisations | Encryption and key management as part of security and confidentiality criteria |
| **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** | Information security management | Control 8.24 "Use of cryptography": policy, key management, lifecycle |
| **HIPAA** | US health data | Encryption as an addressable safeguard; breach safe harbour for properly encrypted data |
| **GDPR** | EU personal data | Encryption as an appropriate technical measure; reduced breach notification duties for encrypted data |
| **EU NIS2 and DORA** | Critical sectors and EU financial entities | Cryptography policies, key management, and ICT risk management; DORA has applied since January 2025 |
| **eIDAS 2.0** | EU electronic identification and trust services | Qualified signatures, certificates and wallets, with certified hardware |
| **CNSA 2.0** | US national security systems | Specific algorithms (AES-256, SHA-384, ML-KEM-1024, ML-DSA-87, LMS/XMSS) and post-quantum deadlines |

Two practical observations:

- Regulators increasingly require a **cryptographic inventory**. PCI DSS 4.0 already does, and post-quantum guidance from governments makes it a near-universal expectation.
- Compliance is the floor. A system can pass an audit with keys in files readable by every administrator. Design to the threat model; then show compliance.

## 24.5 Governance

A cryptography programme needs:

- **A policy** approved by leadership: approved algorithms, key lengths, protocols, storage requirements and lifetimes, reviewed at least annually.
- **Standards and patterns**: approved, reusable designs (for example "how we encrypt a database field", "how services authenticate to each other") so teams do not reinvent them.
- **A design review** step for new systems that handle sensitive data or keys.
- **Exceptions** with owners and end dates.
- **Metrics** reported to leadership: inventory coverage, automation coverage, policy compliance, expiry incidents, time to rotate a key on demand.
- **Clear ownership** for PKI, KMS and secrets platforms, each with an on-call rotation.

## 24.6 Incident response for cryptographic failures

Two kinds of cryptographic incident need prepared playbooks.

### Key compromise

When a key is known or suspected to be exposed:

1. **Contain.** Disable the key or remove its access where possible (disable a KMS key, revoke a token's permissions, remove a compromised CA from trust stores).
2. **Assess scope.** Use the inventory: what does the key protect or sign, where is it used, who trusts it, and since when could it have been exposed?
3. **Replace.** Issue new keys and certificates, deploy them everywhere, and verify.
4. **Revoke and re-protect.** Revoke certificates after replacement (Chapter 17). Re-wrap or re-encrypt data protected by a compromised KEK. Re-sign artefacts if a signing key was compromised, and publish which signatures to distrust.
5. **Investigate and notify.** Determine how the exposure happened, review logs of the key's use for misuse, and meet regulatory and contractual notification duties.
6. **Learn.** Fix the root cause and update the playbook.

### Algorithm or implementation break

When a widely used algorithm, library or device is found weak (ROCA, Heartbleed, a new attack on a mode):

1. Query the inventory for every affected system, key and certificate.
2. Prioritise by exposure and blast radius.
3. Patch, then **replace keys** generated by or exposed to the flaw.
4. Track remediation to zero and report progress.

Speed in both cases depends almost entirely on work done in advance: the inventory, automation and rehearsals.

Choose a system you know, or use this example: a multi-tenant SaaS application that stores customer documents in cloud object storage, encrypts them with per-document DEKs wrapped by per-tenant KEKs in a cloud KMS, and lets support staff view documents on request.

1. Draw the data flow and trust boundaries.
2. List every key, where it lives, who can use it, and its rotation.
3. Apply STRIDE at each boundary.
4. Find the single change that would most reduce blast radius.
5. Write the first three steps of a playbook for "a support engineer's credentials were phished".
6. Identify everything that would need to change for the post-quantum transition (Part VII).

Compare your answer with a colleague's. The differences are usually where the real risks hide.

## Failure story: the update server everyone trusted

In June 2017, a destructive malware outbreak known as NotPetya spread across the world, causing an estimated 10 billion US dollars in damage to shipping lines, pharmaceutical companies, logistics firms and many others. It began with the update server of a widely used Ukrainian accounting software package. Attackers had compromised the vendor's infrastructure and pushed a malicious update that customers' systems installed automatically, because it came through the trusted update channel.

Cryptography was not broken; it was bypassed by an architecture that concentrated trust in one weakly protected place. The update mechanism had limited protection, and once the attackers controlled the vendor's servers, every customer trusted what they delivered. Inside victim networks, the malware then spread using stolen administrator credentials, showing the second half of the blast radius problem.

The lessons are architectural: sign updates with keys kept in hardware and separate from distribution servers; verify signatures on the client; limit what automatically installed software can reach; segment networks so one compromised host cannot reach everything; and protect administrative credentials as the high-value keys they are.

## Chapter summary

- Cryptographic systems include keys, identities, protocols, trust anchors and processes; attackers seek the weakest link across all of them.
- Threat model by drawing data flows and trust boundaries, listing keys, asking who can decrypt or sign, and prioritising by blast radius.
- Crypto agility spans protocols and formats, applications and organisations; build it before you need it, because the post-quantum transition requires it.
- Compliance frameworks increasingly require inventories and documented key management, but compliance is the floor, not the goal.
- Prepare playbooks for key compromise and algorithm breaks; speed depends on inventory, automation and rehearsal.
