## 21.1 What a key management service does

A **key management service (KMS)** stores keys, usually inside HSMs, and performs operations with them on request, so that applications never handle the master keys directly. A typical KMS offers:

- **Key creation** with chosen algorithms, often with the option of HSM-backed storage.
- **Cryptographic operations by API:** encrypt and decrypt small data, wrap and unwrap DEKs, sign and verify, generate random bytes, compute MACs.
- **Access policies** controlling which identities can perform which operations on which keys.
- **Automatic rotation** with key versions.
- **Audit logs** of every operation.
- **Integrations** with storage, databases and other services, which use the KMS for envelope encryption behind the scenes.

The defining property: **master keys are non-exportable.** You can ask the KMS to use a key, but you cannot ask it to give you the key.

## 21.2 Cloud KMS

All major cloud providers offer a KMS. The details differ, but the model is the same:

1. You create a **customer-managed key** in a region.
2. Cloud services (object storage, block storage, managed databases, data warehouses) are configured to protect your data with it, using envelope encryption.
3. When a service needs to read your data, it asks the KMS to unwrap a DEK, and the KMS checks that the request is allowed by your key policy.
4. Every request appears in your audit log.

Clouds typically offer several protection levels: keys protected by software inside the provider's hardened KMS, keys in multi-tenant HSMs validated to FIPS 140-3 level 3, and dedicated single-tenant HSMs that you manage. They also distinguish **provider-managed keys** (encryption on by default, but you control nothing) from **customer-managed keys** (you control policy, rotation and deletion).

> **In practice.** Default encryption with provider-managed keys protects against stolen disks in a data centre, a threat you could not address anyway. Customer-managed keys add something real: you control who may decrypt, you can see every use, and you can make data unreadable by disabling the key.

## 21.3 Designing key policies

A KMS is only as safe as its access policy. Good policies follow these rules:

- **Separate administration from use.** Key administrators can create, rotate, disable and schedule deletion of keys, but cannot encrypt or decrypt with them. Applications can use keys but cannot change their policies. No human routinely has both.
- **Least privilege by operation.** An application that only writes data needs `encrypt` or `generate data key`, not `decrypt`. A verifier needs only the public key.
- **Scope keys narrowly.** One key per application, environment and data class, rather than one company-wide key. A breach or misconfiguration then has a limited blast radius.
- **Use conditions.** Restrict use to particular networks, services or contexts. Many KMSs support **encryption context**: key-value pairs bound to each ciphertext as associated data and checked on decryption, so a wrapped key for tenant A cannot be unwrapped in a request for tenant B.
- **Protect deletion.** Deletion is irreversible and destroys all data under the key. Use mandatory waiting periods (typically 7 to 30 days), alerts, and separate approval.
- **Watch the logs.** Alert on policy changes, disabling or scheduling deletion, decryption from unusual identities or locations, and spikes in volume.

## 21.4 BYOK, HYOK and external keys

As organisations moved sensitive data to the cloud, they asked: can the provider read our data? Several models answer this, with very different guarantees.

| Model | How it works | What it gives you | What it does not give you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Provider-managed keys** | Provider creates and controls keys | Encryption at rest by default | Control, visibility or revocation |
| **Customer-managed keys (CMK)** | You create and control keys in the provider's KMS | Policy control, audit, ability to disable | Independence from the provider's KMS |
| **BYOK (bring your own key)** | You generate key material in your own HSM and import it into the provider's KMS | Proof of how the key was generated; an escrow copy outside the cloud | Protection from the provider: once imported, the key is used inside their KMS |
| **HYOK / external key management (EKM, XKS)** | The master key stays in your own key manager or HSM, outside the cloud. The cloud KMS calls your key manager for each wrap or unwrap | The provider cannot decrypt without your system's cooperation; you can cut off access instantly | Simplicity and availability: your key manager is now in the critical path of every cloud data access |

Two points are often misunderstood.

**BYOK is mostly about generation and escrow, not control.** Importing a key gives you assurance about its origin and lets you keep a backup, but the provider's KMS uses it like any other key. If your goal is that the provider *cannot* decrypt, BYOK alone does not achieve it.

**HYOK trades availability for control.** If your external key manager is unreachable, every service depending on it stops. HYOK suits a small set of highly sensitive datasets, often driven by data sovereignty requirements, rather than everything.

> **Current development.** European data sovereignty requirements have driven rapid growth in external key management and "sovereign cloud" offerings, where keys are held by an EU-based operator or the customer. Regulators increasingly ask not only "is data encrypted?" but "who can access the keys, and under which jurisdiction?"

## 21.5 Enterprise key managers and KMIP

Inside their own data centres, organisations use **enterprise key managers**: appliances or software, usually backed by HSMs, that centrally manage keys for storage arrays, databases, backup systems, tape libraries and virtualisation platforms.

Many of these consumers speak the **Key Management Interoperability Protocol (KMIP)**, an OASIS standard. KMIP defines a common language for creating, retrieving, locating, rotating and destroying keys and other managed objects, so that a storage array from one vendor can use a key manager from another. Typical KMIP uses:

- Self-encrypting drives and storage arrays fetching their encryption keys at boot.
- Database transparent data encryption (TDE) master keys.
- Backup and tape encryption keys.
- Hypervisor VM encryption keys.

When evaluating an enterprise key manager, check: KMIP version and profile support, HSM backing and FIPS 140-3 validation level, high availability and clustering across sites, backup and disaster recovery of key material, role separation and quorum controls, and audit integration with your security monitoring.

## 21.6 PKCS#11 and other cryptographic APIs

Applications reach keys in HSMs and key managers through a handful of APIs:

| API | What it is | Typical users |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PKCS#11** (Cryptoki) | The standard C interface to cryptographic tokens and HSMs: sessions, objects, mechanisms | CAs, TLS servers, signing systems, OpenSSL via providers |
| **Microsoft CNG / KSP** | Windows cryptography API with pluggable key storage providers | Windows services, AD CS |
| **Java JCA/JCE** | Java's provider-based cryptography architecture | Java applications |
| **OpenSSL 3 providers** | Plug-in modules that route OpenSSL operations to HSMs, TPMs or KMSs | Linux services |
| **KMIP** | Key lifecycle management protocol | Storage, databases, backup |
| **Cloud KMS REST APIs** | HTTPS APIs with cloud identity | Cloud-native applications |

A practical rule: put the decision about **where a key lives** in configuration, not in application code. Code that calls a generic interface (a PKCS#11 provider, an OpenSSL provider, or a small internal key service) can move from a software key to an HSM or a cloud KMS without rewriting, which is exactly the agility the post-quantum transition will demand.

## 21.7 Choosing the right home for a key

| Need | Usually best served by |
| --- | --- |
| Encrypting data in cloud services | Cloud KMS with customer-managed keys |
| Sovereignty or provider-independence for a sensitive dataset | External key management (HYOK) for that dataset only |
| Storage arrays, databases, backups on premises | Enterprise key manager with KMIP |
| Root CA, code signing, payment keys | Dedicated HSMs with quorum controls (Chapter 23) |
| Very high-volume signing or TLS offload | HSMs or cloud HSMs sized for throughput |
| Developer secrets and API tokens | A secrets manager (Chapter 22), not a KMS |

## Lab: least-privilege key policy

You can do this lab in any major cloud's free tier, or with the open-source **OpenBao** transit engine locally.

1. Create a symmetric key for an application called `orders`.
2. Create two identities: `orders-writer` and `orders-reader`.
3. Grant `orders-writer` only the operations needed to encrypt or generate data keys; grant `orders-reader` only decrypt.
4. Encrypt a value as the writer with an encryption context of `{"app": "orders", "env": "prod"}`.
5. Try to decrypt as the writer (denied), as the reader with the same context (allowed), and as the reader with `{"env": "test"}` (denied).
6. Open the audit log and find all four requests, including the denials.
7. Disable the key and confirm that even the reader can no longer decrypt. Re-enable it.

Write down how you would detect, within minutes, if someone scheduled this key for deletion.

## Failure story: the key policy that let everyone in

A recurring pattern in cloud security reviews and published breach reports is not broken cryptography but **over-broad key policies**. A common example: a key created early in a project grants decrypt permission to a wildcard principal, such as every role in the account, "to make things work". Data is encrypted with a customer-managed key, dashboards show "encrypted at rest: 100 percent", and auditors are satisfied. But any compromised workload or over-privileged user in the account can decrypt everything.

In the 2019 breach of a large US bank's cloud environment, an attacker exploited a server-side request forgery flaw in a web application firewall to obtain the credentials of the role it ran under. That role had permission to list and read storage buckets containing over 100 million customer records. The data was encrypted at rest, but the stolen role was authorised to read it, so the platform decrypted it transparently.

Encryption at rest only helps against attackers who are *not* authorised to use the key. The value of a KMS is in its policy: narrow keys, least-privilege grants, conditions, and monitored use.

## Chapter summary

- A KMS performs operations with non-exportable master keys on request, with policy and audit, and powers envelope encryption across cloud and enterprise services.
- Customer-managed keys add real control; provider-managed keys mainly protect against physical theft.
- Separate key administration from key use; grant least privilege by operation; bind encryption context; protect deletion; monitor logs.
- BYOK proves key origin and gives escrow; HYOK keeps the provider from decrypting but puts your key manager in the critical path.
- KMIP, PKCS#11 and provider interfaces let applications and devices reach keys; keep key location in configuration for agility.
