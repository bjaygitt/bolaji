## 25.1 What a quantum computer is, briefly

A classical computer stores bits that are either 0 or 1. A quantum computer uses **qubits**, which can be in a **superposition** of 0 and 1, and can be **entangled** so that their states are linked. A quantum algorithm arranges a computation so that, when the qubits are measured, wrong answers cancel each other out and right answers reinforce each other, through **interference**.

Two misconceptions are worth clearing away:

- Quantum computers are **not** simply faster classical computers that "try every answer at once". For most problems they offer no advantage at all.
- They are good at specific problems with special mathematical structure. Unfortunately for us, the problems behind today's public-key cryptography have exactly that structure.

Today's machines have from tens to around a thousand **physical qubits**, which are noisy: they make errors constantly. Useful, long computations need **error correction**, which combines many physical qubits into one reliable **logical qubit**. A machine able to break cryptography is usually called a **cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC)**.

## 25.2 Shor's algorithm: the end of RSA and ECC

In 1994, Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could find the **period** of a function efficiently. Factoring a number and computing a discrete logarithm can both be turned into period-finding problems. So a CRQC running **Shor's algorithm** could:

- Factor RSA moduli, recovering private keys from public keys.
- Solve discrete logarithms, breaking Diffie-Hellman and DSA.
- Solve elliptic curve discrete logarithms, breaking ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA.

Larger keys do not help: the work grows only modestly with key size. **Every public-key algorithm in Parts III to VI, as deployed today, falls to Shor's algorithm.** This includes the key exchange in TLS, SSH, IPsec and messaging, the signatures on certificates, software and tokens, and RSA key wrapping in HSMs.

## 25.3 Grover's algorithm: a smaller threat to symmetric crypto

**Grover's algorithm** (1996) speeds up unstructured search. Finding one key among 2^n possibilities takes about 2^(n/2) quantum steps instead of 2^n classical ones. On paper, this halves the security of symmetric keys and hash preimages: AES-128 drops to 64-bit security.

In practice the threat is much smaller:

- Grover's steps must run **one after another** on an error-corrected machine; they cannot be split efficiently across many machines the way classical key searches can.
- Each step involves running AES as a quantum circuit, which is enormously expensive.

Expert consensus, reflected in NIST guidance, is that AES-128 remains acceptable and AES-256 is comfortably secure against quantum attack. SHA-256 and SHA-3 remain secure for their uses. The response to Grover is simply to prefer 256-bit symmetric keys for long-term protection.

| Cryptography | Quantum attack | Effect | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RSA, DH, ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA | Shor | Completely broken | Replace with ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA |
| AES-128 | Grover | Weakened on paper, practically safe | Prefer AES-256 for long-lived data |
| AES-256 | Grover | Safe | None needed |
| SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-3 | Grover | Safe for practical purposes | None needed |
| HMAC, HKDF | Grover | Safe | None needed |

## 25.4 How close are we?

No one knows exactly when a CRQC will exist, but three trends inform the estimate.

**Hardware is improving.** Companies and national programmes are building machines with more and better qubits. In December 2024, Google demonstrated error correction improving as the logical qubit grew, a key milestone toward practical fault tolerance. Several companies have published roadmaps targeting fault-tolerant machines around the end of this decade.

**Resource estimates are falling.** In 2019, the best estimate for factoring 2048-bit RSA was about 20 million noisy qubits running for eight hours. In 2025, a revised analysis by Craig Gidney at Google estimated that fewer than one million noisy qubits could do it in under a week. Algorithmic improvements, not only hardware, are shrinking the gap.

**Expert surveys** of quantum researchers, such as those published annually by the Global Risk Institute, put a meaningful probability, often estimated at one in three or higher, on a CRQC within about 10 to 15 years, with wide disagreement.

The honest summary: a CRQC is not imminent, its arrival date is uncertain, and that uncertainty itself is the risk, because migration takes years.

## 25.5 Mosca's inequality

Michele Mosca proposed a simple way to decide whether you are already late. Define:

- **X:** how many years your data must stay confidential (its shelf life).
- **Y:** how many years it will take to migrate your systems to quantum-safe cryptography.
- **Z:** how many years until a CRQC exists.

> **Mosca's inequality.** If X + Y is greater than Z, you have a problem: some of your data will still need protection after attackers can break it.

For example, if medical records must stay confidential for 25 years (X), and migration will take 7 years (Y), then even a CRQC 30 years away (Z) leaves a gap. Large organisations typically estimate Y at 5 to 15 years, because cryptography is embedded in so many products, protocols and partners.

## 25.6 Harvest now, decrypt later

The most urgent threat is not future attacks on future traffic. It is **harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL)**: adversaries recording encrypted traffic and stealing encrypted data **today**, to decrypt once a CRQC exists. Intelligence agencies are widely assumed to do this, and data storage is cheap.

HNDL affects **confidentiality**, through key exchange and public-key encryption. It is why the first post-quantum upgrades, deployed by browsers, messaging apps and SSH between 2022 and 2024, all protect key exchange.

**Signatures** face a different timeline. A signature only needs to be unforgeable until it is verified, so a CRQC in 2035 cannot retroactively forge a TLS handshake from 2026. But long-lived signature keys must be replaced before a CRQC exists: root CA keys, firmware and code signing keys burned into devices, document signatures that must remain valid for decades, and identity signing keys. If a CRQC could derive a root CA's private key from its public key, it could issue certificates for anything.

| Risk | Driven by | Urgency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recorded traffic decrypted later | Key exchange (ECDH, RSA key transport) | Highest: affects data sent today |
| Stored data wrapped with RSA or ECC keys decrypted | Public-key encryption and key wrapping | High for long-lived data |
| Forged signatures on new artefacts | Signature keys still trusted when a CRQC arrives | Must complete before CRQC; long lead times for roots and devices |
| Forged signatures on old artefacts | Long-term signatures without post-quantum timestamps | Specific to archives and legal documents |

## 25.7 Government timelines

Governments have converted uncertainty into deadlines. The main ones, as published by late 2026:

| Authority | Key dates |
| --- | --- |
| **US NIST (IR 8547, draft, 2024)** | Deprecate 112-bit RSA and ECC after 2030; disallow RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, DH and ECDH entirely after 2035 |
| **US NSA (CNSA 2.0)** | Prefer quantum-safe algorithms for software and firmware signing, browsers, servers and cloud services from 2025; new national security system acquisitions must be CNSA 2.0 compliant from January 2027; exclusive use in many categories by 2030 to 2033; all national security systems quantum-resistant by 2035 |
| **US federal policy** | National Security Memorandum 10 (2022) and the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act require agencies to inventory cryptography and plan migration, with 2035 as the overall goal |
| **UK NCSC (2025)** | By 2028: discovery and migration plan complete; by 2031: high-priority migrations complete; by 2035: migration complete |
| **European Union (coordinated roadmap, 2025)** | Begin transition by the end of 2026; protect high-risk use cases by the end of 2030; complete as far as feasible by 2035 |
| **Others** | Australia's guidance targets ceasing traditional asymmetric cryptography by 2030; Canada, Germany, France, the Netherlands, Japan, Singapore and others have published plans with similar horizons |

> **Current development.** These dates are converging on a common pattern: **inventory now, protect high-risk systems by about 2030, finish by 2035.** Regulators in finance and critical infrastructure increasingly ask organisations to show a post-quantum plan. Check your sector's guidance, and treat 2030 as the practical deadline for anything important.

## 25.8 What not to do

- **Do not wait for certainty** about the CRQC date. Migration time is the long pole.
- **Do not deploy unvetted "quantum-proof" products.** Use the NIST-standardised algorithms in Chapter 26.
- **Do not rely on quantum key distribution (QKD)** as a general solution. QKD uses quantum physics to distribute keys over special optical links, but it needs dedicated hardware, works only point to point over limited distances, does not authenticate on its own, and is not recommended by the NSA, NCSC, ANSSI or BSI as a replacement for post-quantum cryptography in most uses.
- **Do not panic about symmetric cryptography.** Moving to AES-256 where feasible is enough.

## Failure story: the long tail of a cryptographic transition

The retirement of SHA-1 is the best rehearsal we have for the post-quantum transition. Theoretical weaknesses were published in 2005. NIST deprecated SHA-1 for signatures in 2011. Browsers began warning about SHA-1 certificates in 2014 and stopped accepting them in early 2017, the same year the first real collision was produced.

Even with 12 years of warning, the transition was painful. Payment terminals, medical devices, industrial systems and older phones could not verify SHA-256 certificates, so some CAs requested exemptions to keep issuing SHA-1 certificates for payment processors. Software signing and Git kept SHA-1 for years longer. Some legacy systems still use it today.

The post-quantum transition is far larger: it replaces public-key algorithms, not one hash function, across protocols, certificates, hardware, firmware and partners, with larger keys and signatures that break assumptions everywhere. If SHA-1 took about 15 years, starting the post-quantum migration in the late 2020s with a 2035 deadline leaves little slack.

## Chapter summary

- Shor's algorithm on a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks RSA, DH and all elliptic curve cryptography; larger keys do not help.
- Grover's algorithm only modestly weakens symmetric cryptography; prefer AES-256 for long-lived data.
- No CRQC exists, but hardware is improving and resource estimates have fallen sharply; uncertainty is the risk.
- Mosca's inequality: if data shelf life plus migration time exceeds time to a CRQC, act now. Harvest-now-decrypt-later makes key exchange the first priority; long-lived signature keys come next.
- Governments converge on inventory now, high-risk systems by about 2030, complete by 2035.
