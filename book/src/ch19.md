## 19.1 Why certificates cause outages

Every certificate has a fixed expiry date, and when it passes, every client that checks it refuses to connect. There is no grace period. Well-known examples include a mobile network outage affecting millions of users in 2018 caused by an expired certificate in network equipment software, a global collaboration-tool outage in 2020, and countless smaller incidents that never make the news.

In almost every case, the certificate was easy to replace. The real failures were:

- **Nobody knew it existed.** It was created years earlier by someone who left.
- **Nobody owned it.** Expiry warnings went to an unmonitored mailbox.
- **Renewal was manual.** Someone had to remember, request, install and restart.
- **Nobody checked the result.** A renewed certificate sat on disk while the service kept presenting the old one.

Shrinking lifetimes multiply the problem. Organisations typically renew when about a third of a certificate's life remains. For 1,000 public certificates, that means:

| Maximum lifetime | Renew roughly every | Renewals per year for 1,000 certificates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 398 days (until March 2026) | 265 days | 1,400 |
| 200 days (now) | 133 days | 2,700 |
| 100 days (from March 2027) | 67 days | 5,500 |
| 47 days (from March 2029) | 31 days | 11,800 |

At 47 days, those 1,000 certificates need about 45 renewals every working day. Automation is the only answer.

## 19.2 ACME: the protocol that made automation normal

The **Automatic Certificate Management Environment (ACME)**, RFC 8555, was created by the Internet Security Research Group for Let's Encrypt, which launched in 2015 and now issues certificates for hundreds of millions of websites. ACME lets software obtain and renew certificates with no human involved:

1. The **ACME client** creates an account key and registers with the CA's ACME server.
2. It places an **order** for the names it wants.
3. The server returns **challenges** to prove control of each name:
   - **HTTP-01:** serve a token at `http://<name>/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>`.
   - **DNS-01:** publish a TXT record at `_acme-challenge.<name>`. The only method that supports wildcards and works for hosts not reachable from the internet.
   - **TLS-ALPN-01:** answer a special TLS handshake on port 443.
4. The server validates the challenges (from multiple network perspectives, Chapter 16).
5. The client sends a CSR, and downloads the certificate.

Clients exist for every environment: Certbot and lego for servers, built-in ACME in web servers such as Caddy, cert-manager in Kubernetes, and ACME support in many load balancers, CDNs and appliances. Most public CAs and many private CA products now offer ACME endpoints, often with **External Account Binding (EAB)**, which ties ACME accounts to a customer account at a commercial CA.

### Delegating DNS validation safely

DNS-01 requires automation to write DNS records, and giving a script power over your whole DNS zone is risky. The common safer pattern is **delegation**: create a CNAME from `_acme-challenge.shop.example.com` to a record in a small, separate zone that only the ACME automation can write. The automation can answer challenges but cannot change any real records. This becomes essential as domain validation reuse falls to 10 days in 2029.

### ACME Renewal Information (ARI)

**ARI**, standardised in 2025 as RFC 9773, lets the CA tell each client when to renew. Normally it suggests a window spread over time to avoid load spikes. If the CA must revoke certificates early, because of a validation bug, a mis-issuance or a key compromise, it can move the window forward, and ARI-aware clients renew within hours without anyone being paged. ARI turns mass revocation events from emergencies into routine.

## 19.3 The six jobs of certificate lifecycle management

ACME automates issuance for clients that support it. A large organisation also has certificates on appliances, legacy systems, cloud services and devices, issued by several CAs. **Certificate lifecycle management (CLM)** is the discipline, and often the product category, that covers all of them. It has six jobs:

1. **Discover** every certificate, wherever it is.
2. **Govern** with policy: allowed CAs, key types, names and lifetimes.
3. **Issue** by talking to public and private CAs.
4. **Provision**: install, bind and verify on the target.
5. **Control access**: who may request and approve what.
6. **Renew and revoke**, safely and on time.

Commercial CLM platforms, offered by specialist security vendors, by public CAs, and by cloud providers, combine these jobs with dashboards, workflows and integrations. Open-source tools and in-house automation can cover them too. The rest of this chapter describes the jobs in a product-neutral way, so the concepts apply whatever tools you use.

## 19.4 Discovery and inventory

You cannot renew a certificate you do not know about. No single discovery method finds everything:

| Method | Finds | Misses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Network scanning** | Certificates served on scanned IPs and ports, including unknown systems | Unscanned ports and networks; sites that need the right hostname (SNI) to answer |
| **Integration discovery** (reading a load balancer's, CDN's or cluster's certificate store through its API) | Everything stored there, including unused certificates, and how each is bound | Systems not integrated |
| **CA account import** | Everything bought through your CA accounts | Where certificates are installed; other CAs |
| **Certificate Transparency monitoring** | Every public certificate for your domains, from any CA | Private certificates; locations |
| **Host agents or file scanning** | Certificate and key files and keystores on disk | Hosts without agents |

Combine several. Then turn raw findings into an **inventory** with two kinds of record, kept separate:

- **Certificate:** one per unique certificate (identified by fingerprint), with names, issuer, key type, expiry and owner.
- **Installation:** one per place the certificate is used, with the target, location, binding and last-seen time.

A dashboard that says "12,000 certificates" should always prompt the question: unique certificates, or installations? The numbers can differ by a factor of five.

### Ownership is the hardest part

Every certificate needs an owning **team** (not a person, who may leave) and an escalation contact. Match certificate names to an application catalogue, use the requester recorded by the CA, use the owner of the hosting system, and route everything unresolved to an **unowned queue** that someone works through every week. Ownership coverage is the first metric to drive up, because every later control depends on someone receiving the alert.

## 19.5 Policy

Policy is enforced twice: **prevent** at request time (refuse a request that breaks the rules) and **detect** after discovery (flag existing certificates that break them). A practical baseline:

| Setting | Typical rule |
| --- | --- |
| Allowed CAs | Named public CAs (at least two) and the internal CA |
| Key types | ECDSA P-256 preferred; RSA 2048 minimum, 3072 for long-lived keys |
| Names | Only domains the organisation owns; no internal names in public certificates |
| Wildcards | Only with approval; each one spreads one key across many servers |
| Lifetime | Public: just under the current maximum. Internal: shorter, such as 30 to 90 days, to force automation early |
| Key reuse | Never; every renewal creates a new key |
| Key location | Created on the target wherever possible |

Layer the rules: a company baseline, then environment rules (internet-facing, internal, non-production), then application rules, each layer only tightening the one above. Exceptions must be written down with an owner and an end date.

## 19.6 Provisioning, and where the private key lives

**Provisioning** means getting the certificate, chain and key onto the target and making the target use them. Common models:

| Model | Typical targets |
| --- | --- |
| Push through the target's management API | Load balancers, CDNs, firewalls, cloud certificate stores |
| Agent on the host | Servers, Windows and Java applications |
| Pipeline or CLI request at deploy time | Applications with mature CI/CD |
| Standard protocol (ACME, EST, SCEP) | Web servers, devices, anything with a built-in client |
| Through a platform issuer | Kubernetes cert-manager, service meshes, secrets managers with PKI engines |

For every integration, ask the most important question in certificate operations: **where is the private key created, and who can read it afterwards?**

- **On the target** (best): the target creates the key and a CSR; only the certificate travels.
- **Centrally, then pushed** (sometimes necessary, for targets that cannot create CSRs or high-availability pairs that must share a key): store it encrypted, ideally under an HSM-protected key, send it only over encrypted management channels, and log every export.
- **Generated by someone else and uploaded by hand:** treat as an exception.

### Install, bind, verify

Installing is not finishing. A complete deployment:

1. **Installs** the certificate and chain.
2. **Binds** it: updates the TLS profile, listener, route or ingress to use it.
3. **Synchronises** clustered or high-availability peers.
4. **Verifies from the outside**: connects as a client and confirms the new certificate, full chain and correct names are served.

Without the last step, automation reports success while users still receive the old certificate.

## 19.7 Renewal and escalation

Set the renewal point as a **percentage of lifetime** (for example, when one third remains), not a fixed number of days, so it adapts automatically as lifetimes shrink. Follow ARI where the CA supports it.

When renewal fails, the old certificate is still valid, so escalate in steps rather than all at once:

| Time before expiry (200-day certificate) | Action | Who is told |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About 66 days | Renewal starts | Nobody unless it fails |
| 60 to 30 days | Automatic retries | Owning team |
| 30 days | Still failing | Team and its manager; ticket raised |
| 14 days | Still failing | Certificate platform on-call takes over |
| 7 days | Still failing | Raised as an incident |

Scale the steps to the lifetime. The rule is that no certificate reaches expiry with only an unread email as warning.

Agree with change management that automated renewals with unchanged names and policy are **standard changes**, pre-approved and automatically logged. If every renewal needs a change ticket and a meeting, automation stops being automatic.

## 19.8 Two CAs, and mass replacement

Several events force many certificates to change at once: a CA revoking certificates after a validation bug (one large CA had to revoke over 80,000 in 2024 within days), a CA being distrusted (Entrust in 2024), a CA outage, or a weak algorithm being banned. Prepare for all of them:

- [ ] Accounts with **at least two public CAs**, both configured in your automation, with templates that can switch CA with one change.
- [ ] **CAA records** listing both CAs on every domain.
- [ ] Domains and organisation details validated (or automatically validatable) at both.
- [ ] No clients **pinning** one CA's intermediate or root, or a documented exception for each that does.
- [ ] A **quarterly test**: issue and install a certificate from the backup CA.
- [ ] A **runbook**: select affected certificates by CA, intermediate or key type; prioritise internet-facing services; renew in batches within CA rate limits; track to zero; chase manual leftovers first.

## 19.9 Access control and audit

Whoever controls certificate automation can obtain trusted certificates for your domains and install them on your most exposed systems. Apply the same discipline as for any privileged system: single sign-on and group-based access, separate credentials for each pipeline and cluster, approval for first issuance of sensitive names but not for routine renewals, protected CA API credentials, and an audit log streamed to your security monitoring with alerts for key exports, CA credential changes and issuance outside policy.

## 19.10 Measuring the programme

Report a handful of metrics monthly, with trends:

| Metric | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership coverage | Certificates with an owning team ÷ all certificates |
| Automation coverage | Certificates renewed without human action ÷ all certificates |
| Policy compliance | Certificates with no violations ÷ all certificates |
| Renewal success rate | Renewals completed automatically ÷ all renewals |
| Warning time | Days before expiry that a human was alerted, for failed renewals |
| Expiry incidents | Outages or failures caused by expired certificates (target: zero) |
| Out-of-band issuance | Public certificates for your domains found in CT but not issued through your process |

Always report public and private certificates separately, and keep a named list of the certificates that cannot yet be automated. Those few are usually the ones that cause the next outage.

## Lab: automate a certificate end to end

**1. Run a local ACME CA.** Start step-ca with ACME enabled (or Pebble, Let's Encrypt's test ACME server):

```bash
docker run -d --name ca -p 9000:9000 -e "DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_NAME=Lab" \
  -e "DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_DNS_NAMES=localhost" -e "DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_ACME=true" smallstep/step-ca
```

**2. Issue with an ACME client.** Use lego or Certbot against `https://localhost:9000/acme/acme/directory`, with HTTP-01 on a local web server, and observe the order, challenge and finalisation steps in the client's verbose output.

**3. Build a five-line inventory.** Write a script that connects to a list of `host:port:servername` entries, records subject, issuer, expiry and SHA-256 fingerprint to CSV, and flags anything expiring within 30 percent of its lifetime. Run it with and without SNI and compare.

**4. Break verification on purpose.** Renew a certificate on disk but do not reload the web server. Run your inventory script: the old expiry still appears. Reload, rerun, and see the new one. You have just reproduced the most common "successful renewal" outage.

## Failure story: the certificate that switched off a security tool

In 2017, attackers stole personal data on about 147 million people from Equifax, a US credit bureau. They entered through an unpatched web application. They remained undetected for 76 days because a network monitoring device that inspected encrypted traffic leaving the affected systems had an **expired certificate**, and it had been expired for 19 months. With the certificate expired, the device could not decrypt traffic, so it silently stopped inspecting it. Nobody noticed, because no user-facing service broke.

On 29 July 2017, staff renewed the certificate, and the device almost immediately showed suspicious traffic, triggering the investigation. A US House of Representatives committee report later found that the company had allowed more than 300 security certificates to expire, including 79 used for monitoring business-critical domains, and concluded that a certificate management process with defined roles and responsibilities would have kept the monitoring device working.

Two lessons stand out. Certificates on security tools matter as much as those on websites, because when they expire, protection fails silently. And an outage is the lucky failure: the dangerous expiries are the ones nobody complains about. Count expired certificates in use, not only outages.

## Chapter summary

- Expired certificates cause outages and silently disable security controls. Lifetimes falling to 47 days make manual renewal impossible.
- ACME automates issuance and renewal; delegate DNS validation safely; use ARI so CAs can bring renewals forward.
- Lifecycle management means discover, govern, issue, provision, control access, and renew or revoke.
- Combine discovery methods, separate certificates from installations, and make ownership the first metric.
- Create keys on the target where possible; install, bind, sync and verify; renew at a percentage of lifetime and escalate in steps.
- Keep two tested CAs with CAA allowing both, and practise mass replacement.
