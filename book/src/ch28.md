## 28.1 Zero-knowledge proofs

A **zero-knowledge proof (ZKP)** lets a prover convince a verifier that a statement is true without revealing anything beyond the fact that it is true. The classic illustration: proving you know the password to a door in a circular cave by coming out of whichever side the verifier calls, many times in a row, without ever saying the password.

Modern ZKPs prove statements about computations: "I know a secret that hashes to this value", "this transaction balances without revealing amounts", "I am over 18 according to a government-signed credential, without revealing my birth date".

Two families dominate:

| Family | Characteristics | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **zk-SNARKs** | Very small proofs, fast verification; some need a trusted setup | Widely used in blockchain systems; many rely on elliptic curve pairings, which are not quantum-safe |
| **zk-STARKs** and hash-based proofs | Larger proofs, no trusted setup | Rely on hash functions; considered quantum-resistant |

Where ZKPs are appearing:

- **Privacy-preserving identity.** Digital identity wallets can prove attributes (age, residency, licence) without revealing the whole credential. The EU's digital identity wallet framework under eIDAS 2.0 and several national schemes are adopting selective disclosure, and some are adding zero-knowledge techniques, such as Google's open-sourced ZK age verification in 2025.
- **Blockchain scaling and privacy**, the largest current deployment of ZK technology.
- **Verifiable computation:** proving a cloud service ran a computation correctly, including emerging work on proving properties of machine learning inference.

## 28.2 Multi-party computation and threshold cryptography

**Secure multi-party computation (MPC)** lets several parties jointly compute a function of their private inputs, learning only the output. Two banks can find customers they share without revealing their customer lists (**private set intersection**); several hospitals can compute statistics over combined patient data without pooling it.

The most commercially important application today is **threshold cryptography**: splitting a private key into **shares** held by different parties or machines, so that a signature or decryption requires a threshold of them (for example, 3 of 5) to cooperate, and **the full key never exists in any one place**, not even during signing.

- **Threshold signatures** such as FROST for Schnorr and Ed25519 signatures (standardised as RFC 9591 in 2024) and threshold ECDSA protocols protect digital asset custody, high-value signing keys and cloud key services.
- **Distributed key generation** creates the shares without anyone ever holding the whole key.

Compare this with an HSM's M-of-N quorum (Chapter 23): there, several people authorise use of a key that does exist inside one device. With threshold cryptography, the key itself is distributed. The two approaches are complementary, and some systems combine them.

NIST is running a project on multi-party threshold cryptography to develop guidance and possibly standards, including for post-quantum threshold schemes, which are an active research area.

## 28.3 Homomorphic encryption

**Homomorphic encryption** lets someone compute on encrypted data without decrypting it. A client encrypts data, a server computes on the ciphertext, and only the client can decrypt the result.

- **Partially homomorphic** schemes support one operation. RSA is multiplicatively homomorphic; Paillier encryption supports addition, which is enough for encrypted voting tallies and some statistics.
- **Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE)**, first shown possible by Craig Gentry in 2009, supports arbitrary computation. Modern schemes (BGV, BFV, CKKS for approximate arithmetic, TFHE for bits) are based on lattices, the same family as ML-KEM, and are believed quantum-resistant.

FHE remains expensive: typically thousands to millions of times slower than computing on plaintext, depending on the task, though performance has improved by orders of magnitude over the last decade and dedicated hardware accelerators are being built. It is practical today for specific workloads, such as private queries, simple analytics on encrypted data, and encrypted machine learning inference on small models. ISO and an industry consortium are standardising FHE schemes.

## 28.4 Other techniques worth knowing

| Technique | What it does | Where you see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Private information retrieval** | Query a database without the server learning which record you wanted | Private contact discovery, some password breach checks |
| **Oblivious pseudo-random functions and PAKE** | Password-based protocols where the server never sees the password; OPAQUE, standardised by the IETF | Password-based login without exposing passwords |
| **Privacy Pass and blind signatures** | Anonymous tokens proving "this user is legitimate" without tracking | CAPTCHA alternatives, private access tokens |
| **Differential privacy** | Statistical noise that limits what aggregate results reveal about individuals | Census data, telemetry |
| **Content provenance (C2PA)** | Signed manifests describing how images, video and audio were created and edited | Camera makers, news organisations, AI-generated content labelling |
| **Key transparency** | Public, auditable logs of users' public keys | End-to-end encrypted messaging (Chapter 13) |
| **Formal verification** | Machine-checked proofs that protocols and code match their specifications | TLS 1.3 analysis, verified libraries such as HACL*, AWS-LC and others |

Two trends cut across these. First, **AI** is both a consumer and a subject of cryptography: signed provenance for generated media, confidential computing and FHE for private inference, and verification of model integrity. Second, **formal verification** is moving from research to industry: several widely used cryptographic libraries now include machine-verified implementations of core algorithms, including post-quantum ones.

## 28.5 Judging readiness

When a vendor or colleague proposes advanced cryptography, ask:

- [ ] **Is the problem real?** Could a simpler design (access control, an HSM, separating duties) solve it?
- [ ] **Is the scheme standardised or widely analysed?** RFCs, NIST, ISO, or years of public cryptanalysis?
- [ ] **What are the assumptions?** Trusted setup? Non-colluding parties? Hardware trust? Quantum resistance?
- [ ] **What does it cost?** Performance, bandwidth, latency, operational complexity.
- [ ] **Are there mature, audited implementations?**
- [ ] **What is the failure mode?** If an assumption breaks, what leaks?

Advanced cryptography is powerful precisely because it changes who must be trusted. Make sure the new trust assumptions are better than the old ones, not just more complicated.

## 28.6 Where to go from here

You have now covered the whole path from modular arithmetic to post-quantum migration. To keep growing:

1. **Pick a specialisation** that matches your work: PKI and certificate operations, key management and HSMs, protocol security, post-quantum migration, implementation security, or one of the frontier areas in this chapter.
2. **Read primary sources.** RFCs, NIST publications, and CA/Browser Forum ballots are more readable than their reputation suggests. Appendix B lists the most important.
3. **Follow the field.** The IACR ePrint archive, the Real World Crypto conference talks, root program announcements, and NIST's post-quantum project pages report changes as they happen.
4. **Practise.** Cryptopals and CryptoHack challenges, labs like the ones in this book, and small tools you build yourself make the ideas permanent.
5. **Teach.** Explaining a concept to a colleague is the fastest way to discover what you have not yet understood.

## Chapter summary

- Zero-knowledge proofs prove statements without revealing why they are true; they power selective-disclosure identity and verifiable computation.
- MPC computes on private inputs; threshold cryptography distributes keys so they never exist in one place, now standardised for Schnorr-family signatures.
- Fully homomorphic encryption computes on encrypted data; it is lattice-based and improving fast but still costly.
- Private retrieval, OPAQUE, Privacy Pass, provenance, key transparency and formal verification are entering mainstream products.
- Judge emerging cryptography by the problem, analysis, assumptions, cost, implementations and failure modes.
