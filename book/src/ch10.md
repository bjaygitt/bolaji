## 10.1 Why definitions matter

For most of history, ciphers were judged by intuition: a design looked complicated, so people assumed it was strong. Many were broken. Modern cryptography, from the 1980s onward, insisted on two things before calling anything secure:

1. **A precise definition** of what "secure" means: what the attacker can do, and what counts as a win.
2. **Evidence** that no efficient attacker can win, usually a **proof** that winning would require solving a problem believed to be hard.

You do not need to write proofs. But you will constantly read claims like "IND-CCA2 secure", "EUF-CMA secure", "provably secure in the random oracle model" or "secure under the Module-LWE assumption". This chapter teaches you what those phrases promise and what they leave out.

## 10.2 Security as a game

Cryptographers define security as a **game** between a **challenger**, who holds the secret key, and an **adversary**, who is any efficient algorithm. The game spells out exactly which questions the adversary may ask and what the adversary must achieve to win. A scheme is secure if every efficient adversary wins with probability only negligibly better than guessing.

This framing is powerful because it does not list specific attacks. It covers **every** attack, including ones nobody has invented yet, as long as the attacker fits the model.

## 10.3 Definitions for encryption

### IND-CPA: indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack

The game:

1. The adversary may ask for the encryption of any messages it likes.
2. It then picks two messages, m0 and m1, of equal length.
3. The challenger secretly flips a coin and encrypts one of them.
4. The adversary may ask for more encryptions, then guesses which message was encrypted.

The scheme is **IND-CPA secure** if no efficient adversary guesses correctly noticeably more than half the time. In plain words: **ciphertexts reveal nothing about which message was encrypted, even to an attacker who can encrypt messages of their choice.**

One immediate consequence: deterministic encryption can never be IND-CPA secure, because the adversary simply encrypts m0 itself and compares. This is the formal reason textbook RSA and ECB mode fail (Chapters 4 and 7), and why encryption needs IVs, nonces or randomness.

### IND-CCA: adding chosen-ciphertext attacks

**IND-CCA2** (often just "IND-CCA") adds a decryption oracle: the adversary may also ask for the decryption of any ciphertext except the challenge itself. This models attackers who can submit modified ciphertexts and observe the result, which is exactly the padding oracle and Bleichenbacher attacks of Chapters 5 and 7.

IND-CCA2 is the standard required for public-key encryption and key encapsulation used in real protocols. ML-KEM, for example, is designed to be IND-CCA2 secure.

### Authenticated encryption: INT-CTXT

For AEAD (Chapter 5), confidentiality is paired with **integrity of ciphertexts (INT-CTXT)**: the adversary must not be able to produce any new ciphertext that decrypts successfully. A scheme that is IND-CPA and INT-CTXT secure is automatically IND-CCA secure, which is the formal reason Encrypt-then-MAC and AEAD work.

## 10.4 Definitions for signatures and MACs

**EUF-CMA** (existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack) is the standard for signatures and MACs:

1. The adversary may ask for signatures on any messages it chooses.
2. It wins if it outputs a valid signature on **any** message it did not ask about.

"Existential" means the forged message can be anything, even gibberish. This is deliberately strict: if an attacker can forge even meaningless messages, a real attacker will find meaningful ones. A stronger variant, **SUF-CMA** (strong unforgeability), also forbids producing a new signature on a message already signed. Some protocols, such as those that use signatures as identifiers, need the stronger property.

## 10.5 Definitions for building blocks

| Primitive | Security notion | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Block cipher | PRP (pseudo-random permutation) | Indistinguishable from a random shuffle of blocks |
| MAC, PRF, KDF | PRF (pseudo-random function) | Outputs look random to anyone without the key |
| Hash function | Collision, preimage, second-preimage resistance | No efficient way to find these (Chapter 6) |
| Key exchange | Key indistinguishability | The agreed key looks random to an eavesdropper, even after other session keys leak |
| Random generator | Next-bit unpredictability | No efficient way to predict the next output bit |

## 10.6 Reductions: how proofs work

Security proofs are almost always **reductions**. A reduction says:

> If there were an efficient adversary that breaks scheme S, we could use it as a subroutine to build an efficient algorithm that solves hard problem P.

Since we believe P is hard, no such adversary should exist. For example, a proof might show that breaking a KEM implies solving the Module-LWE problem, or that forging HMAC implies breaking the underlying compression function as a PRF.

Three details of reductions matter in practice:

- **The assumption is the foundation.** A proof is only as good as the problem it reduces to. Proofs based on factoring or discrete logarithms become worthless against quantum computers, even though the proofs themselves are correct.
- **Tightness.** A reduction may lose a factor: "breaking S with probability ε implies solving P with probability ε / 2^40". A loose reduction means parameters must be larger than they look to keep the promised security. Some deployed schemes have notably loose proofs.
- **The model.** The proof covers only attackers who behave as the game allows. An attacker who measures timing, injects faults, or exploits a parsing bug is outside the model.

## 10.7 The random oracle model

Many efficient schemes, including RSA-OAEP, RSA-PSS, Ed25519 and parts of ML-KEM, are proven secure in the **random oracle model (ROM)**. The proof pretends that a hash function is a perfect random function: a magic box that returns truly random output for every new input.

Real hash functions are not random oracles, and in 1998 researchers constructed (artificial) schemes that are secure in the ROM but insecure with any real hash function. So a ROM proof is weaker than a proof in the **standard model**, which makes no such idealisation. In practice, ROM proofs have an excellent track record for natural designs: no widely used scheme with a ROM proof has been broken through the gap between hashes and random oracles. Treat ROM proofs as strong evidence, not a guarantee.

A related idealisation, the **quantum random oracle model (QROM)**, lets quantum attackers query the hash in superposition. Post-quantum schemes need proofs in this model, which is technically harder and one reason post-quantum security analysis took years.

## 10.8 Why proven-secure systems still fail

Proofs are about mathematical models. Real systems break for reasons outside the model:

| Gap | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Side channels | Timing leaks of a few nonce bits broke ECDSA in smart cards (Chapter 9) |
| Implementation bugs | Heartbleed leaked memory, including keys, from a correct protocol (Chapter 12) |
| Misuse | Nonce reuse destroys GCM's proven security (Chapter 5) |
| Wrong definition | A scheme proven IND-CPA used where IND-CCA was needed |
| Composition | Two individually secure components combined insecurely (MAC-then-encrypt in old TLS) |
| Broken assumption | Quantum computers break the problems behind RSA and ECC proofs |
| Model mismatch | Protocol proofs that ignored real-world details such as session resumption |

This is why mature engineering combines proofs with public scrutiny, formal verification of protocols and code, careful implementation, and conservative parameters.

## 10.9 Reading a security claim: a checklist

When a vendor, paper or colleague says something is secure, ask:

- [ ] **Secure in what sense?** Which definition: IND-CPA, IND-CCA2, EUF-CMA, something else? Is it the right one for the use?
- [ ] **Under what assumption?** Factoring, discrete log, lattices, an idealised hash? Does it survive quantum attackers?
- [ ] **Proved, or argued?** Is there a published proof, ideally peer-reviewed or machine-checked?
- [ ] **What is outside the model?** Side channels, faults, misuse, key management.
- [ ] **What parameters?** Do the chosen key sizes actually give the claimed security, given the reduction's tightness?
- [ ] **Who has attacked it?** Years of public cryptanalysis count for more than any marketing claim.

## Failure story: when the proof assumed away the attack

TLS 1.2 and earlier used **MAC-then-encrypt**: compute a MAC over the plaintext, append it, add padding, then encrypt with CBC. Analyses in the early 2000s gave this composition security arguments under certain assumptions. Implementations, however, had to check padding and MAC in some order, and the time taken differed depending on where the check failed.

In 2013, Nadhem AlFardan and Kenny Paterson published **Lucky Thirteen**, a timing attack that recovered plaintext from TLS connections by measuring differences of about a microsecond in how long servers took to reject malformed records. The difference came from how many hash compression blocks were processed, which depended on the padding length. Every major TLS library was affected and had to be patched with painstaking constant-time code.

The mathematics had not been wrong. The model had not included time. TLS 1.3 removed the problem at its root by allowing only AEAD ciphers, whose security definitions cover tampering directly and whose implementations naturally reject altered records before revealing anything.

## Chapter summary

- Modern cryptography defines security as a game against any efficient adversary, then proves no such adversary can win, assuming a hard problem.
- IND-CPA means ciphertexts reveal nothing; IND-CCA2 adds resistance to tampering-and-observing; EUF-CMA means no forgeries, even of meaningless messages.
- Proofs are reductions to assumptions. Check the assumption, the tightness, and the model.
- Random oracle proofs idealise hash functions; they are strong evidence with an excellent record, not a guarantee.
- Real breaks happen outside the model: side channels, bugs, misuse, composition and broken assumptions.
