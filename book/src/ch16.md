## 16.1 The players

The **web public key infrastructure (web PKI)** is the system that lets any browser verify any website's certificate. Five groups make it work:

| Player | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Certificate authorities (CAs)** | Verify applicants and issue certificates. Some are commercial companies; Let's Encrypt is a non-profit that issues most of the web's certificates for free |
| **Root programs** | Run by browser and operating system makers (Apple, Google Chrome, Microsoft, Mozilla). Decide which CA roots go into their trust stores, and remove them |
| **The CA/Browser Forum** | An industry body of CAs and browser makers that writes the **Baseline Requirements**, the common rulebook for publicly trusted TLS certificates |
| **Auditors** | Independent firms that audit CAs annually against WebTrust or ETSI criteria |
| **Domain owners and site operators** | Request certificates, prove domain control, and watch for mis-issuance |

The central fact of the web PKI is uncomfortable: **any trusted CA can issue a certificate for any domain.** Your bank's site is only as safe as the weakest CA in your browser's trust store. Most of the system's machinery exists to manage that risk.

## 16.2 Root programs hold the real power

A CA is "publicly trusted" only because root programs include its root certificates. Each program publishes its own policy, layered on top of the Baseline Requirements, and can remove a CA whose behaviour falls short. Removal, called **distrust**, is the strongest sanction in the system, and it has been used repeatedly:

| Year | CA | Reason | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2011 | DigiNotar | Hacked; fraudulent certificates for major sites used to spy on users | Removed within days; the company went bankrupt |
| 2015 to 2016 | CNNIC, WoSign and StartCom | Unauthorised intermediate; backdated certificates and concealed ownership | Distrusted |
| 2017 to 2018 | Symantec (and its brands) | Years of mis-issuance and inadequate oversight of partners | Gradually distrusted; business sold |
| 2019 to 2020 | DarkMatter | Concerns about trustworthiness, never admitted | Inclusion refused |
| 2024 | Entrust | A long pattern of compliance failures and slow incident responses | Chrome, Mozilla and others distrusted new certificates from November 2024 |
| 2025 | Chunghwa Telecom and Netlock | Compliance failures and unmet improvement commitments | Distrusted by Chrome from August 2025 |

Modern distrusts are usually **gradual**: browsers stop trusting certificates issued after a cut-off date, while existing certificates remain valid until they expire. This protects users while giving site operators months to switch CAs.

> **In practice.** Every organisation using public certificates should be able to answer: "If our CA were distrusted tomorrow, how long would it take to move every public certificate to another CA?" Organisations that relied on Entrust in 2024 had a few months. Those with automation and a tested second CA switched in days.

## 16.3 The Baseline Requirements and validation levels

The Baseline Requirements set rules on key sizes, algorithms, certificate contents, validity periods, revocation, audits and, above all, **validation**: how a CA confirms an applicant should get a certificate.

| Level | What the CA verifies | What the certificate shows | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DV (Domain Validated)** | That the applicant controls the domain | Only the domain name | The large majority of web certificates |
| **OV (Organisation Validated)** | Domain control plus that the organisation legally exists | Organisation name and location in the subject | Common for businesses |
| **EV (Extended Validation)** | Domain control plus extensive organisation checks | Detailed organisation information | Browsers removed the special EV indicator around 2019, because studies found users did not notice it |

### Proving domain control

The approved **domain control validation (DCV)** methods include:

- **DNS:** publish a random value in a DNS TXT record (or a CNAME, depending on the method).
- **HTTP:** serve a random value at a specific path on the website.
- **TLS-ALPN:** answer a special TLS handshake on port 443.
- **Email:** click a link sent to a constructed address such as admin@ the domain. Constructed-email and WHOIS-based methods have been progressively restricted after abuse.

> **Current development.** Since March 2025, CAs must check domain control from **multiple network perspectives** in different regions (Multi-Perspective Issuance Corroboration, adopted in Ballot SC-067). This defends against attackers who hijack routing (BGP) to one CA's validation server for a few minutes, a technique demonstrated by researchers and used in real cryptocurrency thefts.

## 16.4 Shorter lifetimes: the 47-day future

Certificate lifetimes have shrunk steadily: from five years before 2015, to three, then two, then 398 days in 2020. In April 2025, the CA/Browser Forum approved **Ballot SC-081**, which shortens them further on a schedule:

| Certificates issued on or after | Maximum validity | Maximum reuse of a domain validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 March 2026 | 200 days | 200 days |
| 15 March 2027 | 100 days | 100 days |
| 15 March 2029 | 47 days | 10 days |

Why shorten lifetimes? Because revocation works poorly (Chapter 17). A certificate that leaks or is mis-issued stays dangerous until it expires. Shorter lifetimes cap that window, and they force automation, which also makes it possible to replace certificates quickly when something goes wrong.

The validation reuse column matters as much as the lifetime. Today, a CA may validate a domain once and issue against that validation for months. By 2029, nearly every renewal will need fresh domain validation, so organisations must automate DCV through DNS APIs or delegated validation records, not only certificate installation.

Some CAs go further already. Let's Encrypt began offering optional **six-day certificates** in 2025, which need no revocation infrastructure at all, and uses them for its new IP address certificates.

## 16.5 Certificate Transparency

How would you know if a CA, through error or compromise, issued a certificate for your domain to someone else? Before 2013, you mostly would not. DigiNotar's fraudulent certificates were discovered only because one Chrome user's browser had a hard-coded expectation for Google's keys.

**Certificate Transparency (CT)**, created at Google and specified in RFC 6962, makes all public certificates visible:

1. Before issuing, the CA submits the certificate to several public **CT logs**, independently operated append-only ledgers built as Merkle trees, so any tampering with history is detectable.
2. Each log returns a **Signed Certificate Timestamp (SCT)**, a promise to include the certificate.
3. The CA embeds the SCTs in the certificate. Browsers such as Chrome and Safari reject public certificates without enough valid SCTs.
4. **Monitors** watch the logs. Anyone can search them, for example through public search tools such as crt.sh, and domain owners can subscribe to alerts for new certificates naming their domains.

CT turned mis-issuance from invisible to detectable, and it has caught many incidents, from test certificates issued for well-known domains to CAs violating the rules. It also has a side effect worth knowing: every hostname in a public certificate becomes public. Internal hostnames such as `payroll-test.example.com` should not appear in public certificates; use a private CA or wildcards for those.

> **Current development.** The CT ecosystem is moving to a newer log design called **static CT** (the "Sunlight" architecture and the static-ct-api specification), which serves logs as static files that are cheaper and more reliable to operate at today's volume of billions of certificates. Chrome began accepting these logs in 2025.

## 16.6 CAA: telling CAs who may issue

A **CAA** (Certification Authority Authorization) record is a DNS record that lists which CAs may issue certificates for a domain. CAs must check it before issuing.

```
example.com.  CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  CAA 0 issue "ca-two.example"
example.com.  CAA 0 issuewild ";"
example.com.  CAA 0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"
```

This example allows two CAs, forbids wildcard certificates entirely, and asks CAs to report refused requests to a security mailbox. Newer CAA parameters can also restrict issuance to a specific CA account or validation method, which makes it harder for an attacker who controls the website, but not your CA account, to obtain a certificate.

> **Watch out.** If you use two CAs so that one can take over when the other fails (Chapter 19), CAA must list **both**. A CAA record that lists only your primary CA silently blocks your backup at the moment you need it.

## 16.7 How users see all this

Users see almost none of it. Browsers removed the padlock's prominence and the EV indicator, and now mainly warn when something is wrong. The web PKI works when it is invisible: certificates issued automatically, logged publicly, validated from multiple vantage points, short-lived, and from CAs under constant scrutiny.

## Lab: watch the web PKI at work

**1. Search Certificate Transparency.** Open `https://crt.sh` and search for a domain you own or a well-known one. Note how many certificates exist, which CAs issued them, and how quickly they are renewed. Search for `%.yourdomain.example` to find every subdomain that ever had a public certificate.

**2. Read SCTs in a certificate.**

```bash
echo | openssl s_client -connect www.mozilla.org:443 -servername www.mozilla.org 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -text | grep -A12 "CT Precertificate SCTs"
```

**3. Check CAA records.**

```bash
dig +short CAA google.com
dig +short CAA github.com
```

**4. Count the days.** For three sites you use, print the validity period and compare it with the SC-081 schedule:

```bash
echo | openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -dates
```

## Failure story: DigiNotar

In the summer of 2011, attackers broke into DigiNotar, a Dutch certificate authority whose roots were trusted by every major browser and by the Dutch government's own PKI. They issued over 500 fraudulent certificates, including one for `*.google.com`. That certificate was used to intercept the Gmail traffic of an estimated 300,000 users, mostly in Iran, by sitting in the middle of their connections with a certificate their browsers fully trusted.

The attack was discovered when Chrome, which had Google's expected keys hard-coded (an early form of pinning), refused the fraudulent certificate, and a user reported the error. DigiNotar's own logs had been tampered with, so the company could not even say which certificates had been issued. Within days, every major browser removed its roots. DigiNotar was declared bankrupt within weeks, and the Dutch government had to replace certificates across its own services.

DigiNotar shaped the modern web PKI. It is the reason Certificate Transparency exists, the reason root programs now enforce strict incident reporting, and the reason "any CA can issue for any domain" is treated as a risk to be monitored rather than a fact to be accepted.

## Chapter summary

- Any publicly trusted CA can issue for any domain; root programs, the Baseline Requirements, audits and transparency manage that risk.
- Root programs can and do distrust CAs. Organisations should be able to switch CAs quickly.
- DV certificates dominate; domain control is now checked from multiple network perspectives.
- Maximum public certificate lifetime is 200 days, falling to 100 in 2027 and 47 in 2029, with domain validation reuse falling to 10 days. Automate issuance and validation.
- Certificate Transparency makes every public certificate visible and monitorable; CAA restricts which CAs may issue.
