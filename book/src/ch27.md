## 27.1 The shape of the problem

Post-quantum migration is unlike most security projects:

- **It touches everything.** Public-key cryptography is in web servers, load balancers, VPNs, SSH, databases, identity providers, code signing, HSMs, smart cards, IoT devices, payment terminals, mobile apps and partner connections.
- **Much of it is not yours.** Cryptography is embedded in libraries, operating systems, appliances and SaaS products. You migrate by upgrading and by pressing vendors.
- **The new keys are bigger.** Systems sized for 32-byte keys and 64-byte signatures will meet kilobytes.
- **It has a deadline you cannot see.** Planning dates (2030 for high-priority systems, 2035 for completion) stand in for an unknown CRQC arrival.

The good news: a well-run migration also builds crypto agility, inventory and automation that pay off for every future change.

## 27.2 A five-phase method

| Phase | Goal | Main outputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Prepare** | Ownership, scope and budget | Executive sponsor, programme lead, policy update, initial timeline |
| **2. Discover** | Know where cryptography is used | Cryptographic inventory and CBOMs |
| **3. Prioritise** | Decide what to migrate first | Risk-ranked list using data lifetime, exposure and migration effort |
| **4. Migrate** | Change algorithms, keys and certificates | Upgraded systems, hybrid deployments, vendor commitments |
| **5. Verify and sustain** | Prove it worked and stays that way | Testing, metrics, continuous discovery, crypto agility built in |

The phases overlap. You should be migrating easy, high-value systems such as public TLS endpoints while discovery of the long tail continues.

## 27.3 Discovery: the cryptographic inventory

A **cryptographic inventory** records where and how cryptography is used. It extends the key and certificate inventories of Chapters 19 and 20 to algorithms and protocols:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| System and owner | Customer portal, team "web-platform" |
| Use | TLS server, mTLS client, data-at-rest wrapping, JWT signing, code signing |
| Algorithms and parameters | ECDHE X25519, ECDSA P-256 certificate, AES-256-GCM |
| Implementation | OpenSSL 3.0.13, vendor appliance firmware 17.1, cloud KMS |
| Keys and certificates involved | Certificate serial, KMS key ID, HSM partition |
| Data protected and its shelf life | Personal data, 7 years |
| Exposure | Internet-facing; internal; partner link |
| Upgrade path | Library upgrade; vendor roadmap; hardware replacement |

### Sources of discovery

- **Network scanning** of TLS, SSH and VPN endpoints, recording negotiated groups and certificate algorithms.
- **Certificate and key inventories** from CLM tools, KMS and HSMs.
- **Code and dependency scanning** for cryptographic library calls and algorithm names.
- **Binary and container image scanning** for embedded libraries and versions.
- **Configuration analysis** of load balancers, web servers, VPNs and identity providers.
- **Vendor questionnaires** for appliances and SaaS.

### CBOM

A **cryptographic bill of materials (CBOM)** describes the cryptographic assets in one application or product, in a machine-readable form. The CycloneDX standard (version 1.6 onward) includes a CBOM format covering algorithms, keys, certificates, protocols and their relationships. Requiring CBOMs from internal teams and vendors turns discovery from a one-off project into a continuous process.

> **In practice.** Do not try to inventory everything perfectly before acting. Start with the systems you know matter most: internet-facing TLS, VPNs, identity providers, PKI, code signing and data stores holding long-lived sensitive data. Discovery of the long tail can run in parallel.

## 27.4 Prioritisation

Rank each system with four questions:

1. **Shelf life:** how long must the data it protects stay confidential? (Mosca's X)
2. **Exposure to harvesting:** does its traffic cross networks an adversary could record, such as the internet or partner links?
3. **Trust lifetime:** does it hold signature keys or trust anchors that must remain trustworthy past 2030? (root CAs, firmware keys, devices in the field for 15 years)
4. **Effort and lead time:** library upgrade, vendor dependency, or hardware replacement?

A simple scoring matrix places systems into waves:

| Wave | Typical systems | Why first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 (now)** | Internet-facing TLS, VPNs, SSH, partner links carrying sensitive data | Harvest-now-decrypt-later; often just a configuration or library upgrade |
| **2** | Long-lived trust anchors, firmware signing, devices with long field lives, data-at-rest wrapped with RSA/ECC | Long lead times; must finish before a CRQC |
| **3** | Internal service-to-service TLS, mTLS, internal PKI, tokens and identity signing | Lower harvesting exposure, large volume |
| **4** | Everything else, legacy systems scheduled for retirement | Migrate or retire |

## 27.5 Migrating key exchange first

Key exchange is the most urgent and often the easiest:

- **TLS.** Upgrade servers, load balancers, CDNs and proxies to versions that support **X25519MLKEM768**, and enable it. Browsers already offer it. Check every termination point, then internal service-to-service TLS, client libraries and language runtimes.
- **SSH.** Upgrade to OpenSSH 10 or equivalent, where mlkem768x25519-sha256 is the default.
- **VPNs.** Plan IKEv2 upgrades with post-quantum KEMs (RFC 9370) as vendors deliver them; for WireGuard, consider the pre-shared key option as an interim measure.
- **Messaging and application protocols.** Use HPKE with post-quantum KEMs where data is encrypted to public keys.

Test for **ossification**: the larger ClientHello in hybrid TLS has broken some middleboxes, firewalls and old servers that mishandle multi-packet handshakes. Roll out gradually and monitor connection failures.

## 27.6 Migrating signatures and PKI

Signatures are harder, because certificates and trust stores connect organisations and devices that upgrade on different schedules.

### Choices for certificates

| Approach | How it works | Trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pure post-quantum chains** | ML-DSA root, intermediates and leaves | Clean; only works where every relying party supports ML-DSA |
| **Composite certificates** | Each certificate carries combined classical and ML-DSA keys and signatures | Secure if either holds; larger; requires composite-aware software |
| **Parallel hierarchies** | Separate classical and post-quantum chains for the same services | Flexible; doubles issuance and management |

For **private PKI**, which you control end to end, you can start now: stand up an ML-DSA or composite test hierarchy, issue certificates to internal services that support it, and measure handshake sizes and performance. Generate new long-lived roots with post-quantum algorithms, or at least plan a post-quantum root well before 2030.

For the **public web PKI**, the path depends on browsers and the CA/Browser Forum. Post-quantum certificates make TLS handshakes significantly larger, and browsers have been exploring designs to reduce that cost, such as trust anchor negotiation and **Merkle Tree Certificates**, which replace most per-handshake signatures with compact inclusion proofs. Expect public post-quantum certificates to arrive later than post-quantum key exchange; follow your CAs' and browsers' announcements.

### Code, firmware and documents

- **Firmware and boot chains:** devices shipping now with 10 to 20 year lives must be able to verify post-quantum signatures, using LMS or XMSS (stateful, in HSMs) or ML-DSA. This is one of the most urgent signature migrations because hardware cannot be updated later.
- **Software signing:** plan dual signatures (classical plus ML-DSA) on packages and updates during the transition.
- **Long-term documents and archives:** add post-quantum timestamps over existing signatures, so evidence remains verifiable.

### Identity and tokens

Identity providers and token systems must move to ML-DSA (or composite) signatures, publish post-quantum keys in JWKS, and ensure every verifier can handle larger keys and tokens. JOSE and COSE specifications for ML-DSA are being finalised at the IETF.

## 27.7 HSMs, KMS and vendors

- **HSMs and KMS:** confirm that your HSMs and key management services support ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA and LMS, ideally within FIPS 140-3 validated configurations. Firmware upgrades may be needed; some older hardware will need replacement. Large keys and signatures may stress HSM throughput and buffer limits.
- **Vendors:** ask every supplier of security-relevant products for a written post-quantum roadmap: which algorithms, in which versions, by when, with FIPS validation dates. Add post-quantum and crypto agility requirements to procurement and contract renewals now; contracts signed in 2026 will govern products still running in 2035.

## 27.8 Testing and performance

Plan for these effects:

| Effect | Where it bites | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Larger handshakes | TLS over slow or lossy links; IoT; first-byte latency | Measure; prefer ML-KEM-768; keep chains short |
| Larger certificates and signatures | Certificate stores, smart cards, headers, tokens, logs | Review size limits and fields |
| Fixed-size assumptions | Database columns, protocol fields, HSM buffers, QR codes | Code and schema review |
| Middlebox ossification | Firewalls, IDS, proxies | Staged rollout with failure monitoring |
| CPU cost | Signature-heavy services | ML-KEM and ML-DSA are fast; SLH-DSA signing is slow |

Run post-quantum configurations in test environments early, add them to continuous integration, and use canary rollouts in production.

## 27.9 Governance and metrics

Treat the migration as a programme with leadership visibility:

- **A named owner** and a cross-functional working group (security architecture, PKI, platform, application teams, procurement, risk).
- **Policy updates** that add post-quantum algorithms to the approved list, set dates after which new systems must use them, and set deprecation dates for RSA and ECC aligned with your regulators.
- **Metrics**, reported quarterly:

| Metric | Example target |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory coverage | 100 percent of high-priority systems inventoried by end of first year |
| Hybrid key exchange coverage for internet-facing TLS | Over 90 percent within 18 months |
| Systems with a documented migration path | 100 percent of wave 1 and 2 |
| Vendors with a written post-quantum roadmap | All critical vendors |
| Long-lived trust anchors with a post-quantum plan | All, before 2028 |
| New systems approved with classical-only public-key crypto | Zero after the policy date |

## Lab: run a mini migration

1. **Discover.** Scan five of your own (or permitted) TLS endpoints with `testssl.sh` or a short `openssl s_client` loop and record the negotiated key exchange group and certificate signature algorithm for each.
2. **Upgrade.** In a test environment, upgrade nginx or another server to a build on OpenSSL 3.5 or later, enable `X25519MLKEM768`, and confirm with a current browser and with `openssl s_client -groups X25519MLKEM768`.
3. **Measure.** Capture handshakes before and after in Wireshark and compare the ClientHello and ServerHello sizes.
4. **Prototype PKI.** Build a small ML-DSA-65 hierarchy with OpenSSL (as in Chapter 18, replacing EC keys with ML-DSA) and serve a post-quantum certificate to a test client that supports it. Record chain size.
5. **Write the CBOM** for one small application: algorithms, libraries, keys, certificates and protocols, in CycloneDX format if you can.

## Failure story: the migration that could not reach the field

Payment terminals, smart meters, cars, medical devices and industrial controllers are designed to run for 10 to 25 years. Many verify firmware updates against a public key written into hardware at manufacture. When the SHA-1 and RSA-1024 transitions arrived, manufacturers repeatedly found devices in the field that could not verify anything else, and the choices were costly: keep trusting a weakened algorithm, or physically replace devices.

The post-quantum transition makes this pattern acute. A device shipped today with only an ECDSA or RSA firmware verification key cannot be fixed with software if that key becomes forgeable: anyone able to forge the signature could push malicious firmware. This is why CNSA 2.0 sets early dates for firmware signing, why device makers are adopting LMS and XMSS now, and why procurement teams should be asking, for every long-lived device bought from now on, "can this verify post-quantum signatures, and can its trust anchor be rotated?"

## Chapter summary

- Post-quantum migration touches nearly every system, much of it vendor-owned, with larger keys and an uncertain deadline; run it as a multi-year programme.
- Discover with a cryptographic inventory and CBOMs; prioritise by data shelf life, harvesting exposure, trust lifetime and effort.
- Migrate key exchange first (hybrid TLS, SSH, VPNs), then long-lived trust anchors and firmware signing, then internal PKI, tokens and the long tail.
- Plan PKI changes: pure, composite or parallel hierarchies; public post-quantum certificates will lag key exchange.
- Engage vendors and HSM providers now, test for size and ossification effects, and track progress with clear metrics.
