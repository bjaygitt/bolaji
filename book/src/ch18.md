## 18.1 Why run a private CA

Use **public** certificates when clients you do not control must trust you: customer browsers, partner systems, public APIs. Use **private** certificates when you control the clients, or can configure them to trust your root:

| Need | Why a private CA fits |
| --- | --- |
| Service-to-service mTLS | Thousands of short-lived certificates; internal names; client authentication |
| Internal web applications | Hostnames that must not appear in public CT logs |
| VPN and Wi-Fi client authentication | Device and user certificates with your own identity data |
| IoT and manufacturing | Device identities with lifetimes and rules public CAs do not offer |
| Kubernetes and service meshes | Automated issuance per workload, lifetimes in hours |
| Code signing for internal tools | Control over who can sign what |

A private CA gives you control over lifetimes, names, extensions and issuance speed, and keeps internal hostnames out of public logs. It also gives you all the responsibility. If your private CA is compromised, an attacker can impersonate any internal service that trusts it, and if its CRL expires or its intermediate expires unnoticed, everything that depends on it can fail at once.

## 18.2 Designing the hierarchy

Most organisations need a **two-tier** hierarchy; large or regulated ones sometimes use three.

| Tier | Role | Key storage | Online? | Typical lifetime |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Root CA** | Signs intermediates and its own CRL only | HSM, offline, in a safe | Powered on only for ceremonies, perhaps twice a year | 10 to 20 years |
| **Policy CA** (optional middle tier) | Separates hierarchies by policy, business unit or region | HSM, offline | Rarely | 5 to 10 years |
| **Issuing CA** | Issues leaf certificates every day | Network HSM or managed key service | Yes | 2 to 5 years |
| **Leaf certificates** | Used by servers, workloads, devices, users | On the endpoint, ideally in hardware | n/a | Hours to months |

Design rules that save pain later:

- **Separate issuing CAs by purpose.** For example: one for TLS servers, one for workload mTLS, one for devices, one for users. A problem in one, such as a compromise or a bad profile, does not affect the others.
- **Constrain intermediates.** Use `pathlen:0` so issuing CAs cannot create further CAs, Name Constraints so they can only issue for your domains, and EKU constraints so a device CA cannot issue code signing certificates.
- **Mind the lifetime ladder.** A CA cannot issue certificates valid beyond its own expiry. Renew issuing CAs well before their remaining life drops below the longest leaf lifetime you issue.
- **Choose algorithms deliberately.** ECDSA P-384 for CAs and P-256 for leaves is a common modern choice. Plan for post-quantum: a new root generated today may still be in service when ML-DSA is required (Chapter 27).

## 18.3 Policy documents

A trustworthy CA writes down its rules:

- A **Certificate Policy (CP)** says what the certificates may be used for and what assurance they carry.
- A **Certification Practice Statement (CPS)** says how the CA actually operates: identity checks, key protection, personnel controls, logging, revocation and disaster recovery.

The public web PKI requires these documents in a standard RFC 3647 structure. For a private CA, a shorter document following the same headings is enough, and it pays off in audits, incidents and handovers.

## 18.4 Protecting CA keys

The CA's private keys are the most sensitive part of the system. Standard controls:

- **Hardware.** Generate and keep CA keys inside a hardware security module (Chapter 23), where they cannot be exported in the clear. For a small internal CA, a FIPS-validated USB HSM or a managed cloud key service with non-exportable keys is a big improvement over a key file on disk.
- **Offline root.** Keep the root's HSM disconnected from any network and physically secured. Bring it out only during scheduled, documented ceremonies.
- **Multi-person control.** Require several people to activate the root, using M-of-N quorums of smart cards (for example, any 3 of 5 key holders).
- **Backups.** Back up CA keys in encrypted, split form to a second secure location. A root key lost to a hardware failure with no backup means rebuilding trust everywhere.

### The key ceremony

A **key ceremony** is the formal, scripted event in which a root key is generated or used. A good ceremony has:

1. A written **script** listing every command and check, approved in advance.
2. Named roles: ceremony administrator, key custodians, an independent witness, and often an auditor.
3. A clean, verified computer that has never been and will never be networked.
4. **Video recording** and a signed log of every step, including hardware serial numbers and tamper-evident bag numbers.
5. Secure storage of all materials at the end, with sign-off by every participant.

It sounds theatrical. Its purpose is evidence: years later, you can prove the root key was created correctly, never left the HSM, and was only used for authorised purposes.

## 18.5 Issuance, automation and enrolment

An issuing CA must decide who may receive which certificate. Common enrolment methods:

| Method | Best for |
| --- | --- |
| **ACME** (Chapter 19) with internal validation | Servers and workloads; the same automation as public certificates |
| **EST** or **SCEP** | Network devices, mobile device management, Wi-Fi certificates |
| **Platform-native issuers** | Kubernetes cert-manager, service mesh certificate authorities, workload identity systems such as SPIFFE/SPIRE |
| **Manual CSR approval** | Rare, high-value certificates only |

Open-source software that implements private CAs includes **step-ca** (Smallstep), **EJBCA** Community, the PKI engine in **OpenBao** (an open-source secrets manager), **cfssl**, and Microsoft **Active Directory Certificate Services** for Windows estates. Every major cloud also offers a managed private CA service.

> **Watch out.** Windows estates often run Active Directory Certificate Services with permissive certificate templates. Researchers showed in 2021 ("Certified Pre-Owned") that misconfigured templates let ordinary domain users request certificates that authenticate as domain administrators. The attack paths, catalogued as ESC1 through ESC15 and counting, are now standard in penetration tests. Audit every template's enrolment rights and subject-name settings.

## 18.6 Operating the CA

Day-to-day operation is where private CAs fail. Plan for:

- **CRL publishing:** re-sign and publish CRLs before their Next Update time, from highly available locations. Monitor for stale CRLs.
- **CA certificate renewal:** track the expiry of every CA certificate in the hierarchy, and practise renewal before it is urgent.
- **Trust distribution:** decide how roots reach clients: operating system trust stores through device management, container base images, Java keystores, language-specific bundles. Each one is a separate place to update.
- **Logging and monitoring:** record every issuance and revocation; alert on unusual volumes or names.
- **Audit:** an annual review of the CP/CPS against what actually happens.
- **Disaster recovery:** a tested plan to restore the CA from backups, including HSM key restoration.

## Lab: build a two-tier private CA with OpenSSL

This lab builds a root and an issuing CA with OpenSSL so you see every moving part. In production, generate keys in an HSM and use a CA product; the concepts are identical.

**1. Root CA (on a machine you will treat as offline).**

```bash
mkdir -p pki/root && cd pki/root
openssl genpkey -algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-384 -out root.key
openssl req -x509 -new -key root.key -sha384 -days 7300 -out root.crt \
  -subj "/O=Lab Corp/CN=Lab Corp Root CA 2026" \
  -addext "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE" \
  -addext "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign"
```

**2. Issuing CA, constrained.** Create its key and CSR, then sign it with the root:

```bash
cd .. && mkdir issuing && cd issuing
openssl genpkey -algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-384 -out issuing.key
openssl req -new -key issuing.key -out issuing.csr -subj "/O=Lab Corp/CN=Lab Corp TLS Issuing CA 1"
cat > issuing.ext <<'EOF'
basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE,pathlen:0
keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign
extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth,clientAuth
nameConstraints=critical,permitted;DNS:.lab.internal
EOF
openssl x509 -req -in issuing.csr -CA ../root/root.crt -CAkey ../root/root.key \
  -CAcreateserial -days 1825 -sha384 -extfile issuing.ext -out issuing.crt
```

**3. A leaf certificate for a service.**

```bash
openssl req -new -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
  -keyout api.key -out api.csr -subj "/CN=api.lab.internal"
printf "subjectAltName=DNS:api.lab.internal\nextendedKeyUsage=serverAuth\nbasicConstraints=CA:FALSE\n" > leaf.ext
openssl x509 -req -in api.csr -CA issuing.crt -CAkey issuing.key -CAcreateserial \
  -days 30 -sha256 -extfile leaf.ext -out api.crt
openssl verify -CAfile ../root/root.crt -untrusted issuing.crt api.crt   # OK
```

**4. Test the name constraint.** Issue a leaf for `api.example.com` from the same issuing CA and verify it. OpenSSL reports a name constraint violation: even a stolen issuing CA key cannot create trusted certificates outside `.lab.internal`.

**5. Rebuild it the automated way.** Install **step-ca**, initialise a CA with `step ca init`, and issue a certificate with `step ca certificate`. Compare the effort, and note that step-ca offers ACME out of the box (Chapter 19).

## Failure story: the internal CA that expired on a Saturday

This pattern has been reported, in various forms, by many organisations, and makes the point of this chapter better than any single incident. An organisation builds an internal issuing CA with a five-year certificate. The engineer who built it leaves. Nobody records the CA's expiry date in any monitoring system, because monitoring watches leaf certificates, not CAs. Five years later, on a weekend, the issuing CA certificate expires.

Every certificate it ever issued, for service-to-service authentication, VPN access and internal websites, instantly becomes invalid, because a chain is only valid while every certificate in it is valid. Services cannot authenticate to each other, staff cannot connect to the VPN to fix anything, and the people who could re-sign the CA need to find the root key ceremony materials, which have not been touched in years.

Prevention is simple and unglamorous: track CA certificates in the same inventory as leaves, alert at least a year before CA expiry, keep ceremony scripts current, practise CA renewal on a schedule, and make sure more than one person knows how.

## Chapter summary

- Use private CAs for internal services, workloads, devices and users; they give control over lifetimes, names and issuance, and keep internal names private.
- A two-tier hierarchy with an offline root and purpose-specific, constrained issuing CAs suits most organisations.
- Protect CA keys in HSMs, with offline roots, M-of-N control, backups and documented ceremonies.
- Automate enrolment with ACME, EST/SCEP or platform-native issuers; audit Windows certificate templates.
- Operations decide success: CRLs, CA renewals, trust distribution, logging and disaster recovery.
