## 8.1 The problem Diffie-Hellman solved

Before 1976, two parties could communicate securely only if they had already shared a secret key, delivered by courier, in person, or through some other trusted channel. For a world of billions of devices that meet each other for the first time every second, that does not scale.

Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a method that lets two parties create a shared secret over a public channel. (It later emerged that researchers at the UK's GCHQ had discovered the same idea secretly a few years earlier.) Their method is called **Diffie-Hellman (DH) key exchange**, and a version of it runs every time your browser opens an HTTPS connection.

## 8.2 The exchange, step by step

Both parties agree on public parameters: a large prime p and a generator g. Then:

| Step | Alice | Public channel | Bob |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Picks secret a | | Picks secret b |
| 2 | Computes A = g^a mod p | A and B are exchanged | Computes B = g^b mod p |
| 3 | Computes B^a = g^(ab) | | Computes A^b = g^(ab) |

Both arrive at the same value, `g^(ab) mod p`, called the **shared secret**. An eavesdropper sees p, g, A and B. To compute the shared secret, they would need a or b, which means solving a discrete logarithm (Chapter 2).

A famous analogy uses paint. Alice and Bob each mix a secret colour into a shared public colour and swap the mixtures. Each then adds their own secret colour to the other's mixture, and both end up with the same final colour. An observer who sees the two mixtures cannot "unmix" them to find the secret colours.

The shared secret is not used directly as a key. It is fed into a key derivation function such as HKDF (Chapter 6) to produce the actual encryption keys.

## 8.3 Elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman

The same exchange works in any group where the discrete logarithm is hard. Using elliptic curves (Chapter 2) gives **Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH)**:

- The public parameter is a curve and a base point G.
- Alice picks a secret number a and publishes the point `A = aG`. Bob publishes `B = bG`.
- Both compute the point `abG`.

Because the elliptic curve discrete logarithm is much harder, ECDH achieves 128-bit security with 256-bit keys, compared with 3072-bit values for finite-field DH. Exchanges are smaller and much faster.

### X25519: the modern default

**X25519**, defined in RFC 7748, is ECDH on Curve25519, designed by Daniel Bernstein in 2005. It has become the default key exchange in TLS 1.3, SSH, WireGuard, Signal and most modern protocols, for reasons that are about safety as much as speed:

- **Every 32-byte string is a valid public key.** There is no need to validate points, which removes a whole class of attacks (section 8.6).
- **Constant-time by design.** The recommended implementation, called the Montgomery ladder, does the same operations for every key bit.
- **Transparent parameters.** The curve's constants were chosen by a documented, minimal process, which addresses suspicions about how some older curves were generated.

The NIST curves **P-256** and **P-384** remain widely used, are FIPS-approved, and are required by some government profiles. They are secure when implemented carefully, but they demand more care: incoming points must be validated.

| Option | Public value size | Security | Strengths | Cautions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Finite-field DH (3072-bit) | 384 bytes | 128 bits | Simple mathematics | Large and slow; old parameters were weak |
| ECDH P-256 | 65 bytes (33 compressed) | 128 bits | FIPS-approved, universal hardware support | Must validate points |
| X25519 | 32 bytes | About 128 bits | Fast, misuse-resistant, default in TLS 1.3 | Not in older FIPS profiles (now allowed under SP 800-186) |
| ECDH P-384 | 97 bytes | 192 bits | Required by CNSA 1.0 | Slower |

## 8.4 Ephemeral keys and forward secrecy

If Alice and Bob use the same DH secrets for every session, then anyone who later steals Alice's secret can decrypt every recorded past session. If instead they generate fresh secrets for each session and delete them afterwards, a later theft reveals nothing about the past. These one-time secrets are called **ephemeral keys**, and the resulting protection is called **forward secrecy** (sometimes "perfect forward secrecy").

Forward secrecy is one of the most important practical security properties in modern protocols:

- **TLS 1.2 with RSA key transport had no forward secrecy.** The browser encrypted the session key with the server's long-term RSA key. Anyone who recorded traffic and later obtained that private key, through a breach, a bug such as Heartbleed, or a legal demand, could decrypt everything ever recorded.
- **TLS 1.3 requires ephemeral (EC)DH** for every handshake. The server's long-term key is used only to sign the handshake, proving identity, never to protect the session key.

> **Watch out.** Forward secrecy protects past sessions from future theft of long-term keys. It does not protect against a quantum computer that can break the recorded ephemeral exchange itself. That is why "harvest now, decrypt later" drives the urgent move to hybrid post-quantum key exchange (Chapter 27).

## 8.5 The missing piece: authentication

Diffie-Hellman alone has a fatal weakness: it does not tell Alice who she is talking to. An attacker in the middle can run one exchange with Alice and a separate exchange with Bob, relaying and reading everything. This is a **man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack**, and plain DH has no defence against it.

Real protocols therefore **authenticate** the exchange: one or both parties sign their DH public value, or the whole handshake transcript, with a long-term signing key whose public half is vouched for by a certificate (Part V) or known in advance (as with SSH host keys). The combination is called **authenticated key exchange**:

- Ephemeral DH provides a fresh shared secret and forward secrecy.
- Signatures with long-term keys prove identity.

Keeping these two roles separate is the design principle behind TLS 1.3, SSH, Noise and Signal.

## 8.6 How Diffie-Hellman fails

### Weak and shared parameters

In 2015, the **Logjam** research showed that many servers still accepted 512-bit "export-grade" DH parameters, a leftover from 1990s US export controls, and that an attacker in the middle could force connections down to them. Worse, millions of servers used the same handful of 1024-bit primes. Because the expensive part of breaking DH can be precomputed once per prime, the researchers estimated that a nation-state could break a single common 1024-bit prime and then passively decrypt a large fraction of VPN and HTTPS traffic. TLS 1.3 now allows only standard, large, well-studied groups (RFC 7919 for finite fields), and only modern curves.

### Small-subgroup and invalid-curve attacks

If an implementation does not check that a received public value is valid, an attacker can send a value that lies in a tiny subgroup, or, for elliptic curves, a point that is not on the agreed curve at all. Combining the victim's secret with such a value leaks a few bits of the secret each time, and repeated exchanges reveal it entirely. In 2015, researchers demonstrated invalid-curve attacks that extracted private keys from TLS servers built on several popular libraries.

The defences:

- **Validate every received public key** (on the curve, correct subgroup, not the identity point) for NIST curves and finite-field groups.
- Prefer **X25519**, where every input is safe by construction.
- Use **ephemeral keys**, so that leaked bits relate to a key that is about to be thrown away.

## 8.7 Diffie-Hellman as a building block

Beyond simple key exchange, DH appears inside many other constructions:

- **ECIES and HPKE.** Public-key encryption built from ECDH plus symmetric encryption. **HPKE** (Hybrid Public Key Encryption, RFC 9180) is the modern standard, used by Encrypted Client Hello and Messaging Layer Security.
- **Signal's X3DH and Double Ratchet** combine several DH exchanges to give messaging both forward secrecy and recovery after compromise (Chapter 13).
- **Key Encapsulation Mechanisms (KEMs).** A KEM is an interface where one party "encapsulates" a fresh random key to another's public key. DH can be framed as a KEM, and the post-quantum ML-KEM is one (Chapter 26). This shared interface is what makes hybrid classical plus post-quantum key exchange straightforward.

## Lab: exchange keys yourself

**1. A toy exchange in Python.**

```python
import secrets
p = 2**127 - 1          # a prime (far too small for real use)
g = 3
a, b = secrets.randbelow(p), secrets.randbelow(p)
A, B = pow(g, a, p), pow(g, b, p)
print(pow(B, a, p) == pow(A, b, p))   # True: same shared secret
```

**2. X25519 with OpenSSL.**

```bash
openssl genpkey -algorithm X25519 -out alice.key
openssl genpkey -algorithm X25519 -out bob.key
openssl pkey -in alice.key -pubout -out alice.pub
openssl pkey -in bob.key   -pubout -out bob.pub
openssl pkeyutl -derive -inkey alice.key -peerkey bob.pub | xxd -p
openssl pkeyutl -derive -inkey bob.key   -peerkey alice.pub | xxd -p
```

The two outputs are identical: both sides derived the same 32-byte secret without ever sending it.

**3. See what your browser negotiates.** Visit any major website, open the browser's developer tools, choose the Security tab, and look at the key exchange. Since late 2024, Chrome, Edge and Firefox negotiate **X25519MLKEM768** with most large sites: classic X25519 combined with a post-quantum KEM. Chapter 27 explains this hybrid.

## Failure story: Logjam and the shared primes

In May 2015, a team of 14 researchers published "Imperfect Forward Secrecy: How Diffie-Hellman Fails in Practice". They found two problems.

First, 8.4 percent of the top one million HTTPS websites still supported 512-bit export-grade DH, and a flaw in TLS 1.2 let an attacker in the middle downgrade normal connections to it. The researchers broke 512-bit groups in about 90 seconds per connection after a week of precomputation per prime.

Second, and more troubling, the expensive precomputation for the number field sieve depends only on the prime, not on individual keys. Because a few standard 1024-bit primes were hard-coded into widely used software, the researchers estimated that breaking a single common prime, at a cost of hundreds of millions of US dollars in custom hardware, would allow passive decryption of connections to about 18 percent of the top one million HTTPS sites and a large share of VPN servers. They noted that this matched descriptions in leaked intelligence documents of large-scale VPN decryption capabilities.

Browsers raised minimum DH sizes immediately, and TLS 1.3 later removed custom DH groups altogether. The lesson reaches beyond DH: shared parameters concentrate risk, and "nobody would spend that much" is not a threat model when the prize is everyone's traffic.

## Chapter summary

- Diffie-Hellman lets two parties create a shared secret over a public channel, based on the hardness of discrete logarithms. The secret is turned into keys with a KDF.
- ECDH gives the same exchange with much smaller, faster keys. X25519 is the modern default because it is fast and hard to misuse.
- Ephemeral keys give forward secrecy: stealing a long-term key later does not expose past sessions. TLS 1.3 requires it.
- DH alone is open to man-in-the-middle attacks. Authenticated key exchange adds signatures.
- Weak or shared parameters (Logjam) and unvalidated public keys (invalid-curve attacks) have broken real deployments.
