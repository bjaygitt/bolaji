## 15.1 The problem certificates solve

Public-key cryptography lets you verify a signature or encrypt to a key. It does not tell you **whose** key it is. If an attacker can make you believe their public key belongs to your bank, every guarantee collapses.

A **certificate** solves this with a signed statement: "this public key belongs to `www.bank.example`, signed by a certificate authority". If you already trust the CA's public key, you can check the signature and trust the binding. A certificate is like a passport: the passport office (CA) vouches that the photo (public key) belongs to the named person (subject), and border staff (clients) trust the passport office, not the traveller.

Certificates are **public**. Anyone can see and copy them. They are useless to an attacker without the matching **private key**, which never appears in the certificate.

The dominant certificate format is **X.509 version 3**, profiled for the internet in **RFC 5280**.

## 15.2 Anatomy of a certificate

A certificate has three parts: the **to-be-signed** data (all the fields below), the **signature algorithm**, and the **signature** by the issuing CA.

| Field | What it holds | Example or notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Version** | Always 3 for modern certificates | Extensions require version 3 |
| **Serial number** | A unique number assigned by the issuing CA | Must include at least 64 random bits for public certificates (Chapter 6) |
| **Signature algorithm** | How the CA signed | `ecdsa-with-SHA384`, `sha256WithRSAEncryption` |
| **Issuer** | The name of the CA that signed it | `C=US, O=Example CA, CN=Example TLS Issuing CA 2` |
| **Validity** | Not Before and Not After dates | Public TLS certificates: at most 200 days from March 2026 |
| **Subject** | Who the certificate is about | Often only a Common Name, sometimes organisation details |
| **Subject public key info** | The public key and its algorithm | ECDSA P-256, RSA 3072, and soon ML-DSA |
| **Extensions** | Everything else that matters (section 15.3) | Names, usages, constraints, policy, revocation pointers |

Names in the Subject and Issuer are **Distinguished Names (DNs)**, built from attributes such as `C` (country), `O` (organisation), `OU` (organisational unit) and `CN` (common name). Public CAs stopped including `OU` in TLS certificates in 2022, because it was rarely validated properly.

## 15.3 Extensions: where the real rules live

Extensions turn a bare key-to-name binding into a precise set of permissions. Each extension can be marked **critical**, meaning a client that does not understand it must reject the certificate.

| Extension | What it does | Typical value for a TLS server | Typical value for a CA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject Alternative Name (SAN)** | The names (DNS, IP, email, URI) the certificate is valid for | `DNS:shop.example.com, DNS:www.shop.example.com` | Not used |
| **Basic Constraints** | Whether this is a CA, and how many CAs may sit below it | `CA:FALSE` | `CA:TRUE, pathlen:0` for an issuing CA |
| **Key Usage** | Which operations the key may perform | `digitalSignature` | `keyCertSign, cRLSign` |
| **Extended Key Usage (EKU)** | Which applications it may be used for | `serverAuth` | Often restricted on intermediates |
| **Authority Key Identifier / Subject Key Identifier** | Fingerprints that link a certificate to its issuer's key | Present | Present |
| **CRL Distribution Points** | Where to download the revocation list | URL | URL |
| **Authority Information Access** | Where to fetch the issuer's certificate, and an OCSP responder if any | URL | URL |
| **Certificate Policies** | Identifiers of the rules under which it was issued | DV, OV or EV policy OIDs | Policy OIDs |
| **Name Constraints** | Limits which names a CA may issue for | Not used | `permitted: .example.com` |
| **SCT list** | Proof that the certificate was logged in Certificate Transparency | Embedded SCTs | Not used |

Three of these deserve emphasis.

**The SAN is the identity.** Browsers and modern clients check only the SAN, not the Common Name, when matching a hostname. A certificate with a CN but no SAN entry for the name fails.

**Basic Constraints is the most important safety switch in PKI.** It decides whether a certificate can sign other certificates. Bugs that ignored it, such as a 2002 flaw in Internet Explorer that accepted ordinary website certificates as CAs, let anyone with a cheap certificate impersonate any website.

**Name Constraints makes private CAs safer.** A CA constrained to `.example.com` cannot issue a trusted certificate for any other domain, even if its key is stolen. It is underused and worth adopting for internal CAs (Chapter 18).

- The **root CA** certificate is self-signed. Its trust comes not from the signature but from its presence in the client's **trust store**, the list of roots shipped by the operating system or browser. Root keys are kept offline in HSMs and used only to sign intermediates and revocation lists.
- **Intermediate CAs** are signed by the root and do the day-to-day issuing. If an intermediate is compromised, it can be revoked and replaced without changing every client's trust store.
- **Leaf** (end-entity) certificates are what servers, clients and devices present.

To validate a leaf, the client builds a **chain** (also called a path) from the leaf to a trusted root, checking at each link that the signature verifies, the issuer is allowed to be a CA, the dates are valid, constraints are respected and nothing is revoked. Chapter 17 covers path validation in detail.

### Cross-signing

A new CA's root takes years to reach enough trust stores. To bridge the gap, an established CA can **cross-sign** the new CA's intermediate or root, so the same key has two certificates: one chaining to the new root, one to the old. Clients with either root can build a valid chain. Let's Encrypt launched in 2015 this way, cross-signed by an established CA, and the expiry of that cross-signature in September 2021 broke connections on many old devices that did not have the newer root.

## 15.5 Encodings and file formats

Certificate files confuse everyone at first because the same content appears in many wrappers.

| Format | What it is | Typical extensions | How to recognise it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DER** | The binary encoding of a certificate (ASN.1 rules) | `.der`, `.cer` | Binary; unreadable in a text editor |
| **PEM** | DER in base64 between header lines | `.pem`, `.crt`, `.cer`, `.key` | Starts with `-----BEGIN CERTIFICATE-----` |
| **PKCS#12 / PFX** | A password-protected bundle of private key plus certificate chain | `.p12`, `.pfx` | Binary; used by Windows and Java |
| **PKCS#7 / P7B** | A bundle of certificates without keys | `.p7b`, `.p7c` | Used for chains on Windows |
| **PKCS#8** | A private key, optionally encrypted | `.key`, `.pem` | `-----BEGIN PRIVATE KEY-----` or `ENCRYPTED PRIVATE KEY` |
| **CSR (PKCS#10)** | A certificate signing request | `.csr` | `-----BEGIN CERTIFICATE REQUEST-----` |

A **certificate signing request (CSR)** contains the public key and requested names, signed with the private key to prove possession. The applicant sends the CSR to the CA; the private key never leaves the applicant.

## 15.6 Certificate types by purpose

| Type | Key usage and EKU | Validated how | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **TLS server (public)** | digitalSignature; serverAuth | Domain control (DV), plus organisation (OV) or extended (EV) checks | Governed by CA/Browser Forum rules (Chapter 16) |
| **TLS client** | digitalSignature; clientAuth | By the private CA's enrolment process | Moving to private PKI as public CAs drop clientAuth |
| **Code signing** | digitalSignature; codeSigning | Organisation validation; keys must be in hardware since 2023 | Paired with timestamps |
| **S/MIME email** | digitalSignature, keyEncipherment; emailProtection | Mailbox control and identity | Governed by its own baseline requirements since 2023 |
| **Document signing** | digitalSignature, nonRepudiation | Identity checks; qualified certificates under eIDAS in the EU | Long-term validation needed |
| **CA certificates** | keyCertSign, cRLSign; Basic Constraints CA:TRUE | Audits | Pathlen and name constraints limit scope |

## Lab: take certificates apart

**1. Fetch and read a real certificate.**

```bash
openssl s_client -connect www.wikipedia.org:443 -servername www.wikipedia.org -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null > chain.txt
openssl x509 -in chain.txt -noout -text | less
```

Find the serial number, issuer, validity dates, SAN list, key usage, EKU, CRL distribution point, authority information access and the embedded SCTs.

**2. Create a CSR and inspect it.**

```bash
openssl req -new -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
  -keyout lab.key -out lab.csr -subj "/CN=lab.example.test" \
  -addext "subjectAltName=DNS:lab.example.test,DNS:www.lab.example.test"
openssl req -in lab.csr -noout -text -verify
```

**3. Convert between formats.**

```bash
openssl x509 -in cert.pem -outform DER -out cert.der           # PEM to DER
openssl pkcs12 -export -inkey lab.key -in cert.pem -out lab.p12 # bundle key + cert
openssl pkcs12 -in lab.p12 -noout -info                         # inspect a bundle
```

**4. Check that a key and certificate match.** Compare the public key hashes:

```bash
openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -pubkey | openssl sha256
openssl pkey -in lab.key -pubout | openssl sha256
```

Identical hashes mean they are a pair. This single check solves a surprising number of deployment mysteries.

## Failure story: the root that expired

On 30 May 2020, a widely used root certificate, the AddTrust External CA Root, reached its expiry date. Modern browsers were unaffected because they had long since learned other paths to the same intermediate. But many servers still sent an old cross-signed chain ending at AddTrust, and many older clients and libraries, including some versions of OpenSSL and GnuTLS used by payment systems, APIs and embedded devices, stopped at the first path they found instead of searching for an alternative. Those clients began failing with "certificate has expired", even though the server's own certificate was valid.

Over the following days, outages hit payment processors, monitoring services and many internal integrations. The fix was to update the chain the servers sent, removing the expired cross-sign, and to update client libraries. A similar event followed in September 2021 when an older root expired and affected devices that had not received updated trust stores.

The lesson: a certificate's validity depends on the whole chain, not just the leaf. Inventory the chains you serve, know which roots your clients trust, and treat root and intermediate expiries as planned events.

## Chapter summary

- A certificate binds a public key to a name with a CA's signature. It is public; the private key is the secret.
- The key fields are serial, issuer, validity, subject and public key; the real rules live in extensions, especially SAN, Basic Constraints, Key Usage, EKU and Name Constraints.
- Chains link leaves to roots through intermediates; trust in the root comes from the client's trust store, not its signature.
- PEM, DER, PKCS#12, PKCS#7, PKCS#8 and CSRs are wrappers for the same few objects; know how to convert and inspect them.
- Certificates expire at every level of the chain. Root and intermediate expiries can break old clients.
