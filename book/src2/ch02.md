## 2.1 Why the map comes first

Chapter 1 ended with a question that took Meridian's GRC team six weeks to answer: which keys protect customer data, and who owns each one? The delay was not caused by missing cryptography. Meridian had plenty. It was caused by the absence of a map.

In a small company, one engineer can hold the whole cryptographic picture in their head: a few certificates on a web server, disk encryption on laptops, a password manager. In an enterprise of 18,000 people, cryptography is spread across hundreds of products bought by a dozen teams over twenty years. Each team sees its own piece. Network Engineering sees load balancer certificates and VPN tunnels. Platform Engineering sees Kubernetes secrets and cloud keys. Payments Security sees payment HSMs and card keys. Nobody sees the whole.

That gap matters for four practical reasons:

- **Outages.** A certificate that nobody knows about expires on schedule, and a service stops. The failure story at the end of this chapter is a famous example.
- **Incidents.** When a CA is distrusted, a library has a flaw or a key leaks, the first question is "where else is this used?" Without a map, the answer is guesswork.
- **Audits.** Regulators and assessors now ask for inventories of keys, certificates and cryptographic protocols, not just policies.
- **Change.** The move to post-quantum algorithms (Part XI) means replacing algorithms in every one of these places. You cannot plan a migration across places you have not found.

This chapter builds the map in layers: first the organisation, then the states of data, then the networks, then the individual places where keys and certificates live, and finally the owners.

## 2.2 Meet Meridian Financial Group

**Meridian Financial Group** is a fictional company, invented for this book. It is a realistic composite of the kind of organisation many readers work in or for, and it is deliberately described in product categories rather than vendor names, so it cannot be mistaken for any real company's environment.

### The business

Meridian is a mid-sized financial group of about 18,000 employees, headquartered in a fictional city. It runs three businesses:

- **Retail and commercial banking.** Current accounts, savings, mortgages and business lending. Its core banking system of record runs on a mainframe.
- **An insurance arm.** Home, motor and life policies, with long-lived customer records, medical information for life cover and large volumes of scanned claims documents.
- **Meridian Pay.** A payments processor that both issues cards to the bank's customers and acquires card payments for merchants, with terminals in shops and an e-commerce gateway.

Each business has different data with different lifetimes. A card number has a life of a few years before the card is reissued. A mortgage file must be kept for decades. A life insurance record may outlive the customer. Those lifetimes shape the threat model, as Chapter 1 showed.

### The footprint

Meridian's technology estate looks like this:

- Two owned data centres, **DC-East** and **DC-West**, linked by dark fibre (private optical fibre that Meridian leases and lights itself). Each runs a full production stack, so either can carry the business if the other fails.
- About 240 branches and offices, connected over an **SD-WAN**, a software-defined wide area network that builds encrypted tunnels over ordinary internet and carrier links.
- Three public clouds. Meridian uses all three hyperscale providers, for different workloads: customer-facing applications in one, data and analytics in another, and collaboration and identity services in the third.
- A **CDN** (content delivery network) and **WAF** (web application firewall) at the internet edge, operated by a specialist provider, through which all public web and API traffic arrives.
- A mobile banking app and a customer website.
- Partner APIs for **open banking**, where licensed third parties access customer accounts with the customer's consent.
- A mainframe for core banking.
- Kubernetes platforms on-premises and in the cloud.

### The scale

At the start of the book, Meridian's cryptographic estate contains about 65,000 certificates (roughly 4,000 publicly trusted, the rest from private CAs), a few thousand secrets in vaults, several HSM clusters (both general-purpose and payment) and about 9,000 physical and virtual servers.

### The regulators

Meridian is regulated as a bank and as a payments firm. It is subject to **PCI DSS** for card data, **SOX** for the integrity of financial reporting, **GLBA** for the protection of customer financial information in the United States, and regional privacy law. Because it also operates in the European Union, **GDPR**, **NIS2** and **DORA** apply. Every one of these frameworks touches cryptography somewhere, and Chapter 41 maps them in detail.

### The teams

Meridian's cryptography is spread across eight groups. You will meet them in every chapter:

| Team | What it runs |
| --- | --- |
| **Network Engineering** | The SD-WAN, data centre networks, firewalls, load balancers, VPN, Wi-Fi, the CDN and WAF configuration, DNS |
| **Platform Engineering** | Servers, virtualisation, storage, Kubernetes, the cloud accounts, the secrets platform, CI/CD, the mainframe platform |
| **Identity and Access Management (IAM)** | Active Directory, the single sign-on identity provider, MFA, privileged access, AD CS and device enrolment |
| **PKI and Cryptography Services** | Private CAs, public CA contracts, the certificate lifecycle management (CLM) platform, general-purpose HSMs, the enterprise key manager, cryptographic standards |

The reader's imagined home is the **PKI and Cryptography Services** team. It is small, about a dozen people, and it owns very little of the estate directly. Most of its work is setting standards, running shared services and persuading other teams.

## 2.3 Three states of data

The first way to sort cryptographic controls is by the state of the data they protect. The industry uses three states.

**Data at rest** is stored data: files on a disk, rows in a database, objects in cloud storage, backup tapes in a vault. The threat is someone who gets hold of the storage without going through the application: a stolen laptop, a discarded disk, a copied snapshot, a cloud operator, a backup administrator. Encryption at rest works at several layers (the disk, the volume, the database, the field), and each layer stops a different attacker. Part VI covers it.

**Data in transit** is data moving between two systems: a browser talking to the website, a microservice calling another, the SD-WAN carrying branch traffic, a payment message crossing to a card network. The threat is anyone on the path who can read or alter traffic. The main tools are TLS, IPsec, MACsec and SSH, all of which also authenticate the endpoints. Part V covers them.

**Data in use** is data being processed in memory by a running program. For most of computing history, cryptography could not protect it, because a CPU had to see the plaintext to compute on it. **Confidential computing**, which runs code inside hardware-protected memory that even the host operating system and cloud operator cannot read, and **privacy-enhancing technologies** such as multi-party computation now narrow that gap. Part VII covers them.

A useful habit is to ask, for any piece of sensitive data, "what protects it in each state?" Take a customer's card number at Meridian:

| State | Where the card number is | What protects it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In transit | From the phone to the CDN, then to the data centre | TLS from the app to the CDN, then a second TLS connection from the CDN to Meridian's load balancer |
| In transit | Between microservices in the payments platform | Mutual TLS in the service mesh |
| In use | Inside the authorisation service | Process isolation only, unless it runs in a confidential VM |
| At rest | In the payments database | Tokenisation or field-level encryption, plus database and volume encryption |
| At rest | In backups | Backup encryption with keys from the enterprise key manager |
| In transit | To the card network | A private link, plus PIN and message keys held in payment HSMs |

Two lessons follow. First, data changes state many times on a single journey, and each transition is a place where it is briefly exposed. Second, the strongest protection usually comes from encrypting early and decrypting late, at the application layer, so that intermediate systems only ever handle ciphertext. Chapter 47 traces a whole payment this way.

> **In practice.** When a project team tells you their data "is encrypted", ask which state they mean. "Encrypted" often means "the cloud provider encrypts the disk", which protects against a stolen drive in the provider's data centre and against nothing else.

## 2.4 Private networks and the public internet

The second way to sort controls is by the network they cross. A **trust boundary** is any line in an architecture where the level of trust changes: traffic or data on one side is controlled by different people, or exposed to different attackers, than on the other. Cryptography is concentrated at trust boundaries, because that is where you can no longer rely on the physical or administrative control of the path.

### The public internet

On the **public internet**, you control nothing in the path. Your customers' traffic crosses home routers, mobile carriers, internet exchanges and transit providers you have never heard of. Every connection must therefore be encrypted and authenticated end to end, and the authentication must work for strangers. That is why public-facing services use the **Web PKI**: certificates from publicly trusted certificate authorities (CAs), which every browser and phone already trusts (Chapter 13). It also means you live by other people's rules. Browser root programs and the CA/Browser Forum decide how long public certificates may last, which is why their maximum lifetime is falling towards 47 days (Chapter 13 and Part IV).

The edge is a special region of the public side. Meridian's CDN and WAF provider terminates customer TLS in its own data centres around the world, inspects the traffic and forwards it to Meridian. That means a third party holds keys for Meridian's public names. How that custody is arranged, and what the contract and the architecture must say about it, is the subject of Chapter 23.

### Private networks

On **private networks**, Meridian controls more of the path: the data centre switches, the dark fibre, the branch routers. It is tempting to treat everything inside as trusted. For many years enterprises did exactly that, encrypting at the perimeter and sending plaintext internally. Three changes ended that model:

- **Attackers get inside.** Phishing, stolen credentials and compromised suppliers put attackers on internal networks routinely. Plaintext internal traffic hands them passwords and data.
- **"Private" links are shared.** SD-WAN tunnels ride over the internet, carrier circuits pass through other people's equipment, and cloud interconnects pass through the provider's network.
- **Regulators expect it.** Card data, for example, must be protected wherever it crosses a network that is not fully trusted, and many security teams now apply the same rule everywhere.

The modern approach, often called **zero trust**, authenticates and encrypts every connection regardless of where it starts, using identities rather than network location. Private networks use private trust: certificates from Meridian's own CAs, which only Meridian's devices trust, and keys that never need to be accepted by a stranger. Chapter 22 covers private network encryption and Chapter 24 covers service-to-service encryption inside platforms.

Fifty-five rows, and Meridian's real discovery exercise found more: an old file transfer appliance with its own embedded CA, a building access system using smart cards, printers with self-signed management certificates. The pattern is consistent. Cryptography lives wherever two systems need to trust each other, and in a large enterprise that is almost everywhere.

Three observations from the table shape the rest of this book:

1. **Most rows are not owned by the cryptography team.** Of the 55, Meridian's PKI and Cryptography Services team owns six outright. The rest belong to teams whose main job is something else: running networks, platforms or applications.
2. **Most rows depend on a few shared services.** Certificates in rows 1 to 31 come from the CAs and CLM platform in rows 42 to 44. Keys in rows 35 to 40 come from the key managers in rows 45 to 47. If those shared services fail, everything fails with them.
3. **Every row has a lifecycle.** Each key or certificate is generated, stored, used, rotated and retired somewhere. The failures in Chapter 1 happened at those stages, and they can happen in any row.

> **Watch out.** The rows most often missing from enterprise inventories are the ones inside products: certificates embedded in appliances, keys baked into vendor software, internal CAs that a product creates on installation. They do not show up in your CA's issuance records, and they expire on a schedule set by someone else.

## 2.6 Who owns what

"Who owns this key?" sounds like a simple question. In practice it has at least three answers, and confusing them is the main reason enterprise cryptography goes unmanaged.

- **The system owner** runs the product where the key lives: the load balancer, the database, the Kubernetes cluster. They control change windows and configuration.
- **The key or certificate owner** is accountable for that specific key: that it is renewed before it expires, rotated on schedule and replaced if compromised. Usually this is the team that runs the service the key protects.
- **The policy owner** decides the rules: which algorithms and key sizes are allowed, which CAs may issue, how long certificates may last, where keys must be stored.

At Meridian, a typical load balancer certificate for an internal application has all three. Network Engineering owns the load balancer. The application team owns the certificate, because their service breaks if it expires. The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the policy and the CLM platform that issues and renews it. A **custodian** may also be involved: someone who handles a key on behalf of its owner, such as the CDN provider holding a key for a public name, or Payments Security staff holding key components during a ceremony.

The usual tool for writing this down is a **RACI** matrix, which records for each activity who is Responsible for doing it, who is Accountable for the outcome, who is Consulted and who is Informed. A simplified version for Meridian looks like this:

| Activity | PKI and Crypto Services | Network Eng | Platform Eng | IAM | App teams | Payments Security | GRC | SOC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cryptographic standard | A, R | C | C | C | I | C | C | I |
| Private CA operation | A, R | I | I | C | I | I | I | I |
| Certificate renewal on load balancers | C | R | I | I | A | I | I | I |
| Kubernetes workload certificates | C | I | A, R | I | I | I | I | I |
| Cloud KMS key policy | C | I | A, R | C | C | I | C | I |
| Payment key ceremonies | C | I | I | I | I | A, R | C | I |
| Cryptographic inventory and evidence | R | C | C | C | C | C | A | I |
| Key compromise response | R | C | C | C | C | C | I | A |

The table shows something important about the reader's home team. The PKI and Cryptography Services team is accountable for a few central services and the standard, but for most activities it is "consulted": it advises, provides tools and sets guardrails, while other teams do the work. Its influence comes from making the secure path the easy path, through automation and shared services, rather than from owning everything. Chapter 42 returns to this operating model.

> **At Meridian.** After the six-week exercise from Chapter 1, GRC made "key and certificate owner" a mandatory field in the configuration management database (CMDB). The CLM platform now refuses to issue a certificate without an owning application, and any certificate discovered with no owner is assigned to the system owner by default. Orphaned certificates fell from about a fifth of the inventory to a few hundred in the first year.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requires accurate network and data-flow diagrams for the cardholder data environment (requirements 1.2.3 and 1.2.4) and an inventory of trusted keys and certificates used to protect card data in transit (4.2.1.1). NIS2 lists policies on cryptography and encryption among the cybersecurity risk-management measures in Article 21. DORA requires financial entities to identify and map their ICT assets and dependencies. A map like the one in this chapter, with owners, is the evidence behind all of these.

## 2.7 The same map, different products

Each row in section 2.5 can be filled by several kinds of product, and each kind comes from several vendors. The book compares them side by side, from public documentation, dated where details may change. As of 2026, for example, CDN and WAF services include Akamai, Cloudflare, Fastly and Amazon CloudFront; general-purpose HSMs include Thales Luna, Entrust nShield, Utimaco and Securosys, alongside cloud HSM services; cloud key management means AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault and Managed HSM, or Google Cloud KMS; CLM platforms include CyberArk Certificate Manager, Keyfactor Command, AppViewX and DigiCert Trust Lifecycle Manager, with open-source tools such as cert-manager alongside; and payment HSMs include Thales payShield, Futurex and Utimaco Atalla. Within each category, products differ in where keys are generated and stored, which management APIs they offer and how they cluster.

No enterprise uses one vendor for every row, and this book never presents one combined stack as "the" enterprise setup. Meridian itself is described only in categories: "its CLM platform", "its cloud KMS", "its payment HSMs". What matters for an architect is not which logo sits in a box but how the box integrates with the rest of the map: which protocols it speaks (ACME, EST, KMIP, PKCS#11), where its keys live and who can reach its management interface.

## 2.8 Design decisions the map forces

Once you have the map, a handful of architectural questions appear in every row. You will meet them repeatedly in later chapters.

- **Where should TLS terminate?** At the CDN, the load balancer, the ingress controller, a sidecar or the application itself? Each termination point is a place where data is briefly in plaintext and a key must be held.
- **Where should keys be generated and stored?** On the device, in a central service, in an HSM or in a cloud KMS? Generating keys inside the device that uses them avoids moving them, but makes backup and inspection harder.
- **Public or private trust?** Anything a stranger's device must trust needs the Web PKI and its rules. Everything else can use private CAs, with longer flexibility and no dependence on browser policy.
- **Central or federated?** Should one team run a shared service, or should each platform run its own? Central services give consistency and a single inventory; federated ones give autonomy and avoid a single point of failure.
- **Who holds the keys to the cloud?** Provider-managed keys, customer-managed keys inside the provider, imported keys or keys held outside the provider entirely each move the trust boundary in Figure 2.1.

There is rarely one right answer. A good architect can explain which option was chosen for each row, why, and what risk was accepted.

## 2.10 Lab: map the cryptography on your own machine

This lab builds a miniature version of Meridian's discovery exercise on a laptop. You need OpenSSL and Docker. Commands are for Linux or macOS.

1. Inspect a public website's certificate chain, as a scanner would.

```bash
openssl s_client -connect www.example.com:443 \
  -servername www.example.com -showcerts < /dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -dates
```

Note the issuer (a public CA) and the expiry date.

2. Count the root CAs your system trusts. On Debian or Ubuntu:

```bash
awk '/BEGIN CERT/{n++} END{print n}' /etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt
```

On macOS, run `security find-certificate -a -p /System/Library/Keychains/SystemRootCertificates.keychain | grep -c BEGIN`.

3. Find your SSH keys and print their fingerprints and types.

```bash
for f in ~/.ssh/*.pub /etc/ssh/ssh_host_*_key.pub; do
  [ -f "$f" ] && ssh-keygen -lf "$f"
done
```

4. Check whether your disk is encrypted at rest: `lsblk -f` on Linux (look for `crypto_LUKS`), `fdesetup status` on macOS, or `manage-bde -status` on Windows.

5. Start a private service with a self-signed certificate, the kind that hides in every enterprise.

```bash
openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
  -nodes -keyout lab.key -out lab.crt -days 30 -subj "/CN=lab.internal"
cat > lab.conf <<'EOF'
server { listen 8443 ssl; ssl_certificate /etc/nginx/lab.crt;
  ssl_certificate_key /etc/nginx/lab.key; location / { return 200; } }
EOF
docker run -d --name lab -p 8443:8443 \
  -v "$PWD/lab.conf:/etc/nginx/conf.d/lab.conf:ro" \
  -v "$PWD/lab.crt:/etc/nginx/lab.crt:ro" \
  -v "$PWD/lab.key:/etc/nginx/lab.key:ro" nginx:stable
```

6. Discover it as a scanner would, without trusting it.

```bash
openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 < /dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -enddate
```

7. Record everything you found in a small inventory file with these columns: location, type (certificate, SSH key, disk key), algorithm, expiry, state of data protected, owner. Then assign each entry to a row of the table in section 2.5. Remove the container with `docker rm -f lab` when you are done.

**Optional cloud extension.** In a cloud account you control, list keys and certificates with the provider's command-line tool, for example `aws kms list-keys` and `aws acm list-certificates`, `az keyvault list`, or `gcloud kms keyrings list --location global`. Add them to your inventory. Cost warning: listing is free or nearly free, but any keys or certificates you create to experiment with may carry monthly charges, so delete them afterwards.

## 2.11 Failure story: the certificate inside someone else's software

On 6 December 2018, mobile data services failed for millions of customers of O2 in the United Kingdom and SoftBank in Japan, and operators in several other countries reported disruption. Phones showed signal but could not connect to data, and businesses and devices that relied on those mobile networks were disrupted too.

The equipment vendor, Ericsson, said its initial analysis found the main issue was an expired certificate in two specific software versions of a core network component (the SGSN-MME, which manages mobile data sessions) installed with the affected operators. Its chief executive apologised publicly, and the faulty software versions were decommissioned. Services were restored within about a day.

The operators did not issue that certificate, and it would not have appeared in any inventory built from their own CAs. It lived inside a vendor's software, on a schedule set by the vendor. The lessons map directly onto this chapter:

- Your map must include cryptography **inside products**, not only certificates you issued. Ask vendors which certificates and keys their products embed, and when they expire.
- **Shared dependencies multiply impact.** One certificate in one component took down data services across a whole national network.
- **Ownership must be explicit**, including for third-party components. Someone has to be accountable for knowing that an embedded certificate exists.

## 2.12 Chapter summary

- Meridian Financial Group is a fictional bank, insurer and payments processor with about 18,000 employees, two data centres, about 240 branches, three clouds, a CDN, a mainframe and Kubernetes, described only in product categories.
- Cryptographic controls can be sorted by the state of data they protect (at rest, in transit, in use) and by the network they cross (public internet or private network).
- Cryptography concentrates at trust boundaries. Modern designs encrypt and authenticate inside private networks too, rather than trusting the perimeter.
- Public-facing services depend on the Web PKI and its external rules; internal services use private trust that the enterprise controls.
- A typical enterprise has well over 40 places where cryptography lives, across the edge, the private network, identity, platforms, data stores, key infrastructure, payments and business processes.
- Most of those places are owned by teams other than the cryptography team. System owner, key owner, policy owner and custodian are different roles.
- A few shared services (CAs, CLM, HSMs, key managers, cloud KMS) underpin almost everything, so their availability and security matter most.
- The rest of the book follows this map, part by part, and ends by tracing one transaction through all of it.
