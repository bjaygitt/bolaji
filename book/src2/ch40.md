## 40.1 Why standards and validation matter

When Meridian's PKI and Cryptography Services team writes "use AES-256 in GCM mode" in a design standard, it is leaning on a chain of other people's work. A US federal standard defines AES. A NIST publication defines GCM. An IETF RFC defines how TLS carries it. A testing laboratory checked that a particular library implements it correctly, and a government programme issued a certificate saying so. If any link in that chain is weak, the design is weak, however carefully the team wrote it.

Two different kinds of assurance are at work here, and the rest of the chapter keeps them apart:

- A **standard** is a published specification that says what something is or how it must behave: an algorithm, a protocol, a key format or a set of security requirements. Standards give everyone the same definition, so that a key wrapped by one vendor's HSM can be unwrapped by another's.
- **Validation** (or **certification**) is an independent check that a specific product meets a standard. A laboratory tests the product, and a scheme owner issues a certificate. Validation says nothing about products that were not tested, or about the same product configured differently.

Enterprises need both. Standards let you write requirements that vendors understand. Validation lets you check a vendor's claim without testing every product yourself. Regulators lean on both: US federal systems must use cryptographic modules validated under FIPS 140, PCI DSS refers to "strong cryptography" defined by industry-tested standards, and EU trust services rely on certified hardware. Chapter 41 shows how those rules map to controls; this chapter explains the machinery underneath them.

## 40.2 The standards bodies

Five organisations produce most of the cryptographic standards an enterprise architect meets. They differ in who can take part, how decisions are made, whether the documents are free, and how much legal weight the results carry.

### NIST

The US **National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)** is a federal agency within the Department of Commerce. Its cryptographic work comes in two main series:

- **Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS)** are mandatory for US federal systems once approved by the Secretary of Commerce. FIPS 197 (AES), FIPS 180-4 (SHA-2), FIPS 186-5 (digital signatures) and FIPS 203 to 205 (the post-quantum algorithms) are examples.
- **Special Publications in the 800 series (SPs)** give recommendations and guidance: SP 800-38D for GCM, SP 800-57 for key management, SP 800-90A for random bit generators, SP 800-131A for algorithm transitions.

NIST publishes drafts for public comment, usually an **initial public draft (ipd)** and sometimes a **final public draft (fpd)**, and publishes the comments it receives. For new algorithms it runs open international competitions: AES (selected in 2000), SHA-3 (selected in 2012) and the post-quantum process that began in 2016 and produced FIPS 203, 204 and 205 in August 2024. Everything NIST publishes is free to download. Although FIPS bind only US federal agencies, the rest of the world adopts them because vendors build to them and other standards reference them.

### ISO/IEC

The **International Organization for Standardization (ISO)** and the **International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC)** work together on IT through a **Joint Technical Committee, JTC 1**. Its subcommittee **SC 27** (information security, cybersecurity and privacy protection) owns ISO/IEC 27001, the Common Criteria (ISO/IEC 15408), the cryptographic module standard ISO/IEC 19790 and algorithm standards such as ISO/IEC 18033 (encryption). Financial cryptography sits separately in **ISO TC 68** (financial services), which owns standards such as ISO 9564 for PIN handling and ISO 11568 for retail key management.

ISO members are **national bodies**, one per country: ANSI for the United States, BSI for the United Kingdom, DIN for Germany. Experts are nominated through their national body. A document moves through formal stages: a new work item proposal, working drafts, committee drafts, a **Draft International Standard (DIS)** ballot, a **Final Draft International Standard (FDIS)** ballot, then publication. Each published standard is reviewed at least every five years. ISO standards are sold, not given away, which is one reason engineers often meet them second-hand through national adoptions or NIST references.

### IETF

The **Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF)** writes the protocols of the internet: TLS, IPsec, SSH, X.509 profiles, ACME, HPKE. Anyone can join by subscribing to a mailing list, and there is no membership or vote. Work happens in **working groups** (TLS, LAMPS, ACME and others) and decisions are made by **rough consensus**, judged by the working group chairs, and approved by the **Internet Engineering Steering Group (IESG)**. A proposal starts as an **Internet-Draft**, which expires after six months unless revised, and only becomes an **RFC** once approved.

Not every RFC is a standard. The standards track has two maturity levels, **Proposed Standard** and **Internet Standard**, and other RFCs are Informational, Experimental or Best Current Practice. Cryptographic algorithm specifications such as X25519 and EdDSA came through the **Crypto Forum Research Group (CFRG)** of the IRTF, the IETF's research sister, as Informational RFCs that standards-track protocols then reference. All RFCs are free.

### ETSI

The **European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI)** is one of the three recognised European Standardisation Organisations, with CEN and CENELEC. Its membership is organisations (vendors, operators, government bodies, universities) and it publishes several document types: **European Standards (EN)**, which go through national approval, and **Technical Specifications (TS)** and **Technical Reports (TR)**, which are faster.

Two parts of ETSI matter most for cryptography. The **Electronic Signatures and Infrastructures (ESI)** committee writes the EN 319 series that underpins EU trust services under eIDAS: policy and security requirements for trust service providers and certificate authorities (EN 319 401 and EN 319 411), certificate profiles (EN 319 412) and signature formats. The **Cyber** technical committee runs work on quantum-safe cryptography, including a specification for hybrid key exchange. ETSI documents are free.

### ANSI X9

**Accredited Standards Committee X9**, accredited by the **American National Standards Institute (ANSI)**, writes standards for the US financial services industry. Its members are banks, payment networks, processors and vendors. X9 standards define the cryptography of card payments: ANSI X9.24 for retail symmetric key management (including DUKPT), ANSI X9.143 for interoperable key blocks, and the technical reports TR-31 and TR-34 that payment HSMs implement (Chapter 37 and Chapter 38). X9 also acts for the United States in ISO TC 68. X9 standards are sold, and PCI requirements refer to them, so a payments team has to buy them.

### How the bodies interlock

These organisations reference each other constantly, and the references are how a requirement in one place reaches a product in another:

| Example | How it connects |
| --- | --- |
| FIPS 140-3 | Adopts ISO/IEC 19790 and ISO/IEC 24759 as its requirements and test methods, with US modifications in the SP 800-140 series |
| FIPS 186-5 | Approves EdDSA by referring to the IETF's RFC 8032 |
| SP 800-208 | Approves the stateful hash-based signatures LMS and XMSS defined in IETF RFC 8554 and RFC 8391 |
| PCI PIN and PCI DSS | Refer to ANSI X9 and ISO TC 68 key management standards |
| eIDAS trust services | Rely on ETSI EN 319 series policies and on Common Criteria protection profiles for signature devices |
| TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) | Uses algorithms defined by NIST (AES, SHA-2) and by the CFRG (X25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305) |

National agencies add their own layer. Germany's BSI publishes technical guidelines such as TR-02102 on recommended algorithms, France's ANSSI publishes its own rules, and the UK NCSC publishes guidance (Chapter 41). These usually select from international standards rather than invent new algorithms.

> **In practice.** When a vendor tells you a product "supports the standard", ask which document, which version and which options. "Supports TLS" may mean TLS 1.2 with RSA key exchange only. "Supports PKCS#11" may mean version 2.20 without the mechanisms you need. Requirements written with document numbers and versions save months of argument later.

## 40.3 How a standard moves from idea to requirement

The bodies differ in style, but the same pattern repeats. Understanding it helps you predict when something will become mandatory and how much warning you will get.

1. **Research and attacks.** New ideas and new breaks appear in academic papers and conferences. The 2017 SHA-1 collision (Chapter 7) and the 2016 start of NIST's post-quantum process both began here.
2. **Specification.** A body writes a precise document. Competitions add years of public cryptanalysis before anything is chosen; the post-quantum process ran for eight years from call to final standards.
3. **Adoption by other standards.** Protocols and profiles pick up the new algorithm. ML-KEM was in IETF drafts for TLS hybrid key exchange before FIPS 203 was final.
4. **Validation programmes.** Test methods and algorithm test vectors are written so laboratories can check implementations.
5. **Policy and regulation.** Governments and industry bodies set dates: SP 800-131A transitions, the NSA's CNSA 2.0 timeline, PCI DSS future-dated requirements.
6. **Retirement.** Old algorithms are deprecated (allowed with risk acceptance), then disallowed. NIST uses precise words for this: **acceptable**, **deprecated**, **legacy use** (allowed only to process existing data, such as verifying old signatures) and **disallowed**.

The gap between step 2 and step 5 is often a decade, and that is good news for architects: almost nothing becomes mandatory without years of visible warning. The organisations that get caught out are the ones that do not read the drafts. Assign someone in your team to watch NIST CSRC, the relevant IETF working groups and your regulators, and to brief the architecture board quarterly.

> **Current development.** As of October 2026, NIST's revision of SP 800-131A (Revision 3, initial public draft October 2024) and its post-quantum transition report, IR 8547 (initial public draft November 2024), are the documents to watch. The drafts propose moving the minimum security strength for symmetric cryptography to 128 bits, deprecating 112-bit quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms after 2030 and disallowing quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms after 2035. Check CSRC for the final versions before you quote dates in policy.

## 40.4 FIPS 140-3 and the CMVP

### What a cryptographic module is

**FIPS 140-3, Security Requirements for Cryptographic Modules**, defines how a **cryptographic module** must be built and tested. A cryptographic module is a defined set of hardware, software or firmware that implements approved security functions and sits within a clear **cryptographic boundary**. The boundary is the key idea. An HSM's boundary is usually its tamper-resistant enclosure. A software library's boundary is a specific shared object, such as one `.so` or `.dll` file, running on a stated operating system. Anything outside the boundary, including the application that calls the library, is not validated.

FIPS 140-3 was approved in March 2019 and took effect in September 2019. It replaced FIPS 140-2 (2001) and, unlike its predecessor, does not write its own requirements: it adopts the international standards **ISO/IEC 19790:2012** (requirements) and **ISO/IEC 24759:2017** (test requirements), with US-specific additions in the **SP 800-140 series**. SP 800-140A to 140F cover the documentation, the security policy format, the approved security functions, approved key generation and establishment methods, approved authentication methods and approved non-invasive attack mitigations.

### The four security levels

FIPS 140-3 defines four increasing **security levels**. A module receives an overall level, which is the lowest level it achieves across the areas tested.

| Level | Physical security | Operator authentication | Typical products |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Production-grade components; no specific physical protection | None required | Software libraries (OpenSSL FIPS provider, operating system crypto) |
| 2 | Tamper-evident coatings, seals or locks | Role-based | Some smart cards, appliances with sealed enclosures |
| 3 | Tamper-resistant enclosure with detection and response that zeroises keys | Identity-based | Most general-purpose and payment HSMs, cloud HSM services |
| 4 | Complete envelope of protection, active tamper response, protection against environmental attacks (voltage, temperature) | Multi-factor, identity-based | A small number of specialised HSMs |

**Zeroisation** means actively destroying keys and other sensitive data, for example when an enclosure is opened. At Level 3 and above, plaintext secret keys may not cross the module's interfaces except under protections such as encryption or split knowledge.

### The twelve areas

ISO/IEC 19790 organises the requirements into twelve areas, and a security policy has one section for each: general; cryptographic module specification; cryptographic module interfaces; roles, services and authentication; software and firmware security; operational environment; physical security; non-invasive security; **sensitive security parameter (SSP)** management; self-tests; life-cycle assurance; and mitigation of other attacks. FIPS 140-3 calls keys and other secrets SSPs, divided into **critical security parameters (CSPs)**, whose disclosure breaks security, and **public security parameters (PSPs)**, such as public keys, whose modification breaks it.

Two areas often surprise newcomers. **Self-tests** require the module to test its own algorithms before use (known-answer tests) and to check its own integrity, for example with an HMAC over the library file; if a test fails, the module refuses to work. **Approved mode** is the state in which only approved services are available. Many modules can also run non-approved algorithms, so the module must make clear which mode it is in, and an application using MD5 through a validated library is not using validated cryptography for that operation.

### Who does the testing

The **Cryptographic Module Validation Program (CMVP)** is run jointly by NIST and the **Canadian Centre for Cyber Security**. The CMVP does not test products itself. Vendors hire an accredited **Cryptographic and Security Testing Laboratory (CSTL)**, accredited through NIST's NVLAP programme, which tests the module and submits a report. CMVP reviewers then examine the report, ask questions and, when satisfied, issue a certificate.

Algorithms are validated first and separately by the **Cryptographic Algorithm Validation Program (CAVP)**. Each approved algorithm in a module must have a CAVP certificate, now produced through the automated **ACVP** protocol, in which the implementation answers test vectors served by NIST. Entropy sources have their own **Entropy Source Validation (ESV)** process against SP 800-90B. A module certificate therefore sits on top of several algorithm and entropy certificates.

The process is slow. Modules wait in queues that have historically stretched past a year, and the CMVP publishes a **Modules In Process** list showing where submissions stand (review pending, in review, coordination). In 2024, to reduce a backlog, the CMVP offered an **interim validation** option for modules submitted before 2024, with a shorter two-year sunset that could later be extended. A product listed "in process" is not validated; it may never be.

### Certificate status and sunset

Every certificate is in one of three states:

- **Active**: currently valid for new procurements.
- **Historical**: no longer valid for new procurements. Agencies may keep using such modules in existing systems after making their own risk determination. Certificates become historical when they reach their **sunset date**, normally five years after validation, or when a policy change moves them.
- **Revoked**: withdrawn, usually because of a serious flaw. Revoked modules should not be used.

## 40.5 Reading a certificate and a security policy

Validation only helps if you read what was validated. The CMVP website lets anyone search by vendor, module name or certificate number. Each certificate page has a consistent layout. The example below is fictional, laid out the way real entries are:

```text
Certificate #9999 (illustrative, fictional module)
Module Name        Example Crypto Library
Standard           FIPS 140-3
Status             Active
Sunset Date        2030-05-14
Overall Level      1
Caveat             When operated in approved mode
Security Level     Physical security: N/A
  Exceptions       Non-invasive security: N/A
                   Mitigation of other attacks: N/A
Module Type        Software
Embodiment         Multi-chip standalone
Tested             Ubuntu 22.04 on Intel Xeon (x86_64)
  Configuration(s) RHEL 9 on AWS Graviton3 (ARMv8)
Approved Algs      AES-GCM A1234, SHA2-256 A1234,
                   ECDSA KeyGen/SigGen A1234, DRBG A1234
Software Version   3.1.2
Related Files      Security Policy, Consolidated Certificate
```

Read it in this order:

1. **Standard and status.** FIPS 140-2 or FIPS 140-3? Active or Historical? When is the sunset date, and will it pass during your contract or the system's life?
2. **Module name and version.** The certificate covers exactly the listed software, firmware or hardware versions. A vendor shipping version 3.4 of a library whose certificate lists 3.1.2 is not shipping a validated module, unless 3.1.2 is the code actually loaded.
3. **Tested configurations.** These are the operating environments the laboratory tested: operating system, processor and sometimes hypervisor. Running on an untested platform is permitted only under specific CMVP rules, usually as a **vendor-affirmed** porting that the vendor, not the laboratory, vouches for. Auditors increasingly ask which category applies.
4. **Overall level and exceptions.** "Level 3 overall" for an HSM is normal. Check whether any area is lower or marked not applicable.
5. **Caveats.** Phrases such as "when operated in approved mode" or notes on entropy strength are conditions, not decoration. If the caveat requires a configuration, your build must set it.
6. **Approved algorithms.** Check that the algorithms you need are listed with CAVP certificate references, and in the modes you use.

The **security policy** is the public, non-proprietary document that accompanies the certificate. Under FIPS 140-3 it follows the SP 800-140B structure, one section per area, so you can go straight to what you need:

- **Section 2 (cryptographic module specification)** describes the boundary, lists approved and non-approved algorithms, and explains how to tell which mode the module is in.
- **Section 4 (roles, services and authentication)** lists every service and whether it is approved.
- **Section 9 (SSP management)** says how keys are generated, entered, stored and zeroised, and which random bit generator is used.
- **Section 11 (life-cycle assurance)** includes the **crypto officer guidance**: the installation and configuration steps required to run in approved mode. This is the section your platform team must implement and your auditors will compare against.

> **Watch out.** A validated HSM can still be configured outside approved mode. Several general-purpose HSMs offer a "FIPS mode" or policy flag that disables non-approved mechanisms, and some settings can only be changed by re-initialising the partition. Decide the mode before you load production keys (Chapter 33), not after an auditor asks.

## 40.6 The FIPS 140-2 transition

For nearly two decades FIPS 140-2 was the module standard, and many products in service today were validated against it. The transition to FIPS 140-3 ran on a long, published timetable:

- FIPS 140-3 took effect in September 2019, and the CMVP began accepting FIPS 140-3 submissions in September 2020.
- The CMVP stopped accepting new FIPS 140-2 submissions in September 2021, although modules already submitted continued through the queue.
- On 21 September 2026 the remaining FIPS 140-2 certificates moved to the **Historical** list.

Historical status does not make a product insecure overnight, and it does not force agencies to rip out installed equipment. It means the module should not be bought for new systems where FIPS 140 validation is required, and that existing use needs a documented risk decision and a replacement plan. For enterprises that are not federal agencies, the practical effect comes through contracts and audits: FedRAMP authorisations, US government customers and some regulators treat only Active certificates as current.

> **At Meridian.** When the PKI and Cryptography Services team reviewed its module inventory in 2025, it found three categories still resting on FIPS 140-2 certificates: an older generation of load balancer cryptographic cards in DC-West, a file transfer appliance used by the insurance arm, and a software library embedded in a vendor's mainframe connector. None was insecure, but Meridian's US government payment programmes require current validation. GRC opened time-limited exceptions for each, owned by the system owners, while procurement replaced the cards in the scheduled hardware refresh and asked the two vendors for FIPS 140-3 certificate numbers and dates. The exceptions are tracked in the same register as every other cryptographic exception (Chapter 42).

## 40.7 Common Criteria and protection profiles

FIPS 140-3 answers a narrow question: does this module implement approved cryptography correctly and protect its keys? **Common Criteria (CC)**, standardised as **ISO/IEC 15408** with its evaluation methodology in ISO/IEC 18045, answers a broader one: does this product meet the security claims made for it? It applies to whole products such as firewalls, operating systems, smart cards and full-disk encryption software.

The vocabulary is specific:

- The **Target of Evaluation (TOE)** is the product and configuration being evaluated.
- The **Security Target (ST)** is the vendor's document stating the security functions the TOE provides, the threats it counters and the assumptions it relies on.
- A **Protection Profile (PP)** is a reusable, implementation-independent set of requirements for a product type, written by a government or industry group. An ST can claim conformance to one or more PPs.
- **Evaluation Assurance Levels (EAL1 to EAL7)** describe how deeply the evaluators examined the product, from functional testing to formal design verification. An EAL can be **augmented** with extra assurance components, written as "EAL4+" with the added components named, for example a vulnerability analysis against attackers of high attack potential.

The **Common Criteria Recognition Arrangement (CCRA)** lets certificates issued by one member nation's scheme be recognised by others, but only up to EAL2 or for evaluations against agreed **collaborative Protection Profiles (cPPs)**. Europe historically recognised higher levels through the SOG-IS arrangement and, since February 2025, operates the **EUCC** scheme under the EU Cybersecurity Act, which gives Common Criteria certificates EU-wide standing.

Different schemes use CC differently:

- **NIAP**, the US scheme, evaluates only against approved PPs with exact conformance and does not assign EALs. Its Product Compliant List is required for commercial security products in US national security systems, and products such as VPN gateways, network devices and disk encryption are evaluated against PPs for those categories. NIAP-evaluated products that perform cryptography are also expected to use CAVP-validated algorithms.
- **European schemes** such as Germany's BSI and France's ANSSI certify smart cards, secure elements and signature devices at high EALs with demanding vulnerability analysis. EU qualified signature creation devices under eIDAS are certified against CC protection profiles.

| | FIPS 140-3 | Common Criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is assessed | A cryptographic module inside its boundary | A product's stated security functions |
| Requirements come from | ISO/IEC 19790 plus NIST SP 800-140 series | The Security Target, often conforming to a Protection Profile |
| Algorithm testing | Mandatory CAVP validation | Depends on the scheme and PP |
| Result | Certificate with overall level 1 to 4 | Certificate with EAL or PP conformance |
| Typical buyers who require it | US and Canadian government, FedRAMP, regulated industries | National security buyers, EU trust services, smart card and payment schemes |

The two are complementary. A good HSM often holds both a FIPS 140-3 Level 3 certificate and a CC certificate, and a payment HSM adds **PCI PTS HSM** approval (Chapter 39). A firewall's CC certificate tells you its VPN feature was evaluated; its FIPS certificate, if it has one, tells you the crypto library inside was validated.

## 40.8 "FIPS compliant", "FIPS validated" and other claims

Marketing language blurs these ideas, sometimes innocently and sometimes not. Learn to translate:

| Claim | What it usually means | What to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FIPS 140-3 validated** | The product contains, or is, a module with an Active CMVP certificate | The certificate number, the version, the tested configuration, and whether the shipped version matches |
| **FIPS validated module inside** or **embeds a validated module** | A validated library is used within a larger product | Is all the product's cryptography routed through that module, and is it in approved mode by default? |
| **FIPS compliant**, **FIPS capable** or **FIPS approved algorithms** | The product uses algorithms that FIPS approves, but the implementation was not validated | Usually nothing more to ask: it does not satisfy a FIPS 140 validation requirement |
| **FIPS mode** | A configuration that restricts the product to approved algorithms, often by loading a validated module | Which module and certificate does FIPS mode load, and what breaks when it is enabled? |
| **In process** or **pending validation** | Submitted to the CMVP, not validated | Accept only with a dated exception and a fallback plan |
| **Military-grade encryption** | Nothing defined | Ask for algorithms, key sizes, modules and certificates |

The key distinction is simple. **FIPS compliant** describes algorithms. **FIPS validated** describes a tested implementation with a certificate. A product can use AES-256 perfectly and still not be validated, and a validated library can be bypassed by a product that also ships its own unvalidated code path.

### How products deliver validated cryptography

Products reach validation by different routes, and the route affects how you configure and patch them (as of 2026, based on public documentation):

- **Open-source libraries.** The OpenSSL project publishes a FIPS provider for OpenSSL 3, validated as a separate module that applications load through configuration. AWS-LC and Google's BoringSSL have validated modules (AWS-LC FIPS and BoringCrypto) used in their respective ecosystems. Bouncy Castle offers separately validated FIPS editions for Java and C#, and wolfSSL offers wolfCrypt FIPS.
- **Operating systems.** Red Hat Enterprise Linux and Ubuntu Pro submit their kernel and user-space crypto libraries for validation and provide a FIPS mode that switches the system to them. Microsoft validates the Windows cryptographic primitives (CNG, built on the SymCrypt library) and offers a group policy setting that restricts applications to FIPS-approved algorithms.
- **Language runtimes.** Go 1.24 introduced a native FIPS 140-3 mode for its standard library cryptography, and Java applications typically reach validation through a validated provider such as Bouncy Castle FIPS or a PKCS#11 provider backed by an HSM.
- **Hardware.** HSMs from Thales, Entrust, Utimaco, Securosys and others, and cloud HSM services from the major providers, carry Level 3 certificates. Cloud KMS services also publish the certificates of the HSMs behind them, which matters when an auditor asks how a KMS key is protected (Chapter 28).

> **Watch out.** Patching and validation pull in opposite directions. A validated library version is frozen at the version tested, but vulnerabilities are found in it later. Most vendors respond by shipping fixes in new versions that are submitted for validation while customers run them. Agree in advance, with GRC and your auditors, how you will choose between running the validated version with a known flaw and running a patched version that is not yet validated. FedRAMP's cryptographic module policy, revised in January 2025, explicitly allows providers to choose a documented update stream for this reason.

## 40.9 Design decisions and procurement

Validation requirements should be written as deliberately as any other requirement. The architect's decisions are:

- **Where validation is required.** Meridian requires FIPS 140-3 validated modules for every system in scope of US government programmes, for all HSMs and for its central KMS, PKI and secrets platforms. For ordinary application TLS it requires approved algorithms through the platform's standard libraries, and it does not demand validation for every internal microservice. Writing "FIPS everywhere" sounds safe but produces a flood of exceptions that hide the ones that matter.
- **Which level.** Level 3 for anything holding root CA keys, payment keys or KMS master keys; Level 1 is normal and acceptable for software libraries.
- **What evidence to collect.** Certificate number, module version, tested configuration, security policy section 11 settings, and proof that the deployed system runs in approved mode.
- **How to handle sunset dates.** Track sunset dates in the cryptographic inventory (Chapter 43) as you track certificate expiry, so that the next transition is a planned refresh and not a surprise.
- **Common Criteria.** Require NIAP or EUCC certificates where a customer or regulator does, for network security products in particular, and remember that PP conformance tells you which functions were evaluated.

Procurement questionnaires should ask for these items specifically. "Is your product FIPS compliant?" invites a yes. "Provide the CMVP certificate number, the module version shipped in this release and the setting that enables approved mode" invites an answer you can check.

> **Compliance hook.** Validated modules satisfy NIST SP 800-53 SC-13 (cryptographic protection) and IA-7 (cryptographic module authentication) where the system's security plan requires FIPS-validated cryptography, the FedRAMP cryptographic module policy, and HIPAA breach guidance that refers to NIST publications. PCI DSS v4.0.1 does not require FIPS 140 validation in general, but PCI PIN Security and PCI P2PE require approved HSMs (FIPS 140 Level 3 or higher, or PCI PTS HSM). ISO/IEC 27001 control 8.24 expects cryptographic standards to be selected deliberately, and many organisations meet it by referencing NIST or ISO algorithm lists. Chapter 41 maps these in detail.

## 40.10 Lab: build and test a FIPS provider

In this lab you build the OpenSSL FIPS provider from source in a container, activate it, and see which operations it refuses. Then you find and read a real security policy.

1. Start a disposable Ubuntu container and install build tools:

```bash
docker run -it --rm --name fipslab ubuntu:24.04 bash
apt-get update && apt-get install -y build-essential perl wget
```

2. Download the OpenSSL source. The OpenSSL project states which version of its FIPS provider is validated; check its website and use that version if it differs from 3.1.2:

```bash
cd /tmp
wget https://github.com/openssl/openssl/releases/download/\
openssl-3.1.2/openssl-3.1.2.tar.gz
tar xzf openssl-3.1.2.tar.gz && cd openssl-3.1.2
./Configure enable-fips --prefix=/opt/fips --openssldir=/opt/fips/ssl
make -j"$(nproc)" && make install && make install_fips
```

3. Run the self-test and integrity check that produces the provider's configuration file:

```bash
export LD_LIBRARY_PATH=/opt/fips/lib64
O=/opt/fips/bin/openssl
$O fipsinstall -module /opt/fips/lib64/ossl-modules/fips.so \
  -out /opt/fips/ssl/fipsmodule.cnf
```

4. Create a configuration that loads the FIPS provider and makes it the default:

```bash
cat > /opt/fips/ssl/fips.cnf <<'EOF'
config_diagnostics = 1
openssl_conf = openssl_init
.include /opt/fips/ssl/fipsmodule.cnf
[openssl_init]
providers = provider_sect
alg_section = algorithm_sect
[provider_sect]
fips = fips_sect
base = base_sect
[base_sect]
activate = 1
[algorithm_sect]
default_properties = fips=yes
EOF
export OPENSSL_CONF=/opt/fips/ssl/fips.cnf
$O list -providers
```

5. Test approved and non-approved operations:

```bash
echo hello | $O dgst -sha256          # works
echo hello | $O dgst -md5             # fails: not approved
$O enc -chacha20 -k test -pbkdf2 -in /etc/hostname   # fails
$O genpkey -algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
  -out ec.pem && echo "EC key generated"
$O list -cipher-algorithms -provider fips | head
```

6. Corrupt the module and rerun `fipsinstall` to see the integrity self-test fail (append one byte to a copy of `fips.so` and point `-module` at the copy).

## 40.11 Failure story: Dual_EC_DRBG and the limits of standards

In 2006 NIST published SP 800-90, a recommendation for deterministic random bit generators. It included four designs, one of which, **Dual_EC_DRBG**, was based on elliptic curve points. The same design also appeared in ANSI X9.82 and ISO/IEC 18031. Dual_EC was slow and its output had measurable bias, and in 2007 two Microsoft researchers, Dan Shumow and Niels Ferguson, showed in a short conference talk that whoever chose its two fixed curve points could, if they knew a secret relationship between them, predict the generator's future output from a small sample. Nobody outside the designers could prove that such a relationship did or did not exist.

The design nevertheless went through the whole standards and validation machinery. Implementations received CAVP algorithm validations and appeared in FIPS-validated modules, and at least one widely used commercial toolkit, RSA's BSAFE, made Dual_EC its default generator. In September 2013, press reports based on leaked documents suggested the US National Security Agency had influenced the standard. NIST reopened SP 800-90A for public comment and advised against using Dual_EC, RSA told its customers to change the default, and in 2014 NIST removed the generator; SP 800-90A Revision 1, published in June 2015, no longer includes it.

Researchers then measured the damage. A 2014 study by Checkoway and colleagues showed that, given the secret, TLS sessions from several Dual_EC implementations could be decrypted in practice. In December 2015 Juniper Networks disclosed unauthorised code in its ScreenOS firewall software; later analysis found that the firewall used Dual_EC and that someone had changed one of its constants, which would let whoever chose the new value decrypt VPN traffic. Validation had confirmed that the products implemented the standard correctly. The standard itself was the problem.

NIST responded by reviewing its own process. An external committee examined its cryptographic standards work in 2014, and NIST published a description of its revised development process, with commitments on openness, the documenting of design rationale and the handling of contributions from other agencies.

The lessons for an enterprise:

- Standards and validation are strong evidence, not proof. Prefer designs with published rationale and broad independent analysis, which is why open competitions such as AES and the post-quantum process carry so much weight.
- Defaults matter. Most Dual_EC exposure came from products that used it without customers choosing it. Your inventory should record which algorithms products actually use, not only which they support.
- Crypto agility matters. Organisations that could change a random generator through configuration recovered quickly; those with it buried in firmware waited for vendors.

## 40.12 Chapter summary

- Standards define what algorithms and protocols are; validation checks that a specific product implements them. Enterprises need both and must not confuse them.
- NIST (FIPS and SP 800 series), ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27 and ISO TC 68, the IETF, ETSI and ANSI X9 produce most cryptographic standards, and they reference each other heavily.
- Standards move from research to specification, adoption, validation, regulation and retirement over years, so reading drafts gives ample warning of change.
- FIPS 140-3 adopts ISO/IEC 19790, defines four security levels and twelve requirement areas, and is administered by the CMVP with laboratory testing and CAVP algorithm validation underneath.
- A certificate covers exact versions, tested configurations and approved mode; the security policy, especially its guidance for crypto officers, says how to run the module as validated.
- FIPS 140-2 certificates moved to the Historical list in September 2026: acceptable for existing systems with a risk decision, not for new procurements that require validation.
- Common Criteria evaluates a product's security claims against a Security Target, often conforming to a Protection Profile; it complements FIPS 140-3 rather than replacing it.
- "FIPS compliant" describes algorithms; "FIPS validated" describes a certified implementation. Ask for certificate numbers, versions and approved-mode settings.
- Dual_EC_DRBG showed that a standardised, validated design can still be backdoored, which is why open process, published rationale and crypto agility matter.
