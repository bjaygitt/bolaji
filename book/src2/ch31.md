## 31.1 Why privacy-enhancing technologies matter

Every earlier chapter in this book protected data by stopping people from seeing it. That works until two parties need to *use* data together. Meridian's fraud team would like to know whether a mule account at Meridian also appears at other banks. A marketing partner wants to measure how many of its ad viewers became Meridian customers. A regulator wants proof that a customer is over 18 without receiving a passport scan. In each case, the conventional answer is that one party hands the other its data and trusts it to behave.

**Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs)** are techniques that reduce how much data must be revealed, or to whom, to get a useful result. Chapter 30 covered the hardware approach, confidential computing, which trusts a processor vendor's enclave. This chapter covers the cryptographic and statistical approaches, which rely on mathematics instead. They answer two different questions:

| Question | Technique |
| --- | --- |
| Can we compute without anyone seeing the inputs? | **Input privacy**: multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption, confidential computing |
| Can we prove a fact without revealing the data behind it? | **Zero-knowledge proofs** |
| Can we publish results without revealing any individual? | **Output privacy**: differential privacy |
| Can partners combine data under agreed rules? | **Data clean rooms**, which combine the above with access controls |

Input and output privacy are independent: a perfectly secure computation of "the average salary of the one employee in the Lisbon office" still reveals the person.

## 31.2 Secure multi-party computation

**Secure multi-party computation (MPC)** lets several parties jointly compute a function of their private inputs while learning nothing except the output. The simplest building block is **additive secret sharing**.

Suppose three banks want the total of their fraud losses without revealing their own. Bank A's loss is 70. It picks two random numbers, say 513 and 288, and sets a third share so that all three add up to 70 modulo some large number. It sends one share to each bank and keeps one. Each share on its own is a uniformly random number and reveals nothing. Banks B and C do the same. Each bank then adds the three shares it holds and publishes only that sum. The three published sums add up to the total of all losses, and no bank ever saw another's figure.

Addition is easy; multiplication on shares needs interaction. General protocols build any function from additions and multiplications, or from logic gates using **garbled circuits**, in which one party encrypts a Boolean circuit and the other evaluates it without learning intermediate values. The main cost is network traffic and rounds, not CPU.

Two security models matter when you read a product claim:

- **Semi-honest** (honest but curious): parties follow the protocol but try to learn from what they see. Cheaper, and adequate when parties are contractually bound and audited.
- **Malicious**: parties may deviate arbitrarily. More expensive, and necessary when a party could profit from cheating.

Protocols also assume enough **non-colluding** parties. The assumption must be real: separate organisations, not three servers run by one administrator.

### Private set intersection

**Private set intersection (PSI)** is the most deployed special case. Two parties each hold a list (customer identifiers, hashed email addresses) and learn only the items they share, or only how many. Typical protocols blind each item with a secret exponent, exchange the blinded values, apply the other party's exponent, and compare. Because the operations commute, matching items produce equal values while non-matching items look random.

### Where MPC is deployed

- The 2008 Danish sugar beet auction, often cited as the first large-scale commercial MPC, cleared contracts between farmers and the processor without revealing individual bids.
- The Boston Women's Workforce Council has used MPC since 2015 to compute gender pay gaps across employers who would not share payroll data.
- Google's Password Checkup lets a browser check whether a username and password appear in a breach corpus without revealing them, using a private set membership protocol.

> **At Meridian.** The fraud team takes part in a consortium of banks that matches suspected mule accounts using PSI over hashed and salted account identifiers. Each bank learns only which of its own flagged accounts also appear elsewhere. The PKI and Cryptography Services team reviewed the protocol for its security model (malicious, with an independent operator) and holds the salt key in its HSMs. GRC recorded the legal basis for the processing, because PSI reduces disclosure but still processes personal data.

## 31.3 Threshold signing for custody

The most commercially important use of MPC today is **threshold cryptography**: splitting a private key into **shares** held by different parties or machines, so that a signature or decryption requires a threshold of them (for example, 3 of 5) to cooperate, and the full key never exists in any one place, not even during signing.

Three designs are easily confused:

| Design | Where the key exists | What the verifier sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **HSM quorum (M-of-N)** | Whole, inside one HSM; people authorise its use (Chapter 33) | An ordinary signature |
| **On-chain multisignature** | Several whole keys, one per signer | Several signatures and a visible policy |
| **Threshold signature** | Never whole; only shares exist | One ordinary signature |

Threshold signing works like this. In **distributed key generation (DKG)**, the parties jointly create a key pair so that each holds a share and the public key is known, but nobody ever computes the private key. To sign, a threshold of parties run an interactive protocol on the message using their shares, producing one standard signature that any verifier checks with the ordinary public key. Shares can be **refreshed** periodically: the parties replace their shares with new ones for the same key, so an attacker must steal a threshold of shares within one refresh period.

For Schnorr and Ed25519 signatures, **FROST** is a two-round threshold protocol standardised as RFC 9591 in 2024. ECDSA is harder to threshold because of its nonce inversion, and the deployed protocols (the GG18 and GG20 family by Gennaro and Goldfeder, CGGMP and Lindell's two-party protocols) use extra machinery such as Paillier encryption and zero-knowledge proofs. That complexity has produced real vulnerabilities, as section 31.10 shows.

Threshold signing is now the default architecture for **digital asset custody**. Products from Fireblocks, Coinbase (which acquired Unbound Security), BitGo, Copper and others split keys between the customer's devices, the provider's servers and sometimes a backup party, often with each share held inside an HSM or a trusted execution environment. Some cloud KMS and HSM vendors also offer threshold or MPC-based key protection. NIST is running a project on multi-party threshold cryptography to develop guidance and possibly standards, including post-quantum threshold schemes, which remain an active research area.

The strongest designs combine approaches: shares in separate HSMs or enclaves, at separate sites, behind a policy engine requiring human approval.

## 31.4 Homomorphic encryption

**Homomorphic encryption** lets someone compute on encrypted data without decrypting it. A client encrypts data, a server computes on the ciphertext, and only the client can decrypt the result.

- **Partially homomorphic** schemes support one operation. Paillier encryption supports addition, which is enough for encrypted tallies and aggregate statistics.
- **Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE)**, first shown possible by Craig Gentry in 2009, supports arbitrary computation. Modern schemes are lattice-based, the same family as ML-KEM (Chapter 45), and are believed quantum-resistant: **BGV** and **BFV** for exact integer arithmetic, **CKKS** for approximate arithmetic on real numbers (useful for machine learning), and **TFHE** for fast operations on bits.

Every FHE ciphertext carries **noise** that grows with each operation, multiplications especially. When the noise gets too large, decryption fails. **Bootstrapping** refreshes a ciphertext by homomorphically evaluating the decryption function, which is expensive. Designers therefore choose parameters for a fixed depth of computation where they can.

FHE remains costly: typically thousands to millions of times slower than computing on plaintext, depending on the task, with ciphertexts much larger than plaintexts. Performance has improved by orders of magnitude, and hardware accelerators are being built. Open-source libraries include OpenFHE, Microsoft SEAL, Zama's TFHE-rs and Apple's swift-homomorphic-encryption.

The clearest large-scale deployment is Apple's. Since 2024, Apple has used BFV-based homomorphic encryption for **private information retrieval (PIR)** in Live Caller ID Lookup, where a server answers a caller-ID query without learning which number was asked, and for private nearest-neighbour search in Photos' Enhanced Visual Search. These are narrow, carefully engineered lookups, which is where FHE is practical today: private queries, simple aggregates and inference on small models. Fully encrypted general analytics over a data warehouse is not yet practical.

## 31.5 Zero-knowledge proofs

A **zero-knowledge proof (ZKP)** lets a prover convince a verifier that a statement is true without revealing anything beyond the fact that it is true. The classic illustration is a circular cave with a locked door at the back: you prove you know the password by emerging from whichever side the verifier calls, many times in a row, without ever saying it.

Modern ZKPs prove statements about computations: "I know a secret that hashes to this value", "this transaction balances without revealing amounts", "I hold a government-signed credential stating a birth date before 2008". Two families dominate:

| Family | Characteristics | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **zk-SNARKs** | Very small proofs, fast verification; some need a trusted setup | Many rely on elliptic curve pairings, which are not quantum-safe |
| **zk-STARKs** and hash-based proofs | Larger proofs, no trusted setup | Rely on hash functions; considered quantum-resistant |

A **trusted setup** generates parameters from secret randomness that must be destroyed, since anyone who kept it could forge proofs.

Where ZKPs are deployed:

- **Digital identity.** Wallets can prove attributes such as age or residency without revealing the whole credential. The EU's digital identity wallet framework under eIDAS 2.0 requires selective disclosure, and in 2025 Google open-sourced its zero-knowledge age verification libraries, which work with existing credential formats. Note that many selective-disclosure formats, such as SD-JWT and ISO mobile driving licences, use salted hashes rather than zero knowledge; they hide undisclosed fields but let verifiers link presentations.
- **Blockchain scaling and privacy**, the largest current deployment, where "rollups" post a succinct proof that thousands of transactions were processed correctly.
- **Verifiable computation**, proving that a service ran a computation correctly, including emerging work on machine learning inference.

An enterprise's likeliest near-term role is as a **verifier** of identity credentials.

## 31.6 Differential privacy

The previous techniques protect inputs. **Differential privacy (DP)** protects outputs. Removing names does not anonymise a dataset, because combinations of attributes (postcode, birth date, sex) identify most people. DP gives a mathematical limit on what any published result reveals about any one individual.

A mechanism is **ε-differentially private** if adding or removing one person's record changes the probability of any output by at most a factor of e^ε. The parameter **ε (epsilon)** is the **privacy loss**: smaller is more private and less accurate. Values below about 1 are considered strong; much larger values give weak guarantees.

The basic tool is the **Laplace mechanism**. For a count, one person changes the answer by at most 1 (the **sensitivity**). Adding random noise drawn from a Laplace distribution with scale sensitivity/ε, for example scale 2 when ε is 0.5, gives ε-DP. A true count of 1,240 customers might be published as 1,238 or 1,243; useful for a dashboard, while hiding whether any single customer is included.

Each query spends part of a **privacy budget**. Repeating a question lets an analyst average away the noise, so a DP system must track and cap the total ε spent.

DP comes in two forms. In **central DP**, a trusted curator holds raw data and adds noise to results. In **local DP**, each device adds noise before sending anything, which needs no trusted curator but far more data for the same accuracy.

Deployments include the US Census Bureau's 2020 Census disclosure avoidance system, the first use of DP for a national census; Apple's and Google's local DP for device telemetry; and DP options in clean rooms (section 31.7). NIST SP 800-226, finalised in March 2025, gives guidance for evaluating DP claims. Open-source libraries include OpenDP, Google's differential privacy library and Tumult Analytics.

> **Watch out.** "Differentially private" without an ε value and a description of the budget is a marketing claim, not a guarantee. Ask for ε per query, the total budget per dataset and period, who can reset it, and whether the noise uses a secure random source.

## 31.7 Data clean rooms

A **data clean room** is a controlled environment where two or more parties combine data and run approved analyses, receiving only aggregate results. Clean rooms grew out of advertising measurement as third-party cookies declined, and are now used for retail media, financial crime collaboration and healthcare research.

Most clean rooms rely on **access controls and query restrictions**: approved query templates, minimum aggregation thresholds (no result for groups under, say, 100 people), blocked row-level output and audit logs. The trust anchor is usually the platform operator. Some add PETs on top:

| Platform (as of 2026) | Basis | PET options |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **AWS Clean Rooms** | Collaboration over each party's data in its own account | Cryptographic Computing for Clean Rooms (client-side encryption before upload); differential privacy, generally available since 2024 |
| **Snowflake Data Clean Rooms** | Data sharing within the Snowflake platform with approved templates | Aggregation and projection policies; differential privacy features |
| **Google Cloud BigQuery data clean rooms** and Ads Data Hub | Shared datasets with analysis rules | Aggregation thresholds; differential privacy in analysis rules |
| **Databricks Clean Rooms** | Delta Sharing between workspaces with approved notebooks | Relies mainly on access controls |

Treat a clean room as a governance product with optional cryptography: ask who operates it, who can change approved queries, whether the operator can see raw data, and how thresholds and DP budgets are enforced.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian Pay runs a measurement collaboration with a retail partner in a cloud clean room: the partner learns how many of its campaign's viewers made card purchases, by week and region, never which customers. GRC required a data protection impact assessment, an aggregation threshold of 50 customers, and DP on all outputs, with the ε budget recorded in the collaboration agreement. Customer identifiers are encrypted client-side under keys managed by the PKI and Cryptography Services team before upload.

## 31.8 Choosing a technique and judging readiness

| Technique | Maturity (2026) | Typical production use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PSI and two-party MPC | Mature for narrow tasks | Matching, measurement, breach checks |
| Threshold signing | Mature, widely deployed | Digital asset custody, high-value signing |
| FHE | Early production for narrow lookups | Private retrieval, encrypted inference on small models |
| Zero-knowledge proofs | Mature in blockchains; emerging in identity | Rollups, age and attribute verification |
| Differential privacy | Mature with expertise | Official statistics, telemetry, clean room outputs |
| Clean rooms | Mature as products | Advertising, retail media, consortia |

When one is proposed, ask whether a contract, access control or HSM would solve the problem more simply; whether the scheme is standardised or widely analysed; what it assumes (non-collusion, trusted setup, hardware trust, quantum resistance); what it costs in performance and operations; whether audited implementations exist; and what leaks if an assumption breaks. PETs change who must be trusted, so make sure the new assumptions are better than the old ones, not just more complicated.

> **Compliance hook.** PETs support GDPR Article 25 (data protection by design) and Article 32 (security of processing), and can help data minimisation. They rarely make data anonymous in the legal sense: MPC and FHE process personal data, so a lawful basis and impact assessment are still required. The UK ICO's 2023 guidance on PETs explains how regulators view each technique. NIST SP 800-226 applies to DP claims, and threshold key custody should be mapped to the key management controls of NIST SP 800-57 and ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control A.8.24.

## 31.9 Lab: secret sharing and differential privacy in Python

This lab needs only Python 3, with no extra packages.

1. Save this as `pets.py`. It splits three banks' fraud losses into additive shares, computes the total without any bank revealing its own figure, then publishes a noisy count with the Laplace mechanism:

    ```python
    import secrets, random, math

    P = 2**61 - 1  # a prime modulus

    def share(x, n=3):
        s = [secrets.randbelow(P) for _ in range(n - 1)]
        return s + [(x - sum(s)) % P]

    losses = {"A": 70, "B": 125, "C": 40}
    shares = {b: share(v) for b, v in losses.items()}
    # bank i receives the i-th share from every bank
    partial = [sum(shares[b][i] for b in losses) % P
               for i in range(3)]
    print("shares sent by A:", shares["A"])
    print("published partial sums:", partial)
    print("total:", sum(partial) % P)

    def laplace(scale):
        u = random.random() - 0.5
        return -scale * math.copysign(1, u) * \
            math.log(1 - 2 * abs(u))

    true_count, eps = 1240, 0.5
    for _ in range(5):
        print("noisy count:", round(true_count + laplace(1 / eps)))
    ```

2. Run it with `python3 pets.py`. Confirm the total is 235 and that A's shares look random.
3. Change `eps` to 0.05 and to 5, and observe the trade-off between accuracy and privacy.
4. Modify the script to answer the same count 1,000 times and average the results. Explain why a DP system must cap the total budget.
5. Note the shortcut: `random` is not a cryptographically secure generator. Production DP libraries use secure randomness and handle floating-point weaknesses; explain why that matters.

**Optional extension.** Install OpenFHE or Microsoft SEAL locally and run the library's BFV example that adds and multiplies encrypted integers, timing it against the plaintext operation. In a cloud account, try a clean room service's sample collaboration. *Cost warning:* clean room services charge per query or compute unit; delete the collaboration and data when you finish.

## 31.10 Failure story: BitForge and threshold ECDSA

In August 2023, Fireblocks' cryptography researchers disclosed vulnerabilities they named **BitForge** in widely used threshold ECDSA implementations. The most serious, in protocols of the GG18 and GG20 family, arose because parties did not verify that another party's Paillier modulus was well formed (for example, free of small factors). A malicious participant could supply a crafted modulus and, over a number of signing sessions, extract the other parties' key shares and reconstruct the full private key, defeating the entire point of threshold custody. A second flaw affected implementations of Lindell's two-party protocol that deviated from the paper. The issues were assigned CVE-2023-33241 and CVE-2023-33242. Several wallet providers had patched before public disclosure; Fireblocks reported that others were still exposed at the time, which those providers disputed.

The lesson: a threshold protocol is only as strong as its least-checked proof, and a flaw in published pseudocode propagates to every faithful implementation. Prefer simpler, standardised protocols such as FROST where the signature algorithm allows, demand independent audits of the specific implementation, and treat a threshold scheme as defence in depth alongside HSMs and approval policies, not as a substitute for them.

## 31.11 Chapter summary

- PETs reduce what must be revealed to compute, prove or publish; input privacy and output privacy are separate goals.
- MPC splits data into shares so parties compute jointly without seeing each other's inputs; it relies on a real non-collusion assumption.
- PSI is the most deployed MPC form, used for matching, measurement and breach checks.
- Threshold signing keeps a key as shares that never combine, producing ordinary signatures; it underpins digital asset custody and is standardised for Schnorr signatures as FROST.
- FHE computes on ciphertexts; it is lattice-based and costly, and is in production for narrow private lookups such as Apple's caller ID and photo search.
- Zero-knowledge proofs prove facts without revealing data; identity wallets and blockchains are the main deployments.
- Differential privacy bounds what outputs reveal about any individual, using noise and a tracked privacy budget ε.
- Clean rooms are governance platforms; check what is cryptographically enforced and what depends on the operator.
