## 45.1 How the standards were chosen

In 2016, NIST launched an open competition for post-quantum public-key algorithms, modelled on the AES and SHA-3 competitions. Researchers worldwide submitted 82 proposals. Over three rounds and six years, the community attacked them in public. Several were broken outright: the signature finalist Rainbow fell to a key-recovery attack in 2022, and SIKE, a fourth-round candidate, fell in 2022 to an attack that ran in about an hour on a single laptop core for its smallest parameter set. Those breaks are why open competitions and conservative choices matter.

In August 2024, NIST published three standards:

| Standard | Algorithm (original name) | Type | Mathematical basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **FIPS 203** | **ML-KEM** (CRYSTALS-Kyber) | Key encapsulation | Module lattices (Module-LWE) |
| **FIPS 204** | **ML-DSA** (CRYSTALS-Dilithium) | Signature | Module lattices |
| **FIPS 205** | **SLH-DSA** (SPHINCS+) | Signature | Hash functions only |

More are on the way:

- **FN-DSA** (Falcon), a compact lattice signature, as FIPS 206. NIST submitted the draft for approval in 2025; as of late 2026 the final standard is still awaited, with publication expected around the end of 2026 or in 2027.
- **HQC**, a code-based KEM selected in March 2025 as a backup to ML-KEM with different mathematics. A draft standard comes first, with a final version expected around 2027.
- An **additional signatures** "on-ramp" competition, now in its second round, looking for schemes with different trade-offs, such as smaller signatures or non-lattice assumptions.

The prefixes are worth decoding once. **ML** means module lattice, **SLH** means stateless hash-based, **FN** means FFT over NTRU lattices, **KEM** means key encapsulation mechanism and **DSA** means digital signature algorithm.

## 45.2 Lattices in one page

Chapter 3 introduced **Learning With Errors (LWE)**. Take a secret vector s. Publish many random linear equations in s, each with a small random error added. Without the errors, schoolbook algebra recovers s instantly. With the errors, recovering s means solving a hard problem about points in a high-dimensional grid, a **lattice**, which is believed hard for both classical and quantum computers. No known quantum algorithm gives more than a modest speed-up against it, unlike factoring.

An analogy helps. Imagine being given hundreds of shop receipts that each list quantities of the same few items and a total, but every total has been nudged by a few pence. With exact totals you could solve for the prices. With nudged totals and hundreds of dimensions instead of a few items, the problem becomes infeasible.

**Module-LWE**, used by ML-KEM and ML-DSA, organises the numbers into small polynomials so that keys stay compact and arithmetic is fast. Multiplication uses the **Number Theoretic Transform (NTT)**, a cousin of the fast Fourier transform, which is why lattice schemes are quick in software and simple to accelerate in hardware.

### How ML-KEM works, intuitively

1. **Key generation.** Bob publishes a matrix A and a vector `t = A·s + e`, where s is his secret and e a small error. Recovering s from t is the Module-LWE problem.
2. **Encapsulation.** Alice picks her own small random values, uses Bob's public key to build a ciphertext that hides a random message, and derives a shared key from that message.
3. **Decapsulation.** Bob uses s to cancel most of the noise, recovers the message and derives the same shared key.

Because of the errors, Bob's recovery is approximate, with a tiny chance of failure: about 2^-164 for ML-KEM-768, far below anything that will happen in practice. ML-KEM wraps this in the **Fujisaki-Okamoto transform**: Bob re-encrypts the recovered message and checks that he gets the same ciphertext, and if not, returns a pseudo-random key instead of an error. That check makes ML-KEM **IND-CCA2** secure (Chapter 11), so a key pair can safely be reused, and it gives attackers nothing to learn from deliberately malformed ciphertexts.

### A KEM is not Diffie-Hellman

A **key encapsulation mechanism (KEM)** has three operations: key generation, encapsulate (to a public key) and decapsulate (with the private key). It is not a drop-in replacement for Diffie-Hellman in every respect:

- DH is symmetric: both sides contribute a key share and compute the same secret. A KEM is asymmetric: one side encapsulates to the other's public key.
- Protocols built on DH's special properties, such as Signal's X3DH, which mixes static and ephemeral keys, needed redesign; that is what PQXDH did.
- For ephemeral key exchange, as in TLS 1.3, the KEM interface fits neatly: the client sends a KEM public key in its key share, and the server returns a ciphertext.

## 45.3 ML-KEM (FIPS 203)

| Parameter set | Security category | Public key | Ciphertext | Shared secret |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ML-KEM-512 | 1 (about AES-128) | 800 bytes | 768 bytes | 32 bytes |
| **ML-KEM-768** | 3 (about AES-192) | 1,184 bytes | 1,088 bytes | 32 bytes |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 5 (about AES-256) | 1,568 bytes | 1,568 bytes | 32 bytes |

NIST's **security categories** compare each parameter set with the cost of breaking a symmetric primitive: category 1 is at least as hard as an AES-128 key search, category 3 as AES-192, and category 5 as AES-256.

For comparison, an X25519 public key is 32 bytes. ML-KEM is very fast, often faster than X25519, but the bytes on the wire grow from tens to over a thousand. **ML-KEM-768** is the default choice; it is what the hybrid TLS group X25519MLKEM768 uses. CNSA 2.0 requires ML-KEM-1024 for US national security systems.

## 45.4 ML-DSA (FIPS 204)

| Parameter set | Security category | Public key | Signature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | 1,312 bytes | 2,420 bytes |
| **ML-DSA-65** | 3 | 1,952 bytes | 3,309 bytes |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | 2,592 bytes | 4,627 bytes |

ML-DSA is the general-purpose post-quantum signature. It follows the **Fiat-Shamir with aborts** pattern: the signer produces a candidate signature from a masked version of the secret, and if the candidate would leak information about the secret it throws it away and tries again. Signing and verification are fast, there is no secret state to track, and by default signing is **hedged**, mixing fresh randomness with a deterministic value so that a weak random number generator does not leak the key.

Its cost is size. An ECDSA P-256 signature is 64 bytes, with a 64-byte public key. A TLS server certificate chain with ML-DSA keys and signatures throughout grows by roughly 10 to 15 kilobytes, which matters for handshake latency, for constrained devices, and for any field, buffer or database column sized for classical signatures.

FIPS 204 also defines **HashML-DSA**, a pre-hash variant for signing a digest, and the cloud KMS services expose an **external mu** mode, where the caller computes a 64-byte message representative and sends only that to the KMS. Know which one your verifier expects; they are not interchangeable.

## 45.5 Hash-based signatures: SLH-DSA, LMS and XMSS

### SLH-DSA (FIPS 205)

SLH-DSA is built **only from hash functions**, so its security rests on the most conservative assumption in cryptography: that SHA-2 or SHAKE behave as expected. It builds a huge tree of one-time and few-time signature keys and picks a leaf pseudo-randomly for each message, so the signer never has to remember which keys it has used. The price is large and slow signatures:

| Parameter set | Public key | Signature | Signing speed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SLH-DSA-128s ("small") | 32 bytes | 7,856 bytes | Slow |
| SLH-DSA-128f ("fast") | 32 bytes | 17,088 bytes | Faster, larger |
| SLH-DSA-256s | 64 bytes | 29,792 bytes | Slow |

SLH-DSA suits uses where signatures are created rarely, must be trusted for a very long time, and where a second mathematical basis is valuable: root CA certificates, firmware roots of trust and long-term archives.

### LMS and XMSS: stateful hash-based signatures

**LMS** (RFC 8554) and **XMSS** (RFC 8391), approved by NIST in SP 800-208 in 2020, predate the FIPS 203 to 205 standards. They are compact and fast to verify, but **stateful**: each one-time key inside them may be used only once, and the signer must reliably record which have been used. Reusing state, for example by restoring an HSM backup or cloning a virtual machine, can let an attacker forge signatures.

That makes them unsuitable for general use but well suited to **firmware and software signing**, where signatures are created rarely in a controlled, HSM-based environment. CNSA 2.0 specifies LMS or XMSS for firmware signing, and SP 800-208 requires the state to be managed inside a hardware module.

## 45.6 FN-DSA and HQC

**FN-DSA** (Falcon) offers the smallest lattice signatures: about 666 bytes with an 897-byte public key at the lower security level. That is attractive where size dominates, such as certificate chains and constrained protocols. Its signing uses floating-point Gaussian sampling, which is notoriously hard to implement in constant time, so expect it mainly in verification-heavy uses, with signing done in well-reviewed libraries or HSMs. Until FIPS 206 is final, treat FN-DSA as something to test, not to deploy for compliance.

**HQC** is a KEM based on error-correcting codes rather than lattices. Its keys and ciphertexts are larger and it is slower than ML-KEM, but it provides a fallback with independent mathematics in case a breakthrough against lattices emerges. Most enterprises will not deploy HQC soon; what matters is that your systems can add a second KEM without redesign.

## 45.7 Choosing an algorithm

| Use case | Recommended choice |
| --- | --- |
| Key exchange in TLS, SSH, VPNs | Hybrid X25519 (or P-256) with ML-KEM-768; ML-KEM-1024 for CNSA 2.0 systems |
| Encrypting data or wrapping keys to a public key (HPKE, KMS import) | ML-KEM-768 or ML-KEM-1024, in hybrid during the transition |
| General signatures: TLS certificates, tokens, documents | ML-DSA-65 (ML-DSA-87 for CNSA 2.0); FN-DSA where size is critical, once standardised |
| Root CAs and long-lived trust anchors | ML-DSA-87, or SLH-DSA for maximum conservatism |
| Firmware and boot signing | LMS or XMSS with state held in an HSM; SLH-DSA or ML-DSA as alternatives |
| Symmetric encryption and hashing | AES-256, SHA-384 or SHA-3; no change of algorithm needed |

> **At Meridian.** The PKI and Cryptography Services team publishes an approved-algorithm list (Chapter 42). Its 2026 revision added ML-KEM-768 in hybrid with X25519 as the preferred key exchange for all new TLS and SSH, ML-DSA-65 as approved for new internal signing systems, ML-DSA-87 or SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s for any new root CA, and LMS for the firmware signing service that Payments Security runs for Meridian Pay's terminal estate. It also set a rule that matters more than any algorithm choice: every new system must state, in its design review, how it would change algorithms without a code release. The list names algorithms, not products, so the same rule applies to the cloud KMS, the HSM clusters and the CLM platform.

## 45.8 Hybrid and composite designs

During the transition, most deployments combine a classical algorithm with a post-quantum one, so that security holds if **either** survives. This protects against a quantum computer (if the classical part breaks) and against an undiscovered flaw in the new algorithms or their implementations (if the post-quantum part breaks).

- **Hybrid key exchange** feeds both shared secrets into the key derivation function. X25519MLKEM768 in TLS 1.3, mlkem768x25519-sha256 in SSH (the default since OpenSSH 10.0), and PQXDH in Signal all work this way. IKEv2 achieves the same with additional key exchanges defined in RFC 9370.
- **Composite signatures** pair a classical and a post-quantum signature in a single certificate or signature object, valid only if both verify. The IETF LAMPS working group has specified composite ML-DSA formats for X.509, alongside "pure" ML-DSA certificates.
- **Dual certificates or parallel chains** are an alternative: issue a classical and a post-quantum certificate for the same service, and let each relying party use what it supports.

Agencies differ on signatures. The NSA prefers moving national security systems directly to pure post-quantum algorithms, while Germany's BSI and France's ANSSI recommend hybrids during the transition. For key exchange, hybrid is the near-universal choice on the public internet today. Follow your sector's guidance and record the decision.

## 45.9 Implementation risks

New algorithms bring new implementation pitfalls:

- **Side channels.** In early 2024, researchers found **KyberSlash**: several Kyber implementations used a division whose timing depended on secret data, allowing key recovery. Mature libraries fixed it quickly; immature ones may not have.
- **Validation and failure handling.** ML-KEM implementations must perform the re-encryption check exactly as specified and check input lengths. An implementation that returns an error instead of the pseudo-random key leaks information.
- **Randomness.** Key generation still needs a strong random source (Chapter 4), especially in virtual machines and containers.
- **State for LMS and XMSS.** Lose or duplicate state and security is gone. HSM backup, clustering and disaster recovery procedures must be designed around it.
- **Size assumptions.** Protocol fields, database columns, HSM buffers, certificate size limits, HTTP header limits and QR codes sized for 64-byte signatures will break.
- **Encoding churn.** Early drafts used different private key formats and object identifiers. Keys generated with pre-standard code may not import into final-standard libraries.

Use well-maintained libraries that implement the final FIPS versions, and prefer FIPS 140-3 validated modules where compliance requires it (Chapter 40).

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requires "strong cryptography" (defined by reference to industry-accepted standards), and requirement 12.3.3 asks for an annual review of cipher suites and protocols. NIST SP 800-53 control SC-13 requires cryptography to follow applicable standards. Recording which FIPS 203, 204 or 205 parameter sets you approve, and in which validated modules, is the evidence auditors will ask for as these algorithms enter production.

## 45.10 Lab: post-quantum cryptography with OpenSSL

You need OpenSSL 3.5 or later (`openssl version`). Work in an empty directory.

**1. List the algorithms.**

```bash
openssl list -kem-algorithms | grep -i ml-kem
openssl list -signature-algorithms | grep -iE "ml-dsa|slh-dsa"
```

**2. Generate keys and compare public key sizes.**

```bash
openssl genpkey -algorithm ML-DSA-65 -out mldsa.key
openssl genpkey -algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
  -out ec.key
openssl pkey -in mldsa.key -pubout -outform DER | wc -c
openssl pkey -in ec.key -pubout -outform DER | wc -c
```

**3. Sign and verify with ML-DSA.**

```bash
echo "post-quantum hello" > msg.txt
openssl pkeyutl -sign -inkey mldsa.key -in msg.txt -rawin \
  -out msg.sig
wc -c msg.sig                       # 3309 bytes for ML-DSA-65
openssl pkeyutl -verify -inkey mldsa.key -in msg.txt -rawin \
  -sigfile msg.sig
```

**4. Encapsulate and decapsulate with ML-KEM.**

```bash
openssl genpkey -algorithm ML-KEM-768 -out kem.key
openssl pkey -in kem.key -pubout -out kem.pub
openssl pkeyutl -encap -inkey kem.pub -pubin -out ct.bin \
  -secret ss_sender.bin
openssl pkeyutl -decap -inkey kem.key -in ct.bin \
  -secret ss_receiver.bin
cmp ss_sender.bin ss_receiver.bin && echo "shared secrets match"
wc -c ct.bin                        # 1088 bytes
```

**5. Make and compare certificates.**

```bash
openssl req -x509 -newkey ML-DSA-65 -nodes -keyout pq.key \
  -out pq.crt -subj "/CN=pq.lab.test" -days 30
openssl req -x509 -newkey EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
  -nodes -keyout ecc.key -out ecc.crt -subj "/CN=ecc.lab.test" \
  -days 30
openssl x509 -in pq.crt -outform DER | wc -c
openssl x509 -in ecc.crt -outform DER | wc -c
```

**6. Run a hybrid TLS handshake locally.** In one terminal start a server; in another, connect and read the negotiated group.

```bash
openssl s_server -accept 8443 -cert pq.crt -key pq.key \
  -groups X25519MLKEM768 -www
openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 -groups X25519MLKEM768 \
  -brief < /dev/null
```

Record the sizes from steps 2, 3, 4 and 5 in a small table. You will reuse them in Chapter 46 when estimating handshake growth.

*Optional cloud extension.* Create an ML-DSA signing key in a cloud KMS that supports it, sign the same message through the CLI, and verify the signature locally with OpenSSL using the exported public key. **Cost warning:** cloud KMS keys are billed per key per month and per operation; schedule the key for deletion when you finish.

## 45.11 Failure story: SIKE, broken in an hour

**SIKE** (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation) was one of the most elegant candidates in NIST's competition. It had the smallest keys of any post-quantum KEM, it had survived years of analysis, and in July 2022 it advanced to the fourth round as a candidate for possible standardisation. Large technology companies had experimented with it in TLS.

Weeks later, in July 2022, Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru published an attack that recovered SIKE's secret key in about an hour on a single classical computer core for the lowest security level. The attack built on a mathematical result about products of elliptic curves published in 1997, a result that had been sitting in the literature for 25 years. The scheme was withdrawn.

Nobody was harmed, because SIKE had only been used experimentally and always in hybrid with classical key exchange. The episode is the strongest argument for three practices in this chapter: prefer algorithms with long and broad analysis (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA), use hybrid designs during the transition, and keep systems agile enough to swap algorithms when the unexpected happens.

## 45.12 Chapter summary

- NIST standardised ML-KEM (FIPS 203), ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) in August 2024; FN-DSA (FIPS 206) and HQC are still on the way.
- ML-KEM and ML-DSA rest on Module-LWE lattices: fast, but with keys, ciphertexts and signatures tens of times larger than today's.
- ML-KEM-768 has a decapsulation failure rate of about 2^-164 and is IND-CCA2 secure through the Fujisaki-Okamoto transform.
- SLH-DSA relies only on hashes: very conservative, with large signatures. LMS and XMSS are compact but stateful, suited to HSM-based firmware signing.
- Use ML-KEM-768 in hybrid for key exchange, ML-DSA-65 for general signatures, and ML-DSA-87 or SLH-DSA for long-lived roots.
- Hybrid and composite designs hedge against both quantum attacks and flaws in the new algorithms.
- Implementation risks include side channels, state handling, encoding churn and size assumptions.
- SIKE, broken in about an hour in 2022, shows why conservative choices and agility matter.
