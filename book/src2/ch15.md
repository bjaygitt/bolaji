## 15.1 Why an enterprise runs its own PKI

Use **public** certificates when clients you do not control must trust you: customer browsers, partner systems, public APIs. Use **private** certificates when you control the clients, or can configure them to trust your own root:

| Need | Why a private CA fits |
| --- | --- |
| Service-to-service mTLS | Thousands of short-lived certificates; internal names; client authentication |
| Internal web applications | Hostnames that must not appear in public CT logs (Chapter 13) |
| Windows logon, smart cards, VPN and Wi-Fi | User and device certificates carrying directory identities |
| Kubernetes and service meshes | Automated issuance per workload, lifetimes in hours |
| IoT, ATMs and payment terminals | Device identities with lifetimes and rules public CAs do not offer |
| Internal code and document signing | Control over who can sign what |

A **private PKI** is the set of CAs, policies, keys, people and processes that issue and manage these certificates. It gives you control over lifetimes, names, extensions and issuance speed. It also gives you all the responsibility. If a private CA is compromised, an attacker can impersonate any internal service or user that trusts it; if its CRL or its own certificate expires unnoticed, everything that depends on it fails at once. In many enterprises the Windows CA is also, without anyone having decided it, one of the easiest routes to domain administrator.

This chapter is about the CAs themselves: their design, policies, keys and platforms. How certificates are discovered, requested, delivered and renewed at scale is the subject of Part IV (Chapters 17 to 19).

## 15.2 Designing the hierarchy

Most organisations need a **two-tier** hierarchy; large or regulated ones sometimes add a middle tier.

| Tier | Role | Key storage | Online? | Typical lifetime |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Root CA** | Signs subordinate CAs and its own CRL only | HSM, offline, in a safe | Powered on only for ceremonies, perhaps twice a year | 15 to 25 years |
| **Policy CA** (optional) | Separates hierarchies by policy, business unit or region | HSM, offline | Rarely | 10 to 15 years |
| **Issuing CA** | Issues leaf certificates every day | Network HSM or managed key service | Yes | 3 to 7 years |
| **Leaf certificates** | Used by servers, workloads, devices, users | On the endpoint, ideally in hardware | n/a | Hours to about a year |

Design rules that save pain later:

- **Separate issuing CAs by purpose.** For example: one for TLS servers, one for workload mTLS, one for users and devices, one for signing. A problem in one, such as a compromise, a bad profile or a forced re-key, does not affect the others, and each can have its own lifetime, constraints and audit evidence.
- **Constrain subordinate CAs.** Use `pathlen:0` so issuing CAs cannot create further CAs, **name constraints** so they can only issue for your domains, and EKU constraints so a device CA cannot issue code signing certificates. Clients enforce these (Chapter 14), so they limit damage even if an issuing CA key is stolen.
- **Mind the lifetime ladder.** A CA should not issue certificates valid beyond its own expiry, and many products refuse to. Renew issuing CAs well before their remaining life drops below the longest leaf lifetime you issue, which in practice means renewing at about half their life.
- **Plan trust distribution first.** A root is only useful once it is in every trust store that needs it: Windows and macOS stores through device management, Linux and container images, Java `cacerts`, mobile apps, appliances and partner systems. The number of trust stores, not the CA software, usually sets the cost of adding or replacing a root.
- **Choose algorithms deliberately.** ECDSA P-384 for CAs and P-256 or RSA-2048 for leaves is a common modern choice; RSA remains necessary where old clients or smart cards require it. A root generated today may still be in service when post-quantum signatures are required, so plan a parallel post-quantum hierarchy rather than assuming this root will last its full term (Chapter 46).

## 15.3 Policies: CP, CPS and policy OIDs

A trustworthy CA writes down its rules in two documents:

- A **Certificate Policy (CP)** says what the certificates may be used for, what assurance they carry and what obligations each party has. It answers "what do these certificates promise?"
- A **Certification Practice Statement (CPS)** says how the CA actually meets the policy: identity checks, key protection, personnel controls, logging, revocation and disaster recovery. It answers "how do we keep that promise?"

RFC 3647 gives a standard outline for both, in nine sections: introduction; publication and repository; identification and authentication; certificate life-cycle operational requirements; facility, management and operational controls; technical security controls; certificate, CRL and OCSP profiles; compliance audit; and other business and legal matters. Public CAs must publish documents in this structure. For a private PKI, a shorter document that follows the same headings is enough, and it pays off three times: auditors can map it to controls, incident responders can see what should have happened, and a new team can run the CA after the original builders leave.

### Policy OIDs

A **policy object identifier (OID)** is a globally unique number, such as `1.3.6.1.4.1.99999.10.1.2`, that names a policy. An organisation obtains its own branch of the OID tree (commonly a free **Private Enterprise Number** from IANA under `1.3.6.1.4.1`) and allocates numbers beneath it. The CA writes the OID into each certificate's **Certificate Policies** extension, and relying systems can then make decisions based on it.

That turns a policy document into something machines can enforce. Suppose Meridian defines two assurance levels for user certificates: "medium", issued after a normal login, and "high", issued only to a smart card after in-person identity checks. Each gets its own OID. A payments approval application can then accept only certificates carrying the "high" OID, even though both chain to the same root. Microsoft AD CS uses the same mechanism for **issuance policies**, and can link an issuance policy OID to an Active Directory group so that holding such a certificate grants group membership at logon (a feature that is also attack class ESC13, section 15.5).

> **In practice.** Allocate the OID arc on day one, even for a small PKI, and record every allocation in a register owned by the PKI team. Retrofitting policy OIDs later means re-issuing every certificate.

## 15.4 Protecting CA keys and key ceremonies

The CA's private keys are the most sensitive part of the system. Standard controls:

- **Hardware.** Generate and keep CA keys inside a **hardware security module (HSM)** validated to FIPS 140-3 Level 3 or equivalent (Chapter 33), where they cannot be exported in the clear. Network HSMs from vendors such as Thales, Entrust and Utimaco, cloud HSM services, and the HSM-backed key services inside cloud private CAs all meet this need. A key file on disk, even encrypted, does not.
- **Offline root.** Keep the root's HSM disconnected from any network and physically secured. Bring it out only during scheduled, documented ceremonies.
- **Multi-person control.** Require several people to activate the root, using **M-of-N** quorums of smart cards or key shares (for example, any 3 of 5 custodians), so no single person can use or copy the key.
- **Backups.** Back up CA keys in encrypted, split form to a second secure location. A root key lost to hardware failure with no backup means rebuilding trust everywhere.
- **Separation of duties.** The people who administer the CA software should not be the people who approve certificate templates or hold HSM custodian cards.

### The key ceremony

A **key ceremony** is the formal, scripted event in which a CA key is generated or used offline. A good ceremony has:

1. A written **script** listing every command and check, approved in advance and rehearsed on test hardware.
2. Named roles: ceremony administrator, key custodians, an independent witness, and often an auditor.
3. A clean, verified computer that has never been and will never be networked, booted from known media.
4. **Video recording** and a signed log of every step, including hardware serial numbers and tamper-evident bag numbers.
5. Secure storage of all materials at the end, with sign-off by every participant.

It sounds theatrical. Its purpose is evidence: years later, you can prove the root key was created correctly, never left the HSM, and was only used for authorised purposes. Root ceremonies recur: signing a new issuing CA, renewing the root's CRL, and eventually retiring the root. Payment key ceremonies follow similar principles with stricter rules (Chapter 38).

> **Watch out.** An offline root still has an online duty: its CRL must be re-signed before Next Update. Schedule CRL ceremonies in the team calendar with the custodians' names, and alert months in advance (Chapter 14).

## 15.5 Microsoft AD CS in depth

**Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS)** is the CA role built into Windows Server. It is the most widely deployed private CA in enterprises, because it is included with Windows and integrates with Active Directory for identity, enrolment and Group Policy.

### How it works

AD CS can run as a **standalone CA**, which does not use Active Directory and suits an offline root, or as an **enterprise CA**, which is integrated with AD and suits issuing CAs. An enterprise CA:

- Reads **certificate templates** stored in AD. A template defines a certificate type: its EKUs, key type and size, validity, how the subject name is built, whether manager approval is needed, and, through its access control list, who may **enroll** and **autoenroll**. Templates have schema versions (1 to 4); version 1 templates are the old built-in ones and cannot be fully edited.
- Publishes its CA certificate into AD containers so that domain members trust it automatically. One of these, the **NTAuth store**, lists CAs trusted to issue certificates for domain authentication (smart card and certificate logon via Kerberos PKINIT, Chapter 16). Membership of NTAuth is therefore a statement that this CA can mint domain identities.
- Supports **autoenrolment** through Group Policy, so domain-joined machines and users obtain and renew certificates silently.

Other enrolment paths include the legacy **web enrolment** pages, the **Certificate Enrollment Policy and Enrollment Web Services (CEP/CES)** for non-domain clients, the **Network Device Enrollment Service (NDES)** for SCEP, and connectors that let Microsoft Intune and other device managers request certificates for managed devices.

### The ESC attack classes

In 2021, researchers showed ("Certified Pre-Owned") that AD CS misconfigurations give ordinary domain users paths to domain administrator, and catalogued them as ESC1 to ESC8. Later work has extended the list to ESC16, and open-source tools such as Certipy and Certify find and exploit them in minutes. They fall into a few conceptual groups:

| Group | ESC classes | The core mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Template lets the requester choose the identity | ESC1, ESC2, ESC3, ESC15 | A template with client-authentication (or any-purpose) EKU lets low-privileged users supply the subject name, or act as an enrolment agent, so they request a certificate "for" an administrator. ESC15 abuses version 1 templates that accept requester-supplied application policies |
| Weak access control on PKI objects | ESC4, ESC5, ESC7 | Users can edit a template, the CA's AD objects or the CA itself, and so create one of the other conditions |
| Dangerous CA-wide settings | ESC6, ESC16 | The CA accepts requester-supplied subject alternative names for every template, or omits the security extension that binds a certificate to an account's security identifier |
| Relay to enrolment endpoints | ESC8, ESC11 | NTLM authentication is relayed to web enrolment over HTTP, or to the RPC interface without required encryption, to obtain a certificate as the victim machine, often a domain controller |
| Weak certificate-to-account mapping | ESC9, ESC10, ESC14 | Domain controllers map certificates to accounts by name or editable attributes, so changing an account's name or mapping attribute lets one account's certificate log on as another |
| Other privilege links | ESC12, ESC13 | Access to the CA host's key storage, or issuance policy OIDs linked to privileged groups |

The common thread is that an enterprise CA in NTAuth is part of the identity system. Anyone who can make it issue a client-authentication certificate naming another user can become that user, and certificates outlive password resets: a stolen certificate keeps working until it expires or is revoked.

### Hardening AD CS

- **Treat CAs as Tier 0.** Enterprise CA servers, their HSMs and their administrators belong at the same tier as domain controllers, with the same administrative isolation.
- **Review every published template.** Unpublish templates you do not use. For the rest, remove "supply in the request" subject names from any template with an authentication EKU, or require manager approval; never use Any Purpose or empty EKUs; and restrict enrolment rights to specific groups rather than Domain Users or Authenticated Users. Duplicate and replace version 1 templates.
- **Lock down CA settings.** Make sure `EDITF_ATTRIBUTESUBJECTALTNAME2` is off, the security extension is not disabled, and RPC enrolment requires encryption.
- **Remove or protect web enrolment.** Disable HTTP enrolment endpoints you do not need; where needed, require HTTPS with Extended Protection for Authentication and disable NTLM.
- **Enforce strong certificate mapping.** Microsoft's KB5014754 changes moved domain controllers to full enforcement of strong mapping in 2025, which defeats several mapping attacks. Confirm no compatibility overrides remain.
- **Prune NTAuth.** Only CAs that genuinely issue logon certificates should be in it.
- **Monitor issuance.** Forward CA audit events (for example, Windows event IDs 4886 and 4887 for requests and issuance) to the SIEM, and alert on certificates whose subject differs from the requester or that are issued from sensitive templates.
- **Audit regularly.** Defensive tools such as Locksmith and PSPKIAudit, as well as Certipy in its find mode, enumerate risky configurations; identity threat detection products from several vendors also watch AD CS.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian's AD CS estate is two enterprise issuing CAs under an offline root, used for laptop and user certificates, Wi-Fi and VPN, and smart card logon for privileged administrators. The IAM team owns the templates and Active Directory; the PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the CAs and HSMs. After a 2024 penetration test found an ESC1 template created years earlier for a printing project, the two teams set a rule: no template is published without a joint review, and a quarterly scan reports every template with an authentication EKU and requester-supplied subject to the SOC.

## 15.6 EJBCA, Vault PKI and step-ca

Microsoft-centric estates rarely run AD CS alone. Workloads, Linux, Kubernetes, network devices and IoT are often better served by other CA platforms. Three widely used ones, compared from public documentation as of 2026:

| | EJBCA (Keyfactor) | Vault PKI (HashiCorp) and OpenBao | step-ca (Smallstep) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Full-featured CA platform; many CAs per instance | PKI as a secrets engine inside a secrets manager; one CA per mount | Lightweight, single-binary online CA |
| Editions | Community (open source) and Enterprise (appliance, cloud images, SaaS) | Vault Community and Enterprise; OpenBao is an open-source fork | Open source; commercial Smallstep platform adds management |
| Enrolment protocols | ACME, EST, SCEP, CMP, REST and web services | API with roles; ACME since Vault 1.14; EST, CMPv2 and SCEP in Enterprise | ACME, SCEP, OIDC, cloud instance identity, Kubernetes tokens and more, through **provisioners** |
| Key protection | PKCS#11 HSMs, cloud HSMs | Managed keys in HSMs or cloud KMS (Enterprise) | PKCS#11 HSMs, YubiKey, cloud KMS |
| Strengths | Rich profiles, RA and validation authority roles, compliance features for regulated CAs | Short-lived certificates for apps already using Vault; policy tied to Vault identity | Simple to run; ACME-first; also issues SSH certificates |
| Typical enterprise role | Central issuing platform for devices, IoT and regulated use | Workload and application certificates | Developer, DevOps and workload CA; labs |

In EJBCA, **certificate profiles** define what a certificate may contain and **end entity profiles** define who may receive one and what they must supply, a split that maps naturally to a CP and CPS. In Vault, a **role** combines both: allowed domains, key types, TTLs and whether certificates are stored. Vault can issue very high volumes when storage is turned off for short-lived certificates, but then those certificates cannot be listed or revoked individually. step-ca's provisioners decide how a requester proves identity, from an ACME challenge to a signed cloud instance identity document.

A common pattern is to keep the root offline (often generated in a ceremony with OpenSSL or the HSM vendor's tools) and have it sign intermediate CAs that live in two or three of these platforms. All of them chain to the same root, so relying parties trust one anchor, while each platform serves the workloads it fits.

## 15.7 Cloud private CAs

The three hyperscalers approach private CAs differently, as of 2026:

- **AWS Private CA** is a managed CA whose keys live in AWS-managed HSMs. It offers a general-purpose mode and a cheaper **short-lived certificate mode** for certificates valid up to seven days. It publishes CRLs to S3 and runs OCSP, can be shared across accounts, integrates with AWS Certificate Manager and with Kubernetes through a cert-manager issuer, and since November 2025 can create CAs and issue certificates using **ML-DSA**, the post-quantum signature standard (Chapter 45).
- **Google Cloud Certificate Authority Service (CAS)** organises CAs into **CA pools** with two tiers. The **Enterprise tier** stores issued certificates, supports revocation and allows customer-managed keys in Cloud KMS; the **DevOps tier** is for high-volume, short-lived certificates that are not stored or individually revoked. CA keys are HSM-protected in both.
- **Microsoft Azure** has no general-purpose managed private CA comparable to the other two. Its options are **Microsoft Cloud PKI**, a managed CA service for Intune-managed devices (with keys in Azure Managed HSM, and the option to anchor it under your own root); AD CS or another CA platform running on Azure virtual machines with Azure Managed HSM or Dedicated HSM; and third-party CA services from the Azure Marketplace. Azure Key Vault manages certificates and integrates with public CAs, but is not itself a private CA.

Cloud CAs remove the hardest operational work: HSM management, high availability, CRL hosting and patching. Their trade-offs are cost (published pricing for a general-purpose CA runs to hundreds of US dollars per CA per month, so a design with many CAs adds up), dependence on one provider's identity and network, and limited flexibility in profiles compared with EJBCA. A multi-cloud enterprise often keeps its root on premises in its own HSM and signs subordinate CAs that live in each cloud, so all workloads chain to one anchor.

> **Current development.** Cloud private CAs are the first place many enterprises can test post-quantum certificates without building their own tooling. AWS Private CA's ML-DSA support (2025) is an example; check the other providers' current documentation, as support is changing quickly.

## 15.8 Public CA enterprise accounts

Public certificates are also an architecture decision, because large organisations do not buy them one at a time. The main enterprise offerings include DigiCert (CertCentral and Trust Lifecycle Manager), Sectigo Certificate Manager, GlobalSign (Atlas and its managed PKI services), and Entrust, which after its 2024 distrust resells Sectigo's public certificates. Let's Encrypt and other ACME CAs serve the same need without a contract. Common enterprise features are:

- **Pre-validation** of the organisation and of domains, so OV certificates can be issued in minutes until the validation expires (Chapter 13 explains the shrinking reuse periods).
- **Sub-accounts** or divisions for business units, with their own users, budgets and approved domains.
- **ACME with External Account Binding (EAB)**, which ties an ACME client to your paid account, plus REST APIs for CLM platforms.
- **Reporting and discovery**, often combined with CT monitoring.
- **Hosted private CAs**, which many public CAs also sell, so that one vendor can run both public and private issuance.

Choose at least two, and set up the second fully before you need it. How these accounts are integrated into automated issuance, including DCV automation and multi-CA failover, is covered in Chapter 17.

## 15.9 Design decisions and operations

Pulling the chapter together, these are the decisions an architect must make and defend:

- **Build, run or buy.** Self-run AD CS or EJBCA gives control and avoids per-CA fees, but needs HSMs, skilled staff and patching. Cloud CAs and hosted CAs trade money and some flexibility for less operational work. Many enterprises combine them under one root.
- **One root or several.** One root simplifies trust distribution. Separate roots for very different populations (for example, internet-facing IoT devices versus internal users) contain the blast radius of a root-level problem.
- **Where AD CS stops.** Keep AD CS for what it does well (Windows users, machines and smart cards) and move workloads and network devices to platforms with ACME and API-first enrolment.
- **Lifetimes.** Short for workloads (hours to days), moderate for servers (90 days or less, aligned with automation), longer for smart cards and devices that are hard to reach.

Day-to-day operation is where private CAs fail. Plan for CRL publishing and freshness monitoring; CA certificate renewal well ahead of expiry; trust distribution through device management and image pipelines; issuance logging to the SIEM; an annual review of the CP and CPS against reality; and a tested disaster recovery plan that includes restoring HSM keys at the second site.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian's private PKI has one offline root, generated in a recorded ceremony in DC-East's vault with keys in a general-purpose HSM and backups in DC-West. Under it sit the two AD CS issuing CAs (users and devices), an EJBCA-class platform for network devices, ATMs and Meridian Pay terminals, a secrets-manager PKI engine for application workloads, and subordinate CAs in two of its three clouds' managed CA services for cloud-native workloads. Payment HSM keys and their ceremonies are a separate domain run by Payments Security (Chapter 38). The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the root, the CP and CPS, and the policy OID register; its CLM platform (Chapter 17) watches every issuing CA's certificate, CRL and issuance volume.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 3.6 and 3.7 cover protection and lifecycle of cryptographic keys, including split knowledge and dual control for manual clear-text key operations, which M-of-N CA activation satisfies. NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 sets crypto-period guidance for CA signing keys. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A 8.24 requires defined key management, which the CP and CPS document. DORA's ICT risk management requirements expect documented cryptographic controls and tested recovery, which the ceremony records and DR tests evidence.

## 15.10 Lab: build a two-tier private CA

This lab builds a root and a constrained issuing CA with OpenSSL so you see every moving part, then repeats the job with step-ca. In production, generate keys in an HSM (SoftHSM is a free stand-in) and use a CA product; the concepts are identical.

1. **Create the root CA** on a machine you will treat as offline.

    ```bash
    mkdir -p pki/root && cd pki/root
    openssl genpkey -algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-384 \
      -out root.key
    openssl req -x509 -new -key root.key -sha384 -days 7300 \
      -out root.crt -subj "/O=Lab Corp/CN=Lab Corp Root CA 2026" \
      -addext "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE" \
      -addext "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign" \
      -addext "certificatePolicies=1.3.6.1.4.1.99999.10.1"
    ```

2. **Create a constrained issuing CA** and sign it with the root.

    ```bash
    cd .. && mkdir issuing && cd issuing
    openssl genpkey -algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-384 \
      -out issuing.key
    openssl req -new -key issuing.key -out issuing.csr \
      -subj "/O=Lab Corp/CN=Lab Corp TLS Issuing CA 1"
    cat > issuing.ext <<'EOF'
    basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE,pathlen:0
    keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign
    extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth,clientAuth
    nameConstraints=critical,permitted;DNS:.lab.internal
    certificatePolicies=1.3.6.1.4.1.99999.10.1
    EOF
    openssl x509 -req -in issuing.csr -CA ../root/root.crt \
      -CAkey ../root/root.key -CAcreateserial -days 1825 -sha384 \
      -extfile issuing.ext -out issuing.crt
    ```

3. **Issue a leaf certificate** for a service and verify the chain.

    ```bash
    openssl req -new -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
      -nodes -keyout api.key -out api.csr -subj "/CN=api.lab.internal"
    printf "subjectAltName=DNS:api.lab.internal\n" > leaf.ext
    printf "extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth\nbasicConstraints=CA:FALSE\n" \
      >> leaf.ext
    openssl x509 -req -in api.csr -CA issuing.crt -CAkey issuing.key \
      -CAcreateserial -days 30 -sha256 -extfile leaf.ext -out api.crt
    openssl verify -CAfile ../root/root.crt -untrusted issuing.crt api.crt
    ```

4. **Test the name constraint.** Issue a leaf for `api.example.com` from the same issuing CA and verify it. OpenSSL reports a name constraint violation: even a stolen issuing CA key cannot create trusted certificates outside `.lab.internal`.

5. **Read the policy OID.** Run `openssl x509 -in issuing.crt -noout -text` and find the Certificate Policies extension. Imagine an application that accepts only certificates carrying this OID.

6. **Rebuild it the automated way.** Run step-ca in Docker, then request a certificate with the `step` CLI.

    ```bash
    docker run -d --name step -p 9000:9000 \
      -e DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_NAME="Lab CA" \
      -e DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_DNS_NAMES="localhost" \
      smallstep/step-ca
    docker logs step 2>&1 | grep -i password
    ```

    Bootstrap the `step` CLI with the CA's fingerprint (printed in the logs), run `step ca certificate svc.lab.internal svc.crt svc.key`, and inspect the result. Note that step-ca's root and intermediate were created for you, and that it offers ACME out of the box.

**Optional cloud extension.** Create a subordinate CA in a cloud private CA service, export its CSR, sign it with your lab root in step 2's style, and import the signed certificate, so the cloud CA chains to your own root. Cost warning: cloud CAs are billed per CA per month, often hundreds of US dollars for a general-purpose CA; use a short-lived or DevOps-style tier where offered, and delete the CA the same day.

## 15.11 Failure story: the internal CA that expired on a Saturday

A composite scenario, based on common patterns: an organisation builds an internal issuing CA with a five-year certificate. The engineer who built it leaves. Nobody records the CA's expiry date in any monitoring system, because monitoring watches leaf certificates, not CAs. Five years later, on a weekend, the issuing CA certificate expires.

Every certificate it ever issued, for service-to-service authentication, VPN access and internal websites, instantly becomes invalid, because a chain is only valid while every certificate in it is valid. Services cannot authenticate to each other, staff cannot connect to the VPN to fix anything, and the people who could re-sign the CA need to find the root ceremony materials and custodians, which have not been touched in years. Recovery takes days, not hours, because a new issuing CA certificate must be signed offline and then every leaf must be re-issued.

Prevention is simple and unglamorous: track CA certificates and CRLs in the same inventory as leaves, alert at least a year before CA expiry, keep ceremony scripts and custodian lists current, renew issuing CAs at half-life, practise renewal on a schedule, and make sure more than one person knows how.

## 15.12 Chapter summary

- Use private CAs for internal services, workloads, devices and users; they give control over lifetimes, names and issuance, and keep internal names out of CT logs.
- A two-tier hierarchy with an offline root and purpose-specific, constrained issuing CAs suits most organisations; trust distribution sets the real cost of a root.
- A CP states what certificates promise and a CPS how the CA delivers it, in RFC 3647 structure; policy OIDs let relying systems enforce assurance levels.
- Protect CA keys in HSMs, with offline roots, M-of-N control, split backups and recorded key ceremonies.
- AD CS is part of the identity system: the ESC attack classes turn template, ACL, CA-setting, relay and mapping mistakes into domain compromise. Treat CAs as Tier 0, review templates, enforce strong mapping and monitor issuance.
- EJBCA, Vault PKI and step-ca suit devices, workloads and DevOps; cloud private CAs remove HSM and availability work at a per-CA cost. Azure's managed option is Cloud PKI for Intune devices.
- Public CA enterprise accounts provide pre-validation, sub-accounts, ACME with EAB and APIs; keep a second CA ready.
- Operations decide success: CRLs, CA renewals, trust distribution, logging and disaster recovery. Lifecycle automation is covered in Chapters 17 to 19.
