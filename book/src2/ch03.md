## 3.1 Why an architect needs any mathematics

Most of your working life with cryptography will be spent on keys, certificates and policies, not equations. But the mathematics leaks into every one of those. A certificate request asks you to choose between "RSA 3072" and "ECDSA P-256". A cloud KMS offers "secp256k1" next to "P-384". An HSM data sheet lists "Curve25519 support: firmware 7.x and later". And the post-quantum transition (Part XI) exists because of one specific mathematical weakness shared by almost every public-key algorithm in use today.

You need enough mathematics to read those choices, defend them and spot nonsense. This chapter gives you exactly that, with numbers small enough to check on paper. Nothing here requires more than school arithmetic.

## 3.2 Arithmetic that wraps around

A clock is the best introduction to cryptographic arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you add 5 hours, you get 3 o'clock, not 15. The numbers wrap around after 12. Mathematicians write this as:

```text
10 + 5 = 3 (mod 12)
```

and say "10 plus 5 is congruent to 3, modulo 12". The number after "mod" is the **modulus**. Working "mod n" means you only keep the remainder after dividing by n. So `17 mod 5 = 2`, because 17 divided by 5 is 3 with remainder 2. This is **modular arithmetic**.

All the usual operations work:

- **Addition:** `(4 + 9) mod 11 = 13 mod 11 = 2`
- **Multiplication:** `(4 × 9) mod 11 = 36 mod 11 = 3`
- **Exponentiation:** `3^4 mod 7 = 81 mod 7 = 4`

Cryptography relies on this for two reasons. First, numbers stay a fixed size: however many operations you do, the result is always between 0 and n minus 1, which computers and hardware handle efficiently. Second, wrapping around scrambles the relationship between input and output. Knowing that `3^x mod 7 = 4` does not easily tell you x, because the results jump around instead of growing steadily. That scrambling is the seed of every "one-way" function in this chapter.

### Fast exponentiation

Public-key cryptography raises numbers to enormous powers, with exponents hundreds of digits long. Multiplying 3 by itself that many times would never finish. Instead, computers use **square-and-multiply**: write the exponent in binary, square at every step, and multiply in the base whenever the binary digit is 1. Computing `3^13 mod 7`:

```text
13 in binary = 1101
start with 1
bit 1: square (1) then multiply by 3  -> 3
bit 1: square (9 mod 7 = 2) then multiply by 3 -> 6
bit 0: square (36 mod 7 = 1)          -> 1
bit 1: square (1) then multiply by 3  -> 3
result: 3^13 mod 7 = 3
```

The number of steps grows with the number of digits in the exponent, not its size, so a 3072-bit exponent takes a few thousand steps instead of an impossible number.

> **Watch out.** The square-and-multiply pattern is also a classic source of leaks. If the "multiply" steps take measurable time or draw measurable power, an attacker who times or monitors the operation can read the exponent's bits, which is the private key. Production libraries and HSMs use **constant-time** implementations that do the same work for every bit. Chapter 11 explains why side channels like this sit outside the mathematical proofs.

### Inverses and the greatest common divisor

In ordinary arithmetic, dividing by 4 means multiplying by 1/4. In modular arithmetic, "dividing" means multiplying by an **inverse**: a number that gives 1 when multiplied. Mod 11, the inverse of 4 is 3, because `4 × 3 = 12 = 1 (mod 11)`.

An inverse exists only when the number and the modulus share no common factor, that is, when their **greatest common divisor (gcd)** is 1. The ancient **Euclidean algorithm** computes gcds quickly, and its extended version finds inverses. RSA key generation (Chapter 8) depends on it, and so does one of the most effective attacks on badly generated keys: if two RSA keys accidentally share a prime, a single gcd calculation reveals it, as Chapter 4 shows.

## 3.3 Primes and why factoring is hard

A **prime** is a whole number greater than 1 whose only divisors are 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 and so on. Every whole number breaks down into a unique product of primes, for example `60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5`.

Two facts about primes power a large part of cryptography:

1. **Multiplying two large primes is easy.** A computer multiplies two 1536-bit primes in a fraction of a millisecond.
2. **Splitting the result back into its primes is hard.** For a 3072-bit product, no known method on classical computers can find the factors in any useful amount of time.

This one-way behaviour is the **factoring problem**, and RSA is built on it. Note the word "known". Nobody has proved factoring is hard. It is believed hard because the best mathematicians have tried for centuries, and the best general method, the number field sieve, still takes time that grows faster than any polynomial in the number of digits. That belief has a sharp exception: a large quantum computer running **Shor's algorithm** could factor efficiently (Chapter 44).

### Finding large primes

If factoring is hard, how do we find primes in the first place? We do not factor anything. We pick a random large odd number and run a **probabilistic primality test**, such as Miller-Rabin, which quickly answers "definitely composite" or "almost certainly prime". Repeating the test enough times pushes the chance of error below one in 2^128. Primes are common enough among large numbers that a few hundred random tries usually find one.

Two things must be right for this to be safe: the random starting point must be truly unpredictable (Chapter 4), and the search method must not give the primes any hidden structure. The failure story at the end of this chapter shows what happens when the second condition fails inside a widely used chip.

## 3.4 Groups: the structure under everything

A **group** is a set of things plus one operation that behaves nicely. Formally, the operation must combine any two members into another member, have an identity element that changes nothing, give every member an inverse, and be associative. You do not need to memorise the definition. What matters is the idea: a group is a closed world where you can combine elements as many times as you like and always stay inside.

Two groups appear constantly in this book:

- **The multiplicative group mod a prime p.** The numbers 1 to p minus 1, combined by multiplication mod p. Used by classic (finite-field) Diffie-Hellman and DSA.
- **The points on an elliptic curve.** Combined by a geometric "point addition" rule. Used by ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA.

### Generators and order

In some groups, one element can produce every other element by repeated combination. That element is called a **generator**. Mod 7, the number 3 is a generator:

```text
3^1 = 3, 3^2 = 2, 3^3 = 6, 3^4 = 4, 3^5 = 5, 3^6 = 1 (mod 7)
```

Every number from 1 to 6 appears exactly once. The number of distinct elements a generator produces before repeating is its **order**. Cryptographic designs choose groups whose order is a large prime, or contains one, because small orders let attackers break the problem into easy pieces. Careless choices here caused real attacks, such as **small-subgroup attacks**, where an attacker sends a value from a tiny subgroup and learns the victim's secret a few bits at a time. That is one reason modern standards fix the groups for you: TLS 1.3, for example, only allows a short list of named groups.

## 3.5 The discrete logarithm problem

In the list above, going forward is easy: given 3 and 4, computing `3^4 mod 7 = 4` takes one step. Going backward is the hard direction: given that `3^x mod 7 = 4`, find x. This is the **discrete logarithm problem**, and with a well-chosen 3072-bit prime, it is about as hard as factoring a 3072-bit number.

Diffie-Hellman key exchange (Chapter 9) uses it like this. Alice picks a secret a and publishes `g^a`. Bob picks a secret b and publishes `g^b`. Each raises the other's public value to their own secret, and both reach `g^(ab)`. An eavesdropper sees `g^a` and `g^b` but cannot compute `g^(ab)` without, as far as anyone knows, solving a discrete logarithm.

With small numbers you can watch it work. Take p = 23 and g = 5. Alice picks a = 6 and sends `5^6 mod 23 = 8`. Bob picks b = 15 and sends `5^15 mod 23 = 19`. Alice computes `19^6 mod 23 = 2`; Bob computes `8^15 mod 23 = 2`. Both now share the number 2, and an eavesdropper who saw only 8 and 19 must recover 6 or 15 to join them. With 23 that is trivial. With a 3072-bit prime it is not.

## 3.6 Finite fields

A **field** is a set where you can add, subtract, multiply and divide (except by zero), with all the familiar rules holding. A **finite field** is a field with a finite number of elements. Two kinds matter:

- **Prime fields, written GF(p).** The numbers 0 to p minus 1 with arithmetic mod a prime p. Elliptic curves such as P-256 and Curve25519 are defined over prime fields.
- **Binary fields, written GF(2^n).** Elements are strings of n bits, and addition is bitwise XOR. AES does its internal mixing in GF(2^8), the field of all byte values (Chapter 5), and the GCM mode computes its integrity tag in GF(2^128) (Chapter 6).

The useful intuition: a finite field is a set of fixed-size values with arithmetic that never overflows and always has a clean answer, which is exactly what both hardware designers and cryptographers want.

## 3.7 Elliptic curves, without the pain

An **elliptic curve** is the set of points (x, y) satisfying an equation such as:

```text
y^2 = x^3 + ax + b
```

Over ordinary real numbers, this draws a smooth curve. Cryptography uses the same equation over a finite field, so the "curve" becomes a scattered set of points, but the rules carry over.

The key trick is **point addition**. Draw a line through two points on the curve. It hits the curve in exactly one more point. Reflect that point across the x-axis, and you have the "sum" of the first two. Adding a point to itself uses the tangent line instead. With this rule, together with a special "point at infinity" that acts as zero, the points form a group.

Now the hard problem. Pick a starting point G and a secret whole number k. Computing `kG`, meaning G added to itself k times, is fast using the same doubling trick as square-and-multiply (here called double-and-add). But given G and the result, finding k is the **elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP)**. For well-chosen curves, the best known attacks are generic ones that take about the square root of the group size, so a 256-bit curve gives about 128 bits of security. Factoring and ordinary discrete logarithms have cleverer attacks and so need far larger numbers, which is why a 256-bit elliptic curve key matches a 3072-bit RSA key.

The curves you will meet are standardised and named:

| Curve | Used in | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P-256 (secp256r1, prime256v1) | TLS, certificates, ECDSA, almost all HSMs and cloud KMS services | NIST curve; the most widely deployed |
| P-384 (secp384r1) | High-assurance government and financial uses | NIST curve; required by some regulated profiles such as CNSA |
| Curve25519 / X25519 | Key exchange in TLS 1.3, SSH, Signal, WireGuard | Designed to be fast and hard to implement wrongly |
| Ed25519 | Signatures in SSH, software signing, many modern protocols | Edwards form of Curve25519 |
| secp256k1 | Bitcoin and other blockchains | Offered by some cloud KMS services for digital asset use; not a NIST-approved curve for general use |

Notice that one curve has three names. P-256 is NIST's name, secp256r1 is the name in the SEC standards, and prime256v1 is what OpenSSL prints. Expect all three in product documentation and certificate dumps.

> **At Meridian.** The PKI and Cryptography Services team publishes an approved-curves list as part of the cryptographic standard: P-256 and P-384 for certificates and signatures, X25519 and P-256 for key exchange, Ed25519 for SSH and internal signing where the platform supports it. Before adding a curve, the team checks that every relevant HSM firmware version, all three cloud KMS services and the Java, Go and mainframe libraries support it. Support for Ed25519 in particular varies between HSM vendors and cloud KMS services, as of 2026, so the list notes where it is allowed and where P-256 must be used instead.

## 3.8 A first look at lattices

Everything above, factoring and both discrete logarithm problems, falls to Shor's algorithm on a large quantum computer. The replacement algorithms standardised by NIST in 2024 rest mostly on a different kind of mathematics: **lattices**.

Picture a sheet of graph paper stretched and skewed so its grid lines run at odd angles. The crossing points form a lattice. In two dimensions, finding the crossing point closest to some random spot is easy. In 500 or 1000 dimensions, with a deliberately awkward description of the grid, it becomes extremely hard, for classical and quantum computers alike, as far as anyone knows.

The specific problem behind ML-KEM and ML-DSA is called **Learning With Errors (LWE)**. In words: you are given many equations that would be easy to solve, except that each one has a small random error added. Without the errors, ordinary school algebra (Gaussian elimination) would recover the secret in moments. With them, the system becomes unsolvable in practice. A tiny example in mod 13 arithmetic, with secret s = 4:

```text
3s + e1 = 1  (mod 13)   true value 12, error +2
7s + e2 = 3  (mod 13)   true value 2,  error +1
5s + e3 = 7  (mod 13)   true value 7,  error 0
```

With one unknown you could try all 13 values of s. With hundreds of unknowns, the noise defeats every known method.

Lattice schemes pay for this with size. An ML-KEM-768 public key is 1,184 bytes and an ML-DSA-65 signature is 3,309 bytes, compared with 32 bytes for an X25519 public key and about 64 bytes for an Ed25519 signature. Those sizes ripple through certificates, handshakes and HSMs (Chapters 45 and 46). For now, remember one sentence: **post-quantum cryptography replaces "hard to factor" and "hard to find the logarithm" with "hard to find the secret hidden under small noise".**

> **Compliance hook.** NIST SP 800-186 lists the approved elliptic curves for US federal use, FIPS 186-5 the approved signature algorithms and key sizes, and SP 800-131A the transition rules that retire older sizes. FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 specify ML-KEM and ML-DSA. When a regulator or an assessor asks why your policy allows a given key size or curve, these are the documents your answer should cite. Chapter 41 maps them to PCI DSS, ISO 27001 and DORA.

## 3.10 Lab: check the mathematics yourself

You need Python 3 and OpenSSL, both installed on most Linux and macOS systems.

1. Start Python with `python3`. Its built-in `pow` takes a third argument, the modulus, and uses square-and-multiply internally.

```python
pow(3, 13, 7)            # 3, matching the worked example
pow(5, 10**18, 10**9+7)  # instant, despite the huge exponent
```

2. Compute an inverse. Since Python 3.8, a power of minus 1 computes the modular inverse.

```python
pow(4, -1, 11)   # 3, because 4 x 3 = 12 = 1 mod 11
```

3. Replay the toy Diffie-Hellman exchange from section 3.5.

```python
p, g, a, b = 23, 5, 6, 15
A, B = pow(g, a, p), pow(g, b, p)
print(A, B, pow(B, a, p), pow(A, b, p))   # 8 19 2 2
```

4. Solve a toy discrete logarithm by brute force, and time it.

```python
p, g, h = 1000003, 3, 123456
x = next(x for x in range(p) if pow(g, x, p) == h)
print(x, pow(g, x, p))
```

This finishes in a second or so because p has only 7 digits. Each extra digit multiplies the work by about ten. At 925 digits, the size of a 3072-bit prime, brute force is hopeless, and even the cleverest known methods are far out of reach.

5. Build and break toy RSA with tiny primes.

```python
p, q, e = 61, 53, 17
n, phi = p*q, (p-1)*(q-1)
d = pow(e, -1, phi)          # private exponent
c = pow(42, e, n)            # encrypt 42
print(pow(c, d, n))          # decrypt: 42
print([f for f in range(2, n) if n % f == 0][:2])   # factor n
```

Factoring 3233 is instant. That is the whole security argument of RSA in miniature: everything rests on n being too large to factor.

6. Leave Python and look at the same mathematics inside a real key.

```bash
openssl prime -generate -bits 256
openssl genpkey -algorithm RSA -pkeyopt rsa_keygen_bits:2048 -out r.key
openssl pkey -in r.key -text -noout | head -20
openssl ecparam -name prime256v1 -text -noout -param_enc explicit \
  | head -20
```

The RSA dump shows the modulus, both primes and the private exponent. The curve dump shows the field prime, the coefficients a and b, the generator point and its order. Compare them with the definitions in this chapter.

**Optional cloud extension.** Create an asymmetric key in a cloud KMS (AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault or Google Cloud KMS) and list which key specifications and curves it offers. Compare that list with your organisation's approved-curves policy. Cost warning: asymmetric KMS keys carry a monthly charge, so schedule the key for deletion when you finish.

## 3.11 Failure story: primes with a hidden pattern

In October 2017, researchers from Masaryk University in the Czech Republic disclosed a flaw they named **ROCA** (Return of Coppersmith's Attack) in an RSA key generation library used in chips made by Infineon Technologies. To find primes faster, the library built them from a special formula instead of searching from a fully random starting point. The resulting primes looked random but carried a hidden algebraic structure.

That structure was enough for a known mathematical technique (Coppersmith's method) to factor the public modulus far faster than the general methods described in section 3.3. The researchers estimated that a 2048-bit key from an affected chip could be factored for the cost of tens of thousands of dollars of cloud computing, rather than being out of reach entirely. The public key alone was enough, and the researchers published a quick test to tell whether any public key came from the vulnerable library.

The affected chips were everywhere enterprises keep high-value keys: TPMs in business laptops, smart cards, security tokens and national electronic identity cards. Estonia suspended the certificates on hundreds of thousands of ID cards and moved holders to new keys. Organisations had to find every key generated on affected hardware and regenerate it, which was far easier for those that already knew which devices held which keys.

The lessons for an architect:

- **The mathematics of RSA was fine; the way primes were chosen was not.** "Approved algorithm, approved key size" is not enough if key generation is flawed.
- **Hardware certification did not catch it.** Affected chips had passed security evaluations, because the evaluations did not test the structure of generated primes.
- **Public keys can be tested.** Collecting and scanning the public keys in your inventory (certificates, SSH keys, TPM endorsement keys) is a cheap way to catch this whole class of flaw.

## 3.12 Chapter summary

- Modular arithmetic keeps numbers a fixed size and scrambles input-output relationships. Fast exponentiation makes huge powers practical, and must be constant-time in real code.
- Multiplying primes is easy; factoring their product is believed hard. RSA rests on this, and on primes being generated without hidden structure.
- Groups give a closed world for repeated operations. In well-chosen groups of large prime order, finding the discrete logarithm is believed hard. Diffie-Hellman rests on this.
- Finite fields give fixed-size arithmetic that hardware handles well; AES and GCM use binary fields.
- Elliptic curves give a group where only generic attacks are known, allowing much smaller keys. Learn the curve names and their aliases.
- All of these fall to large quantum computers. Lattice problems such as Learning With Errors are the main replacement, at the cost of much larger keys and signatures.
- Approved curves, key sizes and algorithms are set by documents such as NIST SP 800-186, FIPS 186-5 and SP 800-131A, and an enterprise standard should cite them.
