## 12.1 The problem certificates solve

Public-key cryptography lets you verify a signature or encrypt to a key. It does not tell you **whose** key it is. If an attacker can make you believe their public key belongs to your bank, every guarantee from Part II collapses.

A **certificate** solves this with a signed statement: "this public key belongs to `www.bank.example`", signed by a **certificate authority (CA)**. If you already trust the CA's public key, you can check the signature and trust the binding. A certificate works like a passport: the passport office (the CA) vouches that the photo (the public key) belongs to the named person (the **subject**), and border staff (the clients) trust the passport office, not the traveller.

Certificates are **public**. Anyone can see and copy them. They are useless to an attacker without the matching **private key**, which never appears in the certificate. This is why stealing a certificate is harmless and stealing its key is a crisis.

The dominant certificate format is **X.509 version 3**, originally an ITU-T standard and profiled for the internet in **RFC 5280**. A **public key infrastructure (PKI)** is everything around it: the CAs, policies, people and systems that issue, publish, check and revoke certificates. Chapters 13 to 15 cover the public and enterprise PKIs; this chapter is about the document itself.

## 12.2 Anatomy of a certificate

A certificate has three parts: the **to-be-signed** data (all the fields below), the **signature algorithm**, and the **signature** by the issuing CA. The whole structure is written in **ASN.1**, a language for describing data structures, and encoded with the **Distinguished Encoding Rules (DER)**, which guarantee that the same certificate always produces exactly the same bytes. That matters, because the signature covers those bytes.

| Field | What it holds | Example or notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Version** | Always 3 for modern certificates | Extensions require version 3 |
| **Serial number** | A unique number assigned by the issuing CA | Up to 20 bytes; public CAs must include at least 64 random bits (Chapter 7) |
| **Signature algorithm** | How the CA signed | `ecdsa-with-SHA384`, `sha256WithRSAEncryption` |
| **Issuer** | The name of the CA that signed it | `C=US, O=Example CA, CN=Example TLS Issuing CA 2` |
| **Validity** | Not Before and Not After dates | Public TLS certificates: at most 200 days from 15 March 2026 (Chapter 13) |
| **Subject** | Who the certificate is about | Often only a Common Name, sometimes organisation details |
| **Subject public key info** | The public key and its algorithm | ECDSA P-256, RSA 3072, and in future ML-DSA (Chapter 45) |
| **Extensions** | Everything else that matters (section 12.3) | Names, usages, constraints, policy, revocation pointers |

Names in the Subject and Issuer are **Distinguished Names (DNs)**, built from attributes such as `C` (country), `O` (organisation), `OU` (organisational unit) and `CN` (common name). Public CAs stopped including `OU` in TLS certificates in 2022, because it was rarely validated properly. Private CAs may still use it, but nothing should make an access decision on an unvalidated field.

> **Watch out.** Validity dates are in UTC, and many outages begin at a time nobody expected because a team read Not After as local time. Monitoring and renewal tools should always work in UTC.

## 12.3 Extensions: where the real rules live

Extensions turn a bare key-to-name binding into a precise set of permissions. Each extension can be marked **critical**, meaning a client that does not understand it must reject the certificate. Non-critical extensions may be ignored by clients that do not know them.

| Extension | What it does | Typical value for a TLS server | Typical value for a CA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject Alternative Name (SAN)** | The names (DNS, IP, email, URI, UPN) the certificate is valid for | `DNS:shop.example.com, DNS:www.shop.example.com` | Not used |
| **Basic Constraints** | Whether this is a CA, and how many CAs may sit below it | `CA:FALSE` | `CA:TRUE, pathlen:0` for an issuing CA |
| **Key Usage** | Which low-level operations the key may perform | `digitalSignature` | `keyCertSign, cRLSign` |
| **Extended Key Usage (EKU)** | Which applications it may be used for | `serverAuth` | Often restricted on intermediates |
| **Authority Key Identifier / Subject Key Identifier** | Fingerprints that link a certificate to its issuer's key | Present | Present |
| **CRL Distribution Points** | Where to download the revocation list | URL | URL |
| **Authority Information Access** | Where to fetch the issuer's certificate, and an OCSP responder if any | URL | URL |
| **Certificate Policies** | Identifiers of the rules under which it was issued | DV, OV or EV policy OIDs | Policy OIDs |
| **Name Constraints** | Limits which names a CA may issue for | Not used | `permitted: .example.com` |
| **SCT list** | Proof that the certificate was logged in Certificate Transparency | Embedded SCTs | Not used |

Many of these values are **object identifiers (OIDs)**: dotted numbers registered in a global tree, such as `1.3.6.1.5.5.7.3.1` for serverAuth or `2.23.140.1.2.1` for the CA/Browser Forum's domain-validated policy. Tools translate the common ones into names, but you will meet raw OIDs in vendor-specific extensions and in logs.

Three extensions deserve emphasis.

**The SAN is the identity.** Browsers and modern clients check only the SAN, not the Common Name, when matching a hostname. A certificate with a CN but no matching SAN entry fails. Many internal tools still generate CN-only certificates, and they break as libraries tighten.

**Basic Constraints is the most important safety switch in PKI.** It decides whether a certificate can sign other certificates. Bugs that ignored it, such as a 2002 flaw in Internet Explorer that accepted ordinary website certificates as CAs, let anyone with a cheap certificate impersonate any website.

**Name Constraints makes private CAs safer.** A CA constrained to `.example.com` cannot issue a trusted certificate for any other domain, even if its key is stolen. It is underused, and worth adopting for internal CAs and for CAs you allow partners to run (Chapter 15).

- The **root CA** certificate is self-signed. Its trust comes not from the signature but from its presence in the client's **trust store**, the list of roots shipped by the operating system, browser or runtime, or pushed by the enterprise. Root keys are kept offline in HSMs and used only to sign intermediates and revocation lists.
- **Intermediate CAs** (also called subordinate or issuing CAs) are signed by the root and do the day-to-day issuing. If an intermediate is compromised, it can be revoked and replaced without changing every client's trust store.
- **Leaf** (end-entity) certificates are what servers, clients, users and devices present.

To validate a leaf, the client builds a **chain** (also called a path) from the leaf to a trusted root, checking at each link that the signature verifies, the issuer is allowed to be a CA, the dates are valid, constraints are respected and nothing is revoked. Chapter 14 covers path validation and revocation in detail.

The most common deployment error is a server that sends its leaf without the intermediate. Browsers often hide the mistake, because they cache intermediates or fetch them from the Authority Information Access URL. Java applications, mobile apps, IoT devices and command-line tools usually do not, so the same server works in a browser and fails for a partner's API client.

### Cross-signing

A new CA's root takes years to reach enough trust stores. To bridge the gap, an established CA can **cross-sign** the new CA's intermediate or root, so the same key has two certificates: one chaining to the new root, one to the old. Clients with either root can build a valid chain. Let's Encrypt launched in 2015 this way, cross-signed by an established CA, and the expiry of that cross-signature in September 2021 broke connections on many old devices that did not have the newer root. Enterprises use the same technique when they replace an internal root: the new root is cross-signed by the old one during the migration.

## 12.5 Encodings and file formats

Certificate files confuse everyone at first because the same content appears in many wrappers.

| Format | What it is | Typical extensions | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DER** | The binary encoding of a certificate | `.der`, `.cer` | Windows exports, Java, embedded devices |
| **PEM** | DER in base64 between header lines | `.pem`, `.crt`, `.cer`, `.key` | Linux, NGINX, HAProxy, Kubernetes secrets, most appliances |
| **PKCS#12 / PFX** | A password-protected bundle of private key plus certificate chain | `.p12`, `.pfx` | Windows, IIS, Java keystores, device and user enrolment |
| **PKCS#7 / P7B** | A bundle of certificates without keys | `.p7b`, `.p7c` | Chains delivered by CAs, Windows |
| **PKCS#8** | A private key, optionally encrypted | `.key`, `.pem` | `BEGIN PRIVATE KEY` or `BEGIN ENCRYPTED PRIVATE KEY` |
| **CSR (PKCS#10)** | A certificate signing request | `.csr` | Sent to every CA, public or private |
| **JKS** | Java's legacy proprietary keystore | `.jks` | Older Java middleware; PKCS#12 is now Java's default |

A **certificate signing request (CSR)** contains the public key and the requested names, signed with the private key to prove possession. The applicant sends the CSR to the CA, and the private key never leaves the applicant. Where keys live in an HSM, a cloud KMS or a smart card, the CSR is generated there too, so the key is never exported at all.

The format question is really a key custody question. A PKCS#12 file containing a private key, emailed to an application team so they can install it, is a key leaving its protection. Automation that installs certificates through device APIs, or generates the key on the target itself, removes those copies (Chapters 17 and 18).

## 12.6 Enterprise certificate profiles

A **certificate profile** is the complete specification for one kind of certificate: key type and size, subject and SAN rules, Key Usage, EKU, validity, issuing CA, where the key is generated and stored, and how the certificate is requested. Products give profiles different names. Microsoft AD CS calls them **certificate templates**, EJBCA splits them into **certificate profiles** and **end entity profiles**, HashiCorp Vault PKI uses **roles**, step-ca uses **provisioners** with certificate templates, AWS Private CA uses **templates**, and Google Cloud CAS uses **certificate templates** and **issuance policies**, as of 2026. The concept is the same in all of them.

Most enterprises need six families of profile. The table shows a typical starting point; your own values will depend on clients, regulators and hardware.

| Profile | Key and algorithm | Key Usage and EKU | Identity in the certificate | Typical validity | Key storage and enrolment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **TLS server** | ECDSA P-256 or RSA 2048/3072 | digitalSignature (plus keyEncipherment for RSA with TLS 1.2); serverAuth | DNS names and IPs in SAN; CN optional | Public: 200 days falling to 47; private: 30-397 days or shorter | On the server, load balancer HSM or cloud service; ACME, CLM agents, EST or APIs |
| **TLS client and workload** | ECDSA P-256 | digitalSignature; clientAuth | Service or user ID in a URI SAN (such as a SPIFFE ID), DNS name or UPN | Hours to one year | On the workload, TPM or mesh agent; mesh CA, EST, ACME or SCEP; private CA only |
| **Code signing** | RSA 3072 or larger, or ECDSA P-256/P-384 | digitalSignature; codeSigning | Organisation name in subject | At most 460 days for public certificates from March 2026 | Hardware only (HSM, cloud HSM or token) for public certificates; signing service with approvals |
| **S/MIME email** | RSA 2048+ or ECDSA | digitalSignature, and keyEncipherment for encryption; emailProtection | Email address in SAN; name or organisation in subject | Typically one to two years | User device, smart card or managed escrow for encryption keys; enrolment through the mail client or MDM |
| **Device** | ECDSA P-256 or RSA 2048 | digitalSignature; clientAuth (and serverAuth where the device serves) | Device ID, serial or MAC-derived name; DNS name if managed | One to three years; manufacturer IDs for the device's life | TPM or secure element; SCEP or PKCS through MDM, EST, or vendor provisioning |
| **Smart card logon** | RSA 2048 or ECDSA P-256/P-384, card-dependent | digitalSignature; clientAuth and smartcardLogon | User principal name in SAN; strong mapping to the directory account | One to three years | On the card or virtual smart card; enrolment by a registration officer or card management system |

### Notes on each profile

- **TLS server.** Public certificates must follow the CA/Browser Forum rules and the shrinking lifetime schedule (Chapter 13). Private server certificates are free of those rules but should copy their discipline: SAN-only identities, no wildcards outside defined cases, and short lifetimes backed by automation.
- **TLS client.** Public TLS hierarchies are becoming server-only under browser root program rules, so client certificates belong on a private CA or a dedicated client-authentication hierarchy. Bind client certificates to an identity the relying system checks (Chapters 16 and 24).
- **Code signing.** Public code signing certificates have required keys in hardware since June 2023, and their maximum lifetime fell to 460 days from 1 March 2026. Signatures should be **timestamped** by a trusted timestamping authority, so they remain verifiable after the certificate expires (Chapter 36).
- **S/MIME.** Publicly trusted S/MIME certificates have followed their own CA/Browser Forum baseline requirements since September 2023. Many organisations issue separate signing and encryption certificates, so the encryption key can be escrowed for recovery of archived mail while the signing key never is (Chapter 25).
- **Device.** Wi-Fi and VPN access with 802.1X and EAP-TLS rely on device certificates delivered by MDM platforms such as Microsoft Intune, Jamf and others (Chapter 22). Some hardware also carries a manufacturer-installed identity, defined by IEEE 802.1AR as an **IDevID**, which an enterprise can use to bootstrap its own locally issued identity (an **LDevID**).
- **Smart card logon.** Windows domain controllers map the certificate to an account. After Microsoft's May 2022 change (KB5014754), CAs should include the account's security identifier in a dedicated extension, or administrators must configure an explicit strong mapping; domain controllers moved to full enforcement during 2025. US federal PIV cards follow FIPS 201 profiles instead (Chapter 16).

> **At Meridian.** The PKI and Cryptography Services team publishes a profile catalogue of 14 profiles derived from these six families, each with an owner, an approved CA and an enrolment method. Network Engineering owns the TLS server profiles on the load balancers and the 802.1X device profile; Platform Engineering owns the mesh workload profile with 24-hour certificates; IAM owns smart card logon for 1,200 privileged administrators; and Payments Security owns a restricted profile for payment terminal management servers. The CLM platform refuses any request that does not match a catalogue profile, and GRC reviews the catalogue once a year.

## 12.7 Designing and governing profiles

Profiles are where an enterprise PKI is won or lost. The design decisions are few, and each has failed in real environments.

1. **Who supplies the identity?** The safest profiles build the subject and SAN from an authoritative source (the directory, the CMDB or a validated DNS zone) rather than from the request. A profile that lets the requester supply any subject name and also carries clientAuth lets a low-privileged user request a certificate as a domain administrator. This is the best known of the AD CS escalation paths, covered in Chapter 15.
2. **Which EKUs, and how many?** Give each profile only the EKUs it needs. A certificate with no EKU, or with the "any purpose" EKU, is valid for everything, including uses you never intended.
3. **Which keys?** Default to ECDSA P-256 where clients support it; keep RSA where hardware or partners require it (Chapter 8). Plan for post-quantum profiles by making key type a parameter, not an assumption (Chapter 45).
4. **How long?** Shorter is safer, and only sustainable with automation. Match validity to the renewal mechanism: 24 hours for a mesh, 30-90 days for automated servers, longer only where a person must touch the device.
5. **Critical or not?** Mark Basic Constraints and Key Usage critical, as RFC 5280 expects for CAs. Avoid marking vendor-specific extensions critical, or clients that do not know them will reject the certificate.
6. **Who approves?** High-risk profiles (code signing, smart card logon for administrators, any CA certificate) need a second person to approve each request.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 4.2.1.1 requires an inventory of trusted keys and certificates used to protect cardholder data in transit, which is far easier when every certificate maps to a known profile. NIST SP 800-57 and ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control A.8.24 expect documented key types, sizes and lifetimes. The certificate policy and practice statement (CP/CPS) of each CA should list its profiles (Chapter 15).

## 12.8 Lab: take certificates apart and build profiles

You need OpenSSL 3.

1. **Fetch and read a real certificate.**

    ```bash
    openssl s_client -connect www.wikipedia.org:443 \
      -servername www.wikipedia.org -showcerts </dev/null \
      2>/dev/null > chain.txt
    openssl x509 -in chain.txt -noout -text | less
    ```

    Find the serial number, issuer, validity dates, SAN list, Key Usage, EKU, CRL distribution point, Authority Information Access and the embedded SCTs.

2. **Create a small root CA.**

    ```bash
    openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-384 \
      -nodes -keyout root.key -out root.pem -days 3650 \
      -subj "/O=Lab/CN=Lab Root CA" \
      -addext "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE" \
      -addext "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign"
    ```

3. **Write two profiles as extension files.**

    ```bash
    printf '%s\n' "basicConstraints=critical,CA:FALSE" \
      "keyUsage=critical,digitalSignature" \
      "extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth" \
      "subjectAltName=DNS:lab.example.test" > server.ext
    printf '%s\n' "basicConstraints=critical,CA:FALSE" \
      "keyUsage=critical,digitalSignature" \
      "extendedKeyUsage=clientAuth" \
      "subjectAltName=URI:spiffe://lab.test/payments" > client.ext
    ```

4. **Issue a certificate from each profile.**

    ```bash
    for p in server client; do
      openssl req -new -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
        -nodes -keyout $p.key -out $p.csr -subj "/CN=lab-$p"
      openssl x509 -req -in $p.csr -CA root.pem -CAkey root.key \
        -CAcreateserial -days 30 -extfile $p.ext -out $p.pem
    done
    openssl x509 -in client.pem -noout -ext extendedKeyUsage,subjectAltName
    ```

5. **Test the purpose check.** OpenSSL can verify a certificate for a named purpose:

    ```bash
    openssl verify -CAfile root.pem -purpose sslserver server.pem
    openssl verify -CAfile root.pem -purpose sslserver client.pem
    ```

    The second command fails, because the client profile lacks serverAuth.

6. **Convert formats and check that a key and certificate match.**

    ```bash
    openssl x509 -in server.pem -outform DER -out server.der
    openssl pkcs12 -export -inkey server.key -in server.pem \
      -certfile root.pem -out server.p12
    openssl pkcs12 -in server.p12 -noout -info
    openssl x509 -in server.pem -noout -pubkey | openssl sha256
    openssl pkey -in server.key -pubout | openssl sha256
    ```

    Identical hashes in the last two lines mean the key and certificate are a pair. This single check solves a surprising number of deployment mysteries.

**Optional cloud extension.** Create a short-lived private CA in AWS Private CA or Google Cloud CAS, issue a certificate from one of the service's templates, and compare its extensions with your lab certificates. Cost warning: managed private CAs carry a monthly charge per CA even when idle (some offer cheaper short-lived modes), so delete the CA as soon as you finish.

## 12.9 Failure story: the root that expired

On 30 May 2020, a widely used root certificate, the AddTrust External CA Root, reached its expiry date. Modern browsers were unaffected, because they had long since learned other paths to the same intermediate through a newer root. But many servers still sent an old chain that included a cross-signed certificate ending at AddTrust, and many older clients and libraries, including some versions of OpenSSL and GnuTLS used by APIs, payment integrations and embedded devices, stopped at the first path they found instead of searching for an alternative. Those clients began failing with "certificate has expired", even though the server's own certificate was valid.

Over the following days, outages hit a range of services and many internal and partner integrations. The fix was to update the chain the servers sent, removing the expired cross-sign, and to update client libraries. A similar event followed in September 2021, when an older root used by Let's Encrypt's cross-signature expired and affected devices that had not received updated trust stores.

The lesson: a certificate's validity depends on the whole chain, not just the leaf. Inventory the chains you serve, not only the leaves; know which roots your clients and partners trust; and treat root and intermediate expiries as planned events with owners and dates, the same way you treat leaf renewals.

## 12.10 Chapter summary

- A certificate binds a public key to a name with a CA's signature. It is public; the private key is the secret.
- The core fields are serial, issuer, validity, subject and public key; the real rules live in extensions, especially SAN, Basic Constraints, Key Usage, EKU and Name Constraints.
- Chains link leaves to roots through intermediates. Trust in the root comes from the client's trust store, not its signature, and servers must send their intermediates.
- PEM, DER, PKCS#12, PKCS#7, PKCS#8, JKS and CSRs are wrappers for the same few objects. Moving a PKCS#12 file around is moving a private key.
- A certificate profile specifies keys, names, usages, validity, CA, key storage and enrolment. Products call profiles templates, roles or provisioners.
- An enterprise needs at least six profile families: TLS server, TLS client and workload, code signing, S/MIME, device and smart card logon, each with its own rules and owner.
- Profiles that let requesters choose their own identity, or that grant extra EKUs, are a common route to PKI compromise.
- Certificates expire at every level of the chain. Root and intermediate expiries can break old clients.
