## 17.1 Why automate now

For most of the history of the web, a public TLS certificate lasted one to three years, and its expiry date lived in a spreadsheet. That way of working is ending, by rule.

In April 2025 the **CA/Browser Forum**, the industry body in which certificate authorities and browser makers agree the rules for publicly trusted certificates, approved **Ballot SC-081v3**, "Introduce Schedule of Reducing Validity and Data Reuse Periods". It cuts the maximum lifetime of a publicly trusted TLS certificate in three steps, and cuts how long a CA may reuse an earlier check of your domain or your organisation's identity:

| From | Maximum certificate lifetime | Domain validation reuse | Organisation identity reuse |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before 15 March 2026 | 398 days | 398 days | 825 days |
| 15 March 2026 | 200 days | 200 days | 398 days |
| 15 March 2027 | 100 days | 100 days | 398 days |
| 15 March 2029 | 47 days | 10 days | 398 days |

Two different things shrink here, and both matter.

- **Certificate lifetime** sets how often you must replace each certificate. Most teams renew when about a third of the lifetime remains, so a 47-day certificate is replaced roughly every 31 days, about twelve times a year.
- **Validation reuse** sets how often the CA must re-check that you control the domain (**domain control validation**, or **DCV**) and, for organisation-validated certificates, that your company details are still correct (**subject identity information**). A 10-day DCV reuse window means that in practice every renewal needs a fresh proof of domain control. If that proof involves a person adding a DNS record or clicking a link in an email, the person becomes the bottleneck twelve times a year for every name you own.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. An organisation with 4,000 public certificates renewing once a year handles about 4,000 renewals a year, or 16 each working day. At 47 days it handles roughly 47,000 renewals a year, about 190 each working day. No team can do that by hand.

Private certificates, issued by an organisation's own CA, are not bound by SC-081. They still expire, and many organisations deliberately shorten their lifetimes to limit the damage from a stolen key and to force automation early.

### Distrust events and mass revocation

Lifetimes are only one driver. Certificates must sometimes be replaced early and all at once:

- **A CA is distrusted.** In 2024 Google and Mozilla announced that they would stop trusting new TLS certificates from Entrust, after a long series of compliance failures. Every Entrust customer had to move to another CA within months. In 2025 Chrome announced similar action against Chunghwa Telecom and Netlock.
- **A CA must revoke.** The CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements oblige a CA to revoke certificates within 24 hours or five days when, among other reasons, it discovers that their validation was flawed. In July 2024 one large CA had to revoke over 80,000 certificates within days after finding a defect in one of its DNS-based validation paths.
- **A key or algorithm is compromised.** A leaked private key, a vulnerable key generator or a deprecated algorithm forces replacement of every affected certificate.

The only defence is the ability to replace any certificate, or every certificate, on demand.

### Outage economics

When a certificate expires, every client that checks it refuses to connect. There is no grace period and no partial failure. Well-documented examples include a mobile network outage affecting millions of users in 2018, caused by an expired certificate in network equipment software, and a global collaboration-tool outage in 2020. Beyond the outage itself come the emergency change, the regulatory notification for a bank or payment firm, and customers who saw a browser warning.

The expensive part is almost never the certificate. It is that nobody knew it existed, nobody owned it, renewal was manual, or nobody checked that the renewed certificate was actually being served.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian Financial Group holds about 65,000 certificates: roughly 4,000 public (customer website, mobile app APIs, open-banking partner endpoints, Meridian Pay merchant gateways) and the rest private. The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the certificate lifecycle management (CLM) platform. Its 2026 plan has a single headline goal: every public certificate renewed with no human action by March 2027, when the 100-day limit arrives, leaving two years to finish the private estate before 47 days and 10-day DCV reuse make any manual step unsustainable.

## 17.2 Essentials: certificates, chains, CAs, CSRs and keys

This Part is written to stand alone, so this section restates the few ideas everything else depends on. Chapters 12 to 15 cover them in full.

A **key pair** is two mathematically linked numbers. The **private key** is kept secret and is used to sign (or, for some algorithms, decrypt). The **public key** can be shared with anyone and is used to verify signatures made with the private key. Whoever holds the private key can impersonate the system it belongs to, which is why so much of this chapter is about where private keys are created and who can read them.

An **X.509 certificate** is a signed statement that binds a public key to a name. Its main fields are:

- the **subject** and, more importantly, the **subject alternative names (SANs)**: the DNS names, IP addresses or other identities the certificate is valid for
- the **public key**
- the **validity period**: a notBefore and a notAfter date
- **extensions** that limit use, such as key usage and extended key usage (server authentication, client authentication, code signing)
- the **issuer**: the name of the CA that signed it, and the CA's signature

A **certificate authority (CA)** is the system, and the organisation running it, that checks a request and signs certificates. A **root CA** has a self-signed certificate that clients trust directly, because it is shipped in their **trust store** (the browser's or operating system's list of trusted roots, or a list your organisation distributes). Roots are kept offline, so day-to-day signing is done by **intermediate** or **issuing CAs** whose certificates the root has signed.

A **certificate chain** is the sequence from the server's own certificate (the **leaf**), through one or more intermediates, to a trusted root. A server must send its leaf and the intermediates; the client already has the root. A missing intermediate is one of the most common causes of "works in my browser, fails in the app" incidents (Chapter 19 covers diagnosis).

A **certificate signing request (CSR)**, defined in PKCS #10, is how you ask for a certificate. The requester generates a key pair, puts the public key and the names it wants into a CSR, and signs the CSR with the private key to prove it holds that key. The CA checks the request, then issues a certificate containing the same public key. The private key never needs to leave the requester. That property is the foundation of safe automation.

**Revocation** is the CA's way of declaring a certificate invalid before it expires, published through **certificate revocation lists (CRLs)** or the **Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP)**. Revocation checking is patchy in practice, which is one reason the industry is moving to shorter lifetimes instead.

## 17.3 The certificate lifecycle

**Certificate lifecycle management (CLM)** is the set of processes and tools that carry every certificate from the moment it is needed to the moment it is safely gone. Twelve stages are worth naming separately, because each one fails in its own way.

1. **Discovery.** Find certificates that already exist, wherever they are, including ones nobody asked the central team for.
2. **Inventory.** Record each certificate, where it is installed and what it protects, as structured data.
3. **Ownership.** Assign every certificate to an accountable team, so alerts reach someone who can act.
4. **Request.** A person, pipeline or device asks for a new certificate, ideally through a standard protocol or API.
5. **Approval.** Policy decides whether the request may proceed automatically or needs a human decision, for example the first certificate for a new public domain.
6. **Issuance.** The CA validates the request (domain control, organisation details, device identity) and signs the certificate.
7. **Installation.** The certificate, chain and (if generated elsewhere) key are placed on the target and bound to the listener, profile or route that uses them.
8. **Validation.** The new certificate is checked from the outside: correct names, full chain, matching key, served by every node.
9. **Monitoring.** Expiry, policy compliance and the health of each installation are watched continuously.
10. **Renewal.** A new certificate, normally with a new key, replaces the old one before it expires, triggered by a fraction of lifetime or by the CA's renewal guidance.
11. **Revocation.** If a key is compromised, a certificate was mis-issued or a service is withdrawn, the certificate is revoked.
12. **Retirement.** When a service is decommissioned, its certificates are revoked or left to expire, keys are destroyed, and inventory records are closed, so that orphaned certificates do not linger.

Notice that only stage 6 involves the CA. Most of the work, and most of the failures, happen on the organisation's side. Stages 7 and 8 are the subject of Chapter 18. Monitoring, validation, runbooks and KPIs are treated in depth in Chapter 19. This chapter concentrates on the foundations: discovery, inventory, policy, the CAs, the protocols and the platform.

A lifecycle is only automated when the loop from monitoring back to renewal runs without people. Measure automation by whether renewal happens unattended, not by whether a request portal exists.

## 17.4 Discovery

You cannot renew a certificate you do not know about. Discovery never finishes, because new certificates appear every day through projects, appliances, cloud consoles and developers working around slow processes. No single method finds everything, so mature programmes combine five.

### Network scanning across private ranges and DMZs

A **TLS scanner** connects to IP addresses and ports, completes enough of a TLS handshake to receive the server's certificate chain, and records it. Scanning finds systems that nobody registered, which is its main value.

Design choices that decide what a scan actually finds:

- **Ports.** Scanning only 443 misses most of the estate. A useful default list includes 443, 8443, 636 (LDAPS), 993 and 995 (mail), 465 and 587 (SMTP with TLS), 3389 (RDP), 5986 (WinRM), 9093 (Kafka), 1414 (IBM MQ), 5432 and 1433 when TLS is enabled, and the management ports of appliances. Protocols that upgrade to TLS after a plaintext greeting, such as SMTP and LDAP with STARTTLS, need a scanner that knows to send the upgrade command.
- **Server Name Indication (SNI).** A server that hosts many sites chooses which certificate to present from the hostname the client sends. A scan by IP address alone sees only the default certificate. Feed the scanner hostnames from DNS zone data and the inventory, and scan by name as well as by address.
- **Reach.** A **DMZ** (demilitarised zone) is the network segment between the internet and the internal network. Firewalls usually stop internal systems from opening connections into it freely, and stop the DMZ from reaching inward at all. A central scanner therefore cannot see every segment. CLM products solve this with **distributed scanners** (also called sensors or satellites) placed in each segment, reporting results outward to the central platform over one authenticated connection.
- **Etiquette.** Scans can trip intrusion prevention systems and upset fragile operational technology. Agree windows and source addresses with network and security operations, and rate-limit.

### Certificate Transparency monitoring

**Certificate Transparency (CT)** is a system of public, append-only logs into which publicly trusted CAs must submit every TLS certificate they issue; browsers such as Chrome and Safari refuse certificates that do not carry proof of logging (Chapter 13). Monitoring CT logs for your domains (directly, through a service such as crt.sh, or through your CLM platform) reveals every public certificate issued for your names, by any CA, whoever requested it.

CT is the only method that finds certificates you did not request. It catches shadow IT (a marketing agency buying a certificate for a campaign subdomain), certificates a CDN or SaaS provider issued on your behalf, and mis-issuance by a CA or an attacker. Treat every public certificate found in CT but not issued through your process as an exception to investigate.

### CA account imports

Every certificate bought through a commercial CA account, or issued by your own private CA, is recorded in that CA's database. Importing from CA accounts through their APIs gives a complete list of issued certificates, with requester and order details. It is the quickest way to seed an inventory.

### Cloud and Kubernetes API discovery

Cloud certificate stores and platforms can be read through their management APIs:

- AWS Certificate Manager and AWS Private CA, Azure Key Vault and App Service certificates, Google Certificate Manager and Certificate Authority Service all list their certificates and, in most cases, the resources they are attached to (load balancers, CDN distributions, API gateways).
- Kubernetes stores TLS material in **Secrets** of type kubernetes.io/tls. Where cert-manager is installed, its Certificate resources describe what is wanted, which issuer serves it and when it renews.
- Load balancers, application delivery controllers and CDNs expose their certificate stores and bindings through REST APIs (Chapter 18).

API discovery returns stored as well as active certificates, and shows the binding: which listener or route uses which certificate. It needs read-only credentials for every account, subscription, project and cluster, which in a multi-cloud estate is itself an inventory problem.

### Agents on hosts

An **agent** is a small service installed on a server that searches the file system, Java keystores, PKCS #12 files and the Windows certificate store, and reports what it finds. Agents find certificates that are not served on any network port: client certificates, keys used by batch jobs, signing certificates and certificates waiting in staging directories. They also see unprotected private key files. The cost is software to deploy and patch on every host.

| Method | Finds | Misses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Network scanning | Certificates served on scanned addresses and ports, including unknown systems | Unscanned ports and segments; non-default SNI names; stored certificates |
| CT monitoring | Every public certificate for your domains, from any CA | Private certificates; installation locations |
| CA account import | Everything issued through known CA accounts | Installation locations; unknown CAs |
| Cloud and Kubernetes APIs | Stored and bound certificates in integrated platforms | Accounts and clusters not connected |
| Host agents | Files, keystores and OS stores, including private keys | Hosts without agents; appliances |

> **In practice.** Start with CA imports and CT monitoring, which take days and cover the public estate. Add cloud and Kubernetes API discovery next, then distributed network scanning per segment. Deploy agents last, where the other methods show gaps. Every method should feed the same inventory, deduplicated by certificate fingerprint.

## 17.5 The inventory data model

Discovery produces raw findings. An **inventory** turns them into structured records that a platform can act on. The single most important design decision is to separate the certificate from the places it is used. A dashboard that says "12,000 certificates" should always prompt the question: unique certificates, or installations? A wildcard certificate deployed on 200 servers is one certificate and 200 installations, and the numbers can differ by a factor of five.

A workable model has seven entities:

| Entity | Identified by | Key attributes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Certificate** | SHA-256 fingerprint of the certificate | Subject, SANs, issuer, serial number, notBefore, notAfter, key algorithm and size, signature algorithm, extended key usage, template or profile, request ID |
| **Key** | Hash of the public key | Algorithm, size, where generated, where stored (file, keystore, HSM, cloud KMS), exportable or not, the certificates that have used it |
| **Endpoint** (installation) | Host, IP, port and SNI name, or platform resource ID | Target type (load balancer, server, CDN, cluster), binding, discovery source, first and last seen |
| **Application** | Application ID from the service catalogue or CMDB | Business service, criticality, data classification, internet-facing or not |
| **Owner** | Team or group, never only a person | Escalation contact, on-call rota, ticket queue |
| **Environment** | Production, staging, development, or a named zone | Network segment, regulatory scope (for example PCI cardholder data environment) |
| **Policy** | Policy or template name | Allowed CAs, key types, validity, SAN rules, approval requirements |

The relationships carry as much value as the entities. A key can appear in several certificates (key reuse, which policy should flag). A certificate can be installed on many endpoints. An endpoint belongs to an application, which belongs to an owner and runs in an environment that determines its policy. With this model the platform can answer the questions that matter during an incident: which internet-facing production endpoints present a certificate from this CA? Which applications share this key? Who must approve replacing it?

### Ownership is the hardest field

Every certificate needs an owning team and an escalation contact. People leave; teams persist. Fill ownership from several sources, in order of trust: the application catalogue or **configuration management database (CMDB)** matched by hostname, the requester recorded by the CA, the owner of the hosting system, and finally DNS zone delegation. Route everything unresolved to an **unowned queue** that someone works through every week. Ownership coverage is the first metric to drive up, because every later control depends on someone receiving the alert. Chapter 19 covers CMDB integration and ownership onboarding in detail.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian's inventory links certificates to the service catalogue that the CMDB already maintains. When the CLM platform first imported its CA accounts and scan results, 31 percent of certificates had no matched owner. The PKI team published a weekly unowned list by network segment, and Network Engineering and Platform Engineering cleared their segments first, because their load balancers and clusters had the most installations per certificate.

## 17.6 Policy

**Certificate policy**, in the CLM sense, is the set of rules a certificate must satisfy to be issued or to remain compliant. (It is related to, but narrower than, the formal Certificate Policy document a CA publishes under RFC 3647.) Policy is enforced twice: **prevent** at request time, by refusing a request that breaks the rules, and **detect** after discovery, by flagging existing certificates that break them.

### What policy covers

- **Templates.** A **template** (also called a profile) is a named bundle of settings for a class of certificate: "public TLS server", "internal TLS server", "mTLS client", "device identity". Requesters choose a template; they do not set extensions by hand.
- **Approved CAs.** Which public and private CAs each template may use. For public TLS, name at least two CAs, so that a distrust event or outage at one does not stop issuance.
- **Key types and sizes.** ECDSA P-256 is the common default for new TLS deployments; RSA 2048 remains the minimum, with 3072 for longer-lived keys. Post-quantum signature algorithms are not yet accepted in publicly trusted certificates as of 2026, so policy should state how they will be introduced (Chapter 19).
- **SAN rules.** Only domains the organisation owns; no internal hostnames in public certificates; wildcards only with approval, because one wildcard spreads one key across many servers; limits on the number of names per certificate.
- **Validity.** Public certificates just under the current maximum; private certificates shorter, such as 30 to 90 days for servers and hours to days for workloads.
- **Key handling.** A new key at every renewal; generation on the target wherever possible; HSM protection for named high-value keys.
- **Who may request what.** Role-based rules tying templates and domains to teams. The payments team may request names under the Meridian Pay domains; an application team may not.

### Layering and exceptions

Layer the rules: a company baseline, then environment rules (internet-facing, internal, non-production), then application rules. Each layer may only tighten the one above. Exceptions, such as an appliance that only supports RSA 2048 with a two-year certificate, must be written down with an owner, a reason, compensating controls and an end date.

Policy is easiest to review and test when it is held as data. A simplified example:

```yaml
template: internal-tls-server
issuers: [meridian-issuing-ca-2, meridian-issuing-ca-3]
key:
  allowed: [ecdsa-p256, ecdsa-p384, rsa-3072]
  generate: on-target
  reuse_on_renewal: false
validity_days: 90
renew_at_remaining_fraction: 0.33
san:
  dns_suffixes: [".corp.meridian.example", ".svc.cluster.local"]
  wildcards: approval-required
  max_names: 20
request:
  allowed_groups: [app-teams, platform-eng]
  approval: none-for-renewal, owner-for-new-name
```

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 4.2.1.1 requires an inventory of the trusted keys and certificates used to protect cardholder data in transit, and requirement 4.2.1 requires that those certificates be confirmed valid and not expired or revoked. NIST SP 800-53 control SC-17 (Public Key Infrastructure Certificates) requires certificates to be issued under an approved policy, and ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A control 8.24 (use of cryptography) expects rules for key management across the lifecycle. A CLM inventory with enforced templates is the most direct evidence for all three.

## 17.7 Public CAs and automated validation

A **public CA** is one whose root certificates are included in browser and operating system trust stores, so its certificates are trusted by anyone on the internet. Enterprises typically hold accounts with several: **DigiCert**, **Sectigo** and **GlobalSign** for commercial certificates with account management and support; **Let's Encrypt**, **Google Trust Services** and **ZeroSSL** for free, ACME-only DV certificates; and the CAs built into cloud services, such as AWS Certificate Manager, which issue certificates only for use inside that provider. **Entrust** was a major choice until browsers stopped trusting its new TLS certificates in late 2024, which is why the earlier advice to hold two CAs is no longer theoretical.

### DV, OV and EV

Every public TLS certificate is one of three validation types:

- **Domain validated (DV).** The CA checks only that the requester controls each domain name. Issuance can be fully automatic, in seconds.
- **Organisation validated (OV).** The CA also checks that the organisation named in the certificate exists and authorised the request, using business registries and a verified telephone call or contact. Under SC-081 this subject identity information may be reused for 398 days.
- **Extended validation (EV).** A stricter version of OV defined by its own guidelines. Browsers no longer display EV differently from other certificates, so its value today lies mainly in specific regulatory or contractual expectations.

The trick with OV and EV is to separate the slow human step from issuance. The organisation is validated once at the account level and refreshed before its reuse period ends; after that, each OV certificate needs only an automated domain check and can be issued by API or ACME as quickly as DV.

### Domain control validation methods

The CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements (section 3.2.2.4) list the permitted DCV methods. The ones that matter for automation are:

- **DNS TXT record.** The requester publishes a random token in a TXT record under the domain. ACME's **DNS-01** challenge uses the name `_acme-challenge.<domain>`. DNS-01 is the only ACME method that can validate wildcard names, and it works for hosts that are not reachable from the internet, because the CA queries public DNS rather than the host.
- **HTTP file.** The requester serves a token at a well-known URL on port 80; ACME's **HTTP-01** challenge uses `/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>`. It needs the CA to reach the web server, so it cannot validate internal-only hosts or wildcards.
- **TLS-ALPN-01.** The server answers a special TLS handshake on port 443 (RFC 8737). It suits TLS terminators that cannot serve files on port 80.
- **Email to constructed addresses** such as admin@ or hostmaster@ the domain. It is still permitted, but it puts a person in the loop. Methods that relied on contact details looked up in WHOIS have been withdrawn.

Since March 2025, CAs must perform **multi-perspective issuance corroboration (MPIC)**: they repeat each validation from several network locations and require them to agree, which makes DNS or routing hijacks of a single path much harder. For you, it means a challenge must be answerable from anywhere on the internet, so split-horizon DNS that answers differently inside and outside can fail validation (Chapter 18).

### DNS-01 at scale: API access and CNAME delegation

DNS-01 requires the automation to write DNS records. Giving every ACME client and pipeline credentials that can rewrite the whole public zone is dangerous: a compromised build server could repoint the customer website. Two patterns reduce the risk.

The first is **scoped API credentials**. Some DNS providers can restrict a token to TXT records, or to names beginning with `_acme-challenge`.

The second is **CNAME delegation**. In the production zone, create once:

```text
_acme-challenge.shop.example.com.  CNAME
    shop.acme-delegate.example.net.
```

The zone `acme-delegate.example.net` lives in a small, separate zone, often on a different DNS service, whose credentials only the validation automation holds. When the CA looks up `_acme-challenge.shop.example.com` it follows the CNAME and finds the token there. The automation can answer challenges but cannot change any real record. Tools such as acme.sh and lego support this directly, and open-source **acme-dns** is a small DNS server built for exactly this purpose. Some commercial CAs offer their own variant, in which you point a fixed CNAME at a CA-hosted name once and the CA validates by looking it up.

CNAME delegation must be exactly right, character for character. The 2024 mass revocation described in Section 17.1 was traced to a CNAME-based validation path that omitted the required underscore prefix in some cases (Chapter 19 tells that story in full).

### Domain pre-validation for many domains

Commercial CA accounts let you **pre-validate** a domain: you prove control of example.com once at account level, and every certificate for that domain and its subdomains can then be issued without a new challenge until the reuse period ends. With 10 days, pre-validation must itself be automated: the CLM platform or a script requests a fresh token from the CA's API, writes it to DNS (or to a delegated name), asks the CA to check, and repeats before each window closes. Several commercial CAs' ACME services, used with External Account Binding (Section 17.9), rely on these account-level validations rather than per-order challenges.

Two more controls belong with DCV:

- **CAA records** (RFC 8659) in DNS list which CAs may issue for a domain. List every CA you use, including the backup, and consider the accounturi parameter (RFC 8657), which limits issuance to a specific ACME account.
- **Rate limits.** Free ACME CAs publish limits, such as a weekly cap on new certificates per registered domain. Design bulk renewals to stay inside them, and use renewal windows from the CA (ARI) to spread load.

> **Watch out.** DNS-01 makes the DNS API credential the most powerful secret in the certificate programme. Whoever holds it can obtain trusted certificates for your names from any CA your CAA records permit. Store it in a secrets manager, scope it, rotate it and alert on its use outside the automation.

## 17.8 Private CAs

A **private CA** is one your organisation runs (or rents) whose root is trusted only by devices you configure. It issues the great majority of an enterprise's certificates: internal TLS, mutual TLS between services, device and user authentication, VPN and Wi-Fi, and workload identity. Private CAs are not bound by CA/Browser Forum rules, so you choose the lifetimes, name forms and validation methods. Chapter 15 covers their design, hierarchy and hardening; here the question is how a CLM platform talks to them.

| Private CA | Typical home | Automation interfaces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Microsoft AD CS** | Windows estates, users, machines, Wi-Fi and VPN | Native DCOM enrolment and auto-enrolment through Group Policy; enrolment web services over HTTPS (CEP and CES); NDES for SCEP; CLM platforms use a gateway or agent on a Windows host |
| **EJBCA** (Keyfactor; Community and Enterprise editions) | General-purpose enterprise and device PKI | ACME, EST, SCEP, CMP, REST and SOAP APIs |
| **HashiCorp Vault PKI** secrets engine | Applications and platforms already using Vault | Vault HTTP API (issue and sign endpoints); ACME in recent versions |
| **Smallstep step-ca** | DevOps teams, workloads, SSH | ACME, OIDC and token-based provisioners, SCEP; open source with a commercial edition |
| **AWS Private CA** | AWS workloads, IoT | AWS API and SDKs; integrates with AWS Certificate Manager; short-lived certificate mode |
| **Google Certificate Authority Service** | Google Cloud workloads | Google Cloud API; DevOps and Enterprise tiers |
| **Azure options** | Intune-managed devices; Azure workloads | Microsoft Cloud PKI for Intune devices; Azure Key Vault issues through integrated partner CAs or self-signed certificates; many Azure estates use a third-party private CA |

A CLM platform connects to each through a **CA connector** (also called a CA gateway or plug-in) that translates the platform's standard request into the CA's API, and imports issued certificates back. Connector coverage is one of the main differences between products, and it is worth testing with your actual CA versions before purchase.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian runs an offline root and two AD CS issuing CAs for Windows machines, users and Wi-Fi, an EJBCA cluster with its keys in the general-purpose HSMs for servers, network devices and mTLS, and the cloud private CA services of two of its three cloud providers for cloud-native workloads. The CLM platform connects to all of them and to two public CAs, so an application team requests "internal-tls-server" without needing to know which CA serves it.

## 17.9 Enrolment protocols in depth

An **enrolment protocol** is the standard conversation by which a client obtains a certificate from a CA or registration authority. The choice determines how the client authenticates, where the key is generated and whether renewal can be automatic.

### ACME (RFC 8555)

The **Automatic Certificate Management Environment (ACME)**, RFC 8555, was created by the Internet Security Research Group for Let's Encrypt, which launched in 2015 and now issues certificates for hundreds of millions of websites. It is the default protocol for TLS server certificates, public and increasingly private.

ACME runs over HTTPS with JSON messages. Every request is signed with the client's **account key**, using JSON Web Signature, so the server knows which account sent it, and every request carries a single-use **nonce** to stop replays. The flow:

1. The client fetches the server's **directory**, a JSON document listing the URLs for each operation.
2. It creates an **account** (newAccount) by sending its account public key, signed with the account private key.
3. It places an **order** (newOrder) listing the identifiers (DNS names or IP addresses) it wants.
4. For each identifier the server returns an **authorisation** containing one or more **challenges** (HTTP-01, DNS-01, TLS-ALPN-01). The client completes one and tells the server it is ready.
5. The server validates. When every authorisation is valid, the order becomes ready.
6. The client **finalises** the order by sending a CSR, generated locally with a fresh key.
7. The server issues the certificate, and the client downloads the chain.

Renewal is simply a new order. Revocation is a signed request to the revokeCert URL. Clients exist for every environment: Certbot, lego and acme.sh for servers; built-in ACME in Caddy, Apache (mod_md) and some load balancers and appliances; cert-manager in Kubernetes; and the agents of every CLM platform. Private CAs and CLM platforms also offer ACME endpoints, so the same client can talk to public and private CAs.

**External Account Binding (EAB)**, defined in RFC 8555 section 7.3.4, ties an ACME account to an existing account at a commercial CA. The CA gives you a key identifier and an HMAC key. The client uses them to sign its new account request, proving the ACME account belongs to that customer. The CA can then apply the customer's contract, pre-validated domains, OV details and billing. Treat the EAB HMAC key as a credential: anyone holding it can create ACME accounts under your contract.

**ACME Renewal Information (ARI)**, standardised in 2025 as RFC 9773, lets the CA tell each client when to renew. The client asks the CA's renewalInfo endpoint about a certificate, identified by its authority key identifier and serial number, and receives a **suggested window**. Normally the window sits in the last third of the lifetime and is randomised to spread load. If the CA must revoke early, it moves the window into the past, and every ARI-aware client renews at its next check, without anyone being paged. The new order says which certificate it **replaces**, so the CA can confirm that the replacement happened. Chapter 19 covers ARI readiness in operations.

> **Current development.** As of 2026, the IETF ACME working group and the CA/Browser Forum are also working on **profiles**, which let a client choose between certificate types (for example a standard or a short-lived certificate) offered by the same CA, and on persistent DNS validation records that would not need rewriting for every challenge. Let's Encrypt already offers profiles, including six-day certificates. Check the status of each before relying on it.

### EST (RFC 7030)

**Enrollment over Secure Transport (EST)**, RFC 7030, was designed for devices and clients that need certificates from an enterprise CA. It runs over HTTPS at fixed paths under /.well-known/est/:

- **cacerts** returns the current CA certificates, so a device can bootstrap its trust.
- **simpleenroll** accepts a PKCS #10 CSR and returns a certificate.
- **simplereenroll** renews, authenticated with the certificate being renewed.
- **csrattrs** tells the client which attributes and key types the CA expects.
- **serverkeygen** (optional) generates the key on the server and returns it with the certificate, for devices that cannot generate good keys.

The client authenticates either with TLS client authentication (using an existing certificate, such as a manufacturer-installed device certificate) or with HTTP authentication over TLS for the first enrolment. Because EST runs over TLS, the client verifies the server, which SCEP never did. EST suits network equipment, IoT and industrial devices, and any client with a TLS stack. EJBCA, most commercial CLM platforms and a growing number of network devices support it, although device support is still less universal than SCEP.

### SCEP (RFC 8894)

The **Simple Certificate Enrollment Protocol (SCEP)** was originally developed at Cisco, used for two decades from Internet-Drafts, and finally published as informational RFC 8894 in 2020. It runs over plain HTTP, with security inside PKCS #7 (CMS) messages: GetCACert fetches the CA certificate, and PKIOperation carries an encrypted, signed CSR.

The weakness is authentication. A first request is authorised by a **challenge password** embedded in the CSR. In many deployments that password is static and shared, so anyone who learns it can request certificates. Modern deployments fix this with a **dynamic challenge**: a mobile device management (MDM) service such as Microsoft Intune generates a one-time challenge for each device, and the SCEP gateway (Microsoft NDES with the Intune connector, or a CLM platform's SCEP service) validates it with the MDM service before passing the request to the CA. SCEP also offers weaker algorithm agility than newer protocols.

Use SCEP where it is the only option the device supports: MDM-managed phones and laptops, older network equipment, many printers and appliances. Always pair it with dynamic challenges, restrict which templates it can reach, and keep the SCEP endpoint off the internet unless an MDM design needs it there.

### CMP (RFC 4210, RFC 9480 and RFC 9810)

The **Certificate Management Protocol (CMP)** is the most complete of the enrolment protocols. RFC 4210 (2005) defined version 2; RFC 9480 (2023) updated it; and in 2025 RFC 9810 replaced both with CMP version 3, which also adds support for key encapsulation (KEM) keys used in post-quantum cryptography, with RFC 9811 covering transfer over HTTP.

CMP defines messages for initial registration (ir), certification requests (cr and p10cr), key update (kur), revocation (rr), key recovery and confirmation, protected either by a shared secret MAC (for first enrolment) or by a signature with an existing certificate. It supports proof of possession for keys that cannot sign, central key generation and multi-hop registration authorities. Its richness made it hard to implement interoperably, so the **Lightweight CMP Profile** (RFC 9483) narrows it to the common cases.

CMP is used where vendors and standards bodies chose it: mobile network equipment (3GPP specifies it for base station certificates), industrial automation, and some smart-meter and railway systems. EJBCA and several commercial CAs support it; OpenSSL includes a CMP client.

### CA REST APIs

Every commercial CA and cloud private CA offers a proprietary **REST API**: DigiCert CertCentral and Trust Lifecycle Manager, Sectigo Certificate Manager, GlobalSign Atlas, AWS Private CA (IssueCertificate), Google CAS, Vault (/pki/issue and /pki/sign) and EJBCA. APIs expose features no standard protocol covers: product and validity choices, OV organisation details, pre-validation workflows, approvals, reporting and bulk revocation. That richness costs portability: integrating directly with one CA's API is exactly the coupling that hurt organisations when Entrust was distrusted. CLM platforms absorb this by putting CA APIs behind connectors.

### When to use each

| Protocol | Best for | Client authentication | Key generation | Main caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ACME | TLS servers, Kubernetes, load balancers with ACME, public and private CAs | Account key, optionally EAB; identifier challenges | On the client | DNS credentials; client must handle ARI and rate limits |
| EST | Network and IoT devices, enterprise clients with TLS | TLS client certificate or HTTP authentication over TLS | Client, or server with serverkeygen | Patchier device support |
| SCEP | MDM-managed endpoints, legacy devices and appliances | Challenge password (use dynamic) | On the device | Static shared secrets; weak algorithm agility |
| CMP | Telecom, industrial and standards-mandated devices | Shared-secret MAC or signature | Client or central | Complexity; use the lightweight profile |
| CA REST API | CLM platforms, OV and EV orders, bulk operations | API keys or OAuth | Wherever the caller chooses | Vendor lock-in; protect API keys |
| AD CS native enrolment | Domain-joined Windows machines and users | Kerberos (domain identity) | On the machine, optionally TPM | Template misconfiguration (Chapter 15) |

### The central service

At the centre sit the inventory database, the policy engine, the workflow engine, the audit log and an API. Everything else is a client of the central service. Commercial platforms are offered as **SaaS** (the vendor runs the central service), **self-hosted** (you run it on your servers or cloud accounts) or both. SaaS removes the burden of patching and scaling, but places your inventory, and possibly stored private keys, in the vendor's environment, and it needs outbound connectivity from every segment you manage. Regulated firms often accept SaaS for inventory and orchestration while insisting that private keys never leave their own networks, which the distributed components below make possible.

### CA connectors

A **CA connector** translates the platform's request into a specific CA's API or protocol, tracks the order and imports the result. Connectors are what make a platform **CA-agnostic**: when a CA is distrusted, you change the CA on the affected templates, and the next renewal of every certificate under them goes to the new CA. Check connectors for every CA you use or might switch to, including OV, pre-validation and revocation features.

### Agents versus agentless delivery

Delivery puts the certificate where it is used. Chapter 18 explores each pattern platform by platform; architecturally there are two families.

- **Agentless push.** The platform (or a connector near the target) logs in to the target's management API, such as a load balancer's REST interface, a CDN's API, a firewall's XML API or a cloud provider's certificate service, and installs and binds the certificate. Nothing is installed on the target, but the platform holds administrative credentials for every target, which makes it a high-value system.
- **Agents.** A small service on the host polls the platform over outbound HTTPS, receives work, generates the key locally, submits a CSR, installs the result and reports back. Agents keep keys on the host and need no inbound firewall rules, but must be deployed, updated and monitored on every server.

Native protocol clients (ACME, EST, SCEP, cert-manager) are a third option in which the target itself does the work and the platform acts as, or proxies to, the CA.

### Satellites and proxies for segmented networks

Enterprise networks are divided into zones by firewalls: internal, DMZ, PCI cardholder data environment, operational technology, partner extranets, cloud VPCs. A central platform cannot open connections into all of them, and security teams will rightly refuse to allow it. Products solve this with a **satellite**, **gateway** or **proxy** component installed inside each zone. It runs scans, pushes to targets and hosts local enrolment endpoints, and it connects outward to the central service over one authenticated, encrypted channel. A well-designed satellite can also generate and hold keys locally, so private keys for targets in that zone never transit the central service.

### RBAC, workflow and approvals

**Role-based access control (RBAC)** decides who may see, request, approve, renew, revoke or export which certificates. Typical roles are platform administrator, policy administrator, application owner, approver, read-only auditor and pipeline service accounts. Scope roles by inventory attributes (application, environment, domain), not by lists of individual certificates.

**Workflow** routes requests through approval when policy requires it and lets the platform call out to other systems: open a change ticket, update the CMDB, notify a chat channel. Keep approvals for decisions that need a human, such as the first certificate for a new public domain or a wildcard, and not for routine renewals with unchanged names. Chapter 19 covers agreeing standard changes with change management.

### HSM-backed keys

The platform holds secrets of its own: CA API keys, EAB credentials, DNS credentials, target administrator passwords and, where it generates keys centrally, private keys awaiting delivery. These must be encrypted at rest under a master key that is itself protected by a **hardware security module (HSM)** or cloud KMS (Chapter 33). For targets that support it, the platform can also have keys generated directly inside an HSM (a network HSM used by a load balancer, or the appliance's own FIPS module) so that the key is never present in software anywhere.

### High availability

Once renewals are automated, the platform is in the critical path: an outage of a week can let short-lived certificates expire across the estate. Run the central service as a cluster across two sites, replicate its database, deploy at least two satellites per zone, and monitor the job queue as well as the servers. For SaaS, check that availability commitments cover enrolment endpoints and connectors, not only the console. Test recovery from backup, including the HSM-protected master key, every year.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian runs its CLM platform self-hosted, active in DC-East and standby in DC-West, with its master key in the general-purpose HSM cluster. Satellites sit in the internet DMZ, the PCI zone for Meridian Pay, the branch management network and each cloud provider's shared-services network. Payments Security insisted that keys for the card-processing endpoints be generated by the satellite inside the PCI zone, so that no key for those systems ever crosses the zone boundary.

## 17.11 Platforms compared

Commercial CLM platforms come from two directions: **specialist vendors** that are CA-agnostic by design, and **public CAs** that have built CLM around their own issuance and added support for other CAs. Open-source tools solve narrower problems very well. The comparison below is by capability category, based on public documentation as of 2026. Product names, packaging and features change often, so verify current details with each vendor.

| Platform | Origin and deployment | Distributed components | CA approach | Distinctive strengths |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| **Keyfactor Command** | Specialist; SaaS and self-hosted | Universal Orchestrator, with integration extensions published as open source | CA-agnostic; owns EJBCA, so can bundle private PKI | Combined CLM and PKI from one vendor; open extension model |
| **AppViewX** (AVX ONE) | Specialist; SaaS and self-hosted | Gateways and agents for segmented networks | CA-agnostic, with PKI as a service available | Workflow and low-code automation, network and ADC automation heritage |
| **DigiCert Trust Lifecycle Manager** | Public CA; part of the DigiCert ONE platform | Sensors for network discovery, agents on hosts | Deepest with DigiCert public and private CAs; selected third-party CAs | Single vendor for public CA, private CA and CLM |
| **Sectigo Certificate Manager** | Public CA; SaaS | Network agents for discovery and delivery | Sectigo public and private CAs; discovers and manages other CAs' certificates | CA-agnostic discovery from a CA vendor; protocol endpoints for devices |

Some fair observations follow from the architectures:

- **CA-agnostic specialists** fit organisations with several CAs, a history of acquisitions or a desire to avoid CA lock-in. The trade-off is another vendor relationship and, usually, a higher price.
- **CA-owned platforms** simplify buying (one contract for certificates and management) and integrate their own validation and issuance tightly. Before committing, test how well they manage certificates from competing CAs, and plan how you would switch issuance if that CA were ever distrusted.
- **Every platform** offers ACME, a REST API, discovery and some form of distributed component in 2026. Differences lie in the breadth and quality of target integrations, the self-hosted option, RBAC granularity and how keys are handled in segmented networks. Run a proof of concept against your hardest targets: a load balancer HA pair, a DMZ appliance, a Java keystore and a Kubernetes cluster.

### Open-source options

| Tool | What it does | What it leaves to you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **cert-manager** | Kubernetes controller (a CNCF project) that requests and renews certificates from ACME, Vault, CA and cloud issuers and stores them as Secrets | Anything outside Kubernetes; estate-wide inventory and ownership |
| **Certbot** | The EFF's ACME client, with plug-ins for web servers and many DNS providers | Discovery, policy, reporting; non-ACME CAs |
| **acme.sh** | ACME client written in shell, with support for well over a hundred DNS provider APIs and DNS alias (CNAME) mode | Same as Certbot; careful handling of its stored DNS credentials |
| **Smallstep** (step-ca and the step CLI) | Private CA with ACME, OIDC, token and SCEP provisioners; SSH certificates; commercial platform also available | Public CAs; estate-wide CLM functions in the open-source edition |

Open-source tools are excellent at the issuance and renewal loop for the systems they run on, and many large organisations use them inside a commercial CLM programme: cert-manager in every cluster, reporting to the platform; Certbot or lego on Linux servers, pointed at the platform's or CA's ACME endpoint. What they do not provide is the enterprise layer: discovery across the estate, a single inventory, ownership, policy, RBAC and audit. Building that layer in-house is possible, but budget for running it as a product.

## 17.12 Where keys are generated

Every certificate integration must answer one question first: **where is the private key created, and who can read it afterwards?** There are three places.

**On the target.** The device, server or application generates the key and a CSR; only the public key and certificate travel. ACME, EST simpleenroll, SCEP, agents and cert-manager all work this way. The key never crosses a network, so a breach of the CLM platform does not reveal it. The target must have a decent random number generator (Chapter 4 explains why embedded devices and freshly cloned virtual machines sometimes do not), and the key is only as safe as the target's storage. Where the target has a **TPM** or secure enclave, the key can be generated inside it and made non-exportable.

**Centrally, then delivered.** The CLM platform or a satellite generates the key and delivers it with the certificate, usually as a password-protected PKCS #12 file or through the target's import API. This is sometimes necessary: for appliances that cannot create CSRs, for high-availability pairs or server farms that must present the same key, and for encryption certificates (such as S/MIME) where the key must be **escrowed** so that old mail can be recovered. The cost is that a copy of the key exists outside the target, at least briefly, and the platform becomes a store of private keys. Mitigate it by generating close to the target (in the satellite for that zone), encrypting under an HSM-protected key, deleting after delivery unless escrow is required, sending only over encrypted management channels and logging every export.

**In an HSM.** The key is generated inside a hardware security module, either the target's own (a load balancer's FIPS module, a network HSM it uses) or a central HSM used by the application through PKCS #11 or a cloud KMS. The key can never be exported in plaintext. This is the strongest protection and is expected for CA keys, payment systems and code signing. It also makes renewal harder: the CSR must be generated through the HSM interface, high-availability peers must share HSM access rather than copy keys, and a lost HSM partition means a lost key (Chapter 19 lists the failure modes).

| Location | Key exposure | Operational cost | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On the target | Lowest in transit; depends on target storage | Low; standard protocols | Default for servers, clusters, devices |
| Central, delivered | A copy exists outside the target | Medium; secure delivery and deletion | Appliances without CSR support, shared-key clusters, escrowed encryption keys |
| HSM | Lowest overall; non-exportable | Highest; HSM capacity and integration | CA keys, payments, code signing, named crown-jewel services |

Two rules hold whichever you choose. Generate a **new key at every renewal**: renewing with the same key means a key stolen last year still works next year. And never generate a TLS server key on a person's laptop and email it to the server team; treat any key generated outside a controlled system as an exception with an end date.

> **Compliance hook.** NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 sets out key lifecycle states and cryptoperiods, which a new-key-per-renewal policy implements directly. PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 3.6.1 requires that cryptographic keys used to protect stored account data be protected against disclosure and misuse, and the same discipline is sensible for TLS keys inside the cardholder data environment. FIPS 140-3 validated modules give the evidence that HSM-held keys are non-exportable.

## 17.13 Lab: a private ACME CA with step-ca, and issuance with lego and Certbot

In this lab you run a private CA with an ACME endpoint on your laptop, obtain and renew certificates with two different ACME clients, watch the protocol at work and cause a validation failure on purpose. You need Docker, curl and OpenSSL. Everything runs locally and costs nothing.

All containers share a private Docker network, so the CA can reach each client by a hostname for HTTP-01 validation, exactly as a public CA reaches your web server.

1. **Create a network and start step-ca with ACME enabled.** The image initialises a new root and intermediate on first start and adds an ACME provisioner named acme.

    ```bash
    docker network create cert-lab
    docker run -d --name ca --network cert-lab -p 9000:9000 \
      -e DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_NAME="Lab CA" \
      -e DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_DNS_NAMES=localhost,ca \
      -e DOCKER_STEPCA_INIT_ACME=true \
      smallstep/step-ca
    docker logs ca 2>&1 | tail -20
    ```

    The log shows the root fingerprint and the generated administrator password. In a real deployment the root and intermediate keys would be created in a ceremony and the intermediate key held in an HSM (Chapter 15).

2. **Fetch the root certificate and read the ACME directory.** Clients must trust the lab root, just as browsers trust public roots.

    ```bash
    mkdir -p lab
    docker cp ca:/home/step/certs/root_ca.crt lab/root_ca.crt
    curl -s --cacert lab/root_ca.crt \
      https://localhost:9000/acme/acme/directory
    ```

    The JSON lists newNonce, newAccount, newOrder, revokeCert and keyChange: the URLs described in Section 17.9.

3. **Issue a certificate with lego using HTTP-01.** The --network-alias option gives the lego container the name app.lab.test on the lab network, so the CA can connect to it on port 80 to fetch the challenge token.

    ```bash
    docker run --rm --network cert-lab \
      --network-alias app.lab.test \
      -v "$PWD/lab:/data" \
      -e LEGO_CA_CERTIFICATES=/data/root_ca.crt \
      goacme/lego \
      --server https://ca:9000/acme/acme/directory \
      --email ops@lab.test --accept-tos \
      --domains app.lab.test --http --path /data/lego run
    ```

    Read lego's output line by line and match it to the ACME flow: account registration, the order, "Trying to solve HTTP-01", "The server validated our request", then the finalisation and download. Run docker logs ca to see the same requests from the CA's side.

4. **Inspect the result.**

    ```bash
    openssl x509 -in lab/lego/certificates/app.lab.test.crt \
      -noout -subject -issuer -dates -ext subjectAltName
    ```

    Note the validity: step-ca issues 24-hour certificates by default. Ask yourself what your current renewal process would do with a 24-hour certificate, then remember that the client you just ran needs no changes at all.

5. **Renew, and confirm the key changed.** Record the public key hash, renew, and compare.

    ```bash
    crt=lab/lego/certificates/app.lab.test.crt
    openssl x509 -in $crt -noout -pubkey | sha256sum
    docker run --rm --network cert-lab \
      --network-alias app.lab.test \
      -v "$PWD/lab:/data" \
      -e LEGO_CA_CERTIFICATES=/data/root_ca.crt \
      goacme/lego \
      --server https://ca:9000/acme/acme/directory \
      --email ops@lab.test --accept-tos \
      --domains app.lab.test --http --path /data/lego \
      renew --days 30
    openssl x509 -in $crt -noout -pubkey | sha256sum
    ```

    The hashes differ: lego generated a new key, which is the behaviour Section 17.12 recommends. (lego has a --reuse-key option; find it and decide when, if ever, you would allow it.)

6. **Issue a second certificate with Certbot.** Certbot is written in Python and trusts the bundle named in REQUESTS_CA_BUNDLE.

    ```bash
    docker run --rm --network cert-lab \
      --network-alias app2.lab.test \
      -v "$PWD/lab:/data" \
      -e REQUESTS_CA_BUNDLE=/data/root_ca.crt \
      certbot/certbot certonly --standalone \
      --server https://ca:9000/acme/acme/directory \
      -d app2.lab.test --agree-tos -m ops@lab.test \
      --no-eff-email --config-dir /data/certbot \
      --work-dir /data/certbot/work \
      --logs-dir /data/certbot/logs
    ```

    Two different clients, one CA, one protocol. This is the portability that lets a CLM programme change CA without touching every server.

7. **Cause a validation failure.** Repeat step 3 for the name missing.lab.test, but leave the --network-alias as app.lab.test. The CA cannot resolve the new name, the challenge fails and no certificate is issued. Read the error: this is what a DCV failure looks like from the client, and it is the first thing to check when renewals fail in production.

8. **Clean up.**

    ```bash
    docker rm -f ca
    docker network rm cert-lab
    ```

**Optional cloud extension.** If you own a test domain hosted on a cloud DNS service, repeat step 3 against the **Let's Encrypt staging** directory using DNS-01 (lego's --dns option with your provider's plug-in). Then create a CNAME from `_acme-challenge` for one name to a record in a second zone and use lego's or acme.sh's alias mode, so the credential you give the client can write only the delegated zone. Use the staging environment to avoid production rate limits. **Cost warning:** domain registration and hosted DNS zones are billed by your registrar and cloud provider, and API credentials left in a lab can be abused; delete the zone and revoke the credentials when you finish.

## 17.14 Failure story: the certificate that switched off a security tool

In 2017, attackers stole personal data on about 147 million people from Equifax, a US credit bureau. They entered through an unpatched web application in March and remained undetected for 76 days. They went unnoticed partly because a network monitoring device that inspected encrypted traffic leaving the affected systems had an **expired certificate**, and it had been expired for 19 months. With the certificate expired, the device could not decrypt the traffic, so it silently stopped inspecting it. No user-facing service broke, so nobody noticed.

On 29 July 2017, staff renewed the certificate. The device almost immediately showed suspicious traffic, and the investigation began. A US House of Representatives committee report later found that the company had allowed more than 300 security certificates to expire, including 79 used for monitoring business-critical domains, and concluded that a certificate management process with defined roles and responsibilities would have kept the monitoring device working.

Map the failure onto the lifecycle in Section 17.3 and almost every stage is missing. There was no reliable inventory that tied the certificate to the security function it enabled. There was no accountable owner receiving escalating alerts. Monitoring watched the services, not the certificates, so an expiry that caused no outage raised no alarm. And there was no renewal loop at all.

Two lessons stand out for a CLM design. First, inventory must record what each certificate protects, not only where it is installed; a certificate on a decryption or monitoring appliance deserves the same criticality as one on the customer website, because when it expires, protection fails silently. Second, measure expired certificates still in use, not only outages. The dangerous expiries are the ones nobody complains about.

## 17.15 Chapter summary

- CA/Browser Forum Ballot SC-081v3 cuts public TLS lifetimes to 200 days (March 2026), 100 days (March 2027) and 47 days (March 2029), and domain validation reuse to 10 days by 2029, so both issuance and domain validation must be automated.
- Distrust events and mass revocations can force any certificate to be replaced at short notice; the real defence is the ability to reissue on demand from more than one CA.
- The lifecycle runs from discovery to retirement; it is only automated when renewal happens without people.
- Discovery needs several methods: network scans with distributed scanners, CT monitoring, CA imports, cloud and Kubernetes APIs, and agents. Inventory must separate certificates, keys and installations and link them to applications, owners, environments and policy.
- Policy, held as data, sets templates, approved CAs, key types, SAN rules, validity and who may request what, enforced at request time and after discovery.
- DNS-01 with scoped credentials or CNAME delegation, and automated domain pre-validation, make public validation scale; CAA records restrict who can issue.
- ACME is the default for TLS, with EAB for commercial accounts and ARI for CA-guided renewal; EST and SCEP serve devices; CMP serves telecom and industrial systems; CA REST APIs belong behind a CLM platform's connectors.
- CLM platforms share an architecture of central service, CA connectors, agents or agentless delivery, satellites for segmented networks, RBAC and workflow, HSM-protected secrets and high availability; compare products on those capabilities and test them against your hardest targets.
- Generate keys on the target by default, centrally only when a target or escrow requires it, and in an HSM for the highest-value keys; always use a new key at renewal.
