## 29.1 Why protection has to follow the data

Chapters 26 and 27 placed encryption on a stack of layers: device, volume, file system, database and application. Every one of those layers protects data **in a place**. Once an authorised user opens a spreadsheet of customer complaints and emails it to a supplier, or saves it to a USB stick, or uploads it to a personal cloud drive, none of those controls applies any more. The copy is plaintext and the organisation has lost all say over it.

**Data-centric protection** reverses the logic. The file itself is encrypted, and the key needed to open it is released only to people and devices that a policy allows, each time they open it. The policy travels with the file as metadata, and the decision is made by a service the organisation controls. If the supplier relationship ends, the organisation changes the policy or revokes the document, and the copy in the supplier's inbox stops opening.

The same idea solves a second problem: deletion you cannot reach. If every copy of a record is encrypted under one key, destroying that key deletes every copy at once, including copies in backups you cannot edit. This is **crypto-shredding**, the subject of the second half of this chapter.

Data-centric protection does not replace the layers below it. It is expensive to operate, breaks search and data loss prevention scanning unless those tools are given keys, and relies on data being labelled correctly. Use it for documents that genuinely leave your boundary.

## 29.2 File and document encryption

The simplest data-centric control is to encrypt a file with a password or a public key before sending it. Three families are common.

**Format-native encryption** is built into the file format. Office documents saved with "Encrypt with Password" use the ECMA-376 agile encryption scheme: the password is stretched with a salted, iterated hash, and the content is encrypted with AES. PDF supports AES-256, and ZIP archives can use AES, although the broken legacy ZipCrypto scheme is still offered by some tools. All of these are only as strong as the password, which usually travels in the same email as the file.

**General-purpose file encryption tools** encrypt any file to one or more recipients' public keys. **OpenPGP** (GnuPG and compatible tools), updated in 2024 with modern algorithms, is the long-standing standard. **age**, a small modern tool with X25519 recipients and a simple format, is increasingly used by engineering teams for secrets and backups. Both use envelope encryption (Chapter 27): a random file key encrypts the content, and that file key is wrapped once for each recipient.

**Operating system file encryption**, such as the Windows Encrypting File System (EFS), encrypts files to a user's certificate, but a copied file is decrypted on the way out, so it is really a location control.

The weakness of all three is that protection is static. Once a recipient has decrypted the file they hold plaintext, with no revocation, audit or expiry, and key management is left to users. Rights management fixes this by putting a service in the loop.

## 29.3 Sensitivity labels and rights management

**Rights management**, also called **information rights management (IRM)** or **digital rights management for documents**, combines three things: encryption of the document, a policy that states who may do what with it, and an online service that checks the policy and releases the key. A **sensitivity label** is the user-facing handle: a named classification such as "Confidential: Customers" that a person or an automatic classifier applies, and that can trigger encryption, visual markings and data loss prevention rules.

### How Microsoft Purview does it

Microsoft Purview Information Protection is the most widely deployed example, because it is built into Microsoft 365. Its encryption is provided by the **Azure Rights Management service (Azure RMS)**. The flow, simplified from Microsoft's public documentation, is:

1. When a user applies a label configured for encryption, the Office client generates a random **content key** and encrypts the document with AES.
2. The client builds a **publishing licence** containing the label's policy (which users or groups get which **usage rights**, such as view, edit, print, copy or export) and the content key. The publishing licence is protected with the organisation's **tenant key**, so only the rights management service can read it, and it is embedded in the document.
3. When anyone opens the document, their client sends the publishing licence and the user's identity to the service. The service unwraps it, checks the policy against the user's Entra ID identity, and returns a **use licence** containing the content key, wrapped for that user, and the rights they hold.
4. The client enforces the rights, for example by disabling printing. Licences expire, so a revoked document stops opening once the cached licence ends.

Usage rights are enforced by the client, not by cryptography: anyone who can view a document can photograph the screen. Encryption stops people with no rights; usage rights only discourage misuse by people with some.

The tenant key sits at the top of the hierarchy. By default Microsoft generates and manages it. Under **bring your own key (BYOK)**, the organisation generates it in its own HSM and imports it into Azure Key Vault (Chapter 28). With **Double Key Encryption (DKE)**, content is encrypted under two keys, one held by Microsoft and one held in a key service the customer runs, so Microsoft alone cannot decrypt it. DKE breaks cloud features that read content, such as search and eDiscovery, so it suits only a small fraction of highly sensitive data.

> **Watch out.** Rights management has a **super user** feature that lets designated accounts or services decrypt any protected content, which eDiscovery, malware scanning and data loss prevention need. Keep super users to named service accounts, enable the feature only when needed where possible, and alert on its use. An attacker who becomes a super user can read everything the organisation has protected.

### Alternatives

| Product | Model | Key control | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Microsoft Purview Information Protection** | Labels plus Azure RMS; built into Microsoft 365 apps | Microsoft-managed, BYOK, or DKE | Deepest Office integration; external sharing needs recipients to authenticate |
| **Google Workspace client-side encryption (CSE)** | Files encrypted in the browser before upload to Drive, Docs and Gmail | Customer's external key service and identity provider | Google cannot decrypt; many collaboration features are reduced |
| **Virtru** and the **OpenTDF** project | Trusted Data Format wraps files and email with a policy and key access service | Customer-hosted or vendor-hosted key access servers | Works across email and files; open format |
| **Seclore**, **Fasoo** | Enterprise digital rights management agents for many file types, including CAD | On-premises or SaaS policy servers | Strong for engineering and partner exchange beyond Office |

As of 2026 all of them share one trade-off: the more the customer alone controls the key, the fewer cloud features work on the content.

## 29.4 Email encryption

Chapter 25 covered transport security for email (STARTTLS, MTA-STS and DANE) and S/MIME signatures. Transport encryption protects a message between servers, but every mail server, gateway and mailbox along the way holds plaintext. **Message-level encryption** protects the message itself. There are four approaches.

- **S/MIME** encrypts a message to each recipient's X.509 certificate. It works well inside an organisation whose PKI issues encryption certificates and escrows their keys (so a departed employee's mail stays readable), but between organisations it needs certificate discovery, which rarely works.
- **OpenPGP** does the same with OpenPGP keys, and is uncommon in business email outside security teams.
- **Rights-managed email**, such as Microsoft Purview Message Encryption, applies a policy ("Encrypt-only" or "Do Not Forward") to a message. Recipients outside Microsoft 365 authenticate with a one-time passcode to read it in a web portal.
- **Gateway and portal encryption**, from products such as Proofpoint Encryption, Cisco Secure Email Encryption Service, OpenText (Zix) and Echoworx, encrypts at the outbound gateway when a policy matches. Recipients receive either an encrypted attachment (**push**) or a link to a portal holding the message (**pull**).

| Recipient | Usual choice |
| --- | --- |
| Internal colleague | Rights-managed label, or S/MIME with escrowed keys |
| Regular partner with its own PKI or tenant | TLS enforced by MTA-STS or a partner-specific TLS rule, plus rights management for the most sensitive items |
| Consumer or occasional external recipient | Portal (pull) encryption, which allows expiry and revocation |

Whatever you choose, keep email keys **recoverable by the organisation**, because retention and eDiscovery obligations do not disappear when a mailbox is encrypted.

> **At Meridian.** The IAM team owns the label taxonomy: Public, Internal, Confidential and Restricted. The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the rights management service's tenant key (imported from its own HSMs) and a small customer-held second-key service used only by Restricted labels in Meridian Pay and the board office. Letters to consumers labelled Restricted go out through the email gateway's portal encryption. GRC reviews the super-user log monthly.

## 29.5 Data lifecycle and retention

Two forces pull on the data lifecycle in opposite directions. **Retention obligations** require keeping records for a minimum period: banks keep transaction and anti-money-laundering records for years after a relationship ends, and broker-dealer rules require some records in non-rewriteable form. **Legal holds** freeze data relevant to litigation. **Erasure obligations** pull the other way: GDPR's storage limitation principle (Article 5) and right to erasure (Article 17) mean you must be able to delete one person's data when the lawful basis ends, unless an exception such as a legal duty to retain applies.

Microsoft Purview **retention labels** and Google Vault apply retention and holds to collaboration content. Object storage offers **write once, read many (WORM)** controls: Amazon S3 Object Lock (governance and compliance modes), Azure Blob immutable storage and Google Cloud Storage bucket lock. In compliance mode, nobody, including the root account, can delete an object before its retention date.

Immutability is exactly what you want against ransomware, and exactly what stops you deleting a person's data from a backup when the law requires it. Crypto-shredding reconciles the two.

## 29.6 Crypto-shredding

**Crypto-shredding** (also called **cryptographic erasure** when applied to media) deletes data by destroying the keys that encrypt it. If the ciphertext is strong and every copy of the key is gone, the ciphertext is computationally indistinguishable from random noise, wherever copies of it remain. NIST SP 800-88 recognises cryptographic erase as a valid sanitisation technique for storage media (Chapter 26); crypto-shredding applies the same principle at the level of records.

### Designing key granularity

Crypto-shredding is only as precise as its keys: to erase one customer, you need keys that map to one customer.

- **Per-subject keys.** Each data subject (a customer, a policyholder) has a **data encryption key (DEK)**, wrapped by a **key encryption key (KEK)** in a KMS or HSM. Every field or document containing that person's personal data is encrypted under their DEK. Erasure means destroying the wrapped DEK and every copy of it.
- **Per-period keys.** Records sharing a retention end date, such as all closed accounts from 2019, share a key. When the retention period ends, destroying one key disposes of millions of records in every backup at once.
- **Combined.** Large designs use both, for erasure requests and scheduled disposal respectively.

Wrapped DEKs live in a small **key table** in a database that is *not* captured in the long-term immutable backups, or whose backups expire on a short cycle. This is the critical design point. If the key table is in the same ten-year immutable backup as the data, restoring that backup restores the keys and nothing has been erased. The design separates a large, long-lived, immutable store of ciphertext from a small, short-lived, deletable store of keys.

### What crypto-shredding does not erase

Erasure fails wherever plaintext or keys escaped the scheme:

- **Plaintext copies**: search indexes, analytics extracts, logs, data lake copies and test environments built from production.
- **Key copies**: cached or exported DEKs and key table backups that outlive their window.
- **Derived data**: model features and aggregates that still identify a person.
- **Weak cryptography**: ciphertext kept for decades must survive future attacks. Use AES-256 for long-lived archives, because that ciphertext is exposed to "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks for its whole retention period (Chapter 45).

Crypto-shredding is therefore a data-mapping exercise as much as a cryptographic one; the GDPR Article 30 record of processing is a good starting point for finding every copy.

### KMS deletion behaviour

Cloud and on-premises key managers deliberately make deletion slow, to protect against mistakes and attackers. As of 2026, AWS KMS requires a waiting period of 7 to 30 days after scheduling key deletion; Google Cloud KMS keeps destroyed key versions in a scheduled-for-destruction state for a configurable period (30 days by default); and Azure Key Vault soft-delete retains deleted keys for 7 to 90 days, with purge protection preventing early purge. HSMs keep keys in backups and in other cluster members. The erasure date you report is the date the last copy is gone, not the date someone pressed delete.

This is why per-subject DEKs are usually wrapped rows in a database, not individual KMS keys: deleting a row is immediate and cheap.

## 29.7 Proving deletion

Regulators and auditors will ask how you know data was erased. A defensible answer has four parts.

1. **A documented design**: key hierarchy, granularity, where wrapped keys live and their backup cycle, plus a data map showing every copy of personal data is under a subject key or excluded with a reason.
2. **An erasure workflow**: request, identity check, legal check for retention exceptions and holds, then deletion of the wrapped DEK and any plaintext copies, recorded in a ticket.
3. **Machine evidence**: audit log entries for the key deletion (AWS CloudTrail, Azure Monitor, Google Cloud Audit Logs or HSM logs), the date the last key backup expired, and a test showing decryption fails.
4. **Periodic assurance**: internal audit samples erased subjects and tries to restore their data from backups.

The completion date you give the data subject should account for backup expiry and KMS waiting periods; state it plainly, and make sure erased data is never restored into live use in the meantime.

> **Compliance hook.** Crypto-shredding supports GDPR Article 17 (erasure), Article 5(1)(e) (storage limitation) and Article 25 (data protection by design). PCI DSS v4.0.1 Requirement 3.2.1 requires that stored account data be kept to the minimum and securely deleted when no longer needed, and Requirement 3.7 covers key retirement and destruction. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 controls A.8.10 (information deletion) and A.8.24 (use of cryptography) apply, and NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 covers cryptographic erase.

> **At Meridian.** The customer data platform encrypts personal data fields under per-customer DEKs, wrapped by a KEK in the cloud KMS. The key table's backups expire after 35 days, while the records archive sits in object storage under compliance-mode WORM for the bank's record retention period. When an erasure request clears the GRC legal check, the application deletes the wrapped DEK and logs the event to the SIEM, where an evidence dashboard tracks it until the last key-table backup expires. Anti-money-laundering records use a separate per-year key and are excluded from erasure, with the reason recorded.

## 29.8 Design decisions and operations

- **Label first, encrypt second.** A taxonomy users understand, applied by default and by automatic classification, matters more than the cipher. Encrypt only labels that justify the friction.
- **Plan for key availability.** If the rights management or DKE key service is down, nobody opens protected files. Run it as resiliently as identity.
- **Keep a recovery path.** Business documents need an organisational recovery route (super user, escrow or an organisation-held key).
- **Monitor.** Alert on super-user activity, mass label removals, bulk decryption and unexpected key deletion requests, which attackers use to cause damage.

## 29.9 Lab: crypto-shredding with Vault transit

This lab uses HashiCorp Vault in dev mode (free, local, in-memory; never for real data) to show per-subject keys and erasure evidence.

1. Start Vault in dev mode in one terminal, then in a second terminal point the CLI at it and enable an audit log and the transit engine:

    ```bash
    vault server -dev -dev-root-token-id=root
    export VAULT_ADDR=http://127.0.0.1:8200 VAULT_TOKEN=root
    vault audit enable file file_path=/tmp/vault-audit.log
    vault secrets enable transit
    ```

2. Create a key for each of two customers and encrypt a record for each. Save the ciphertexts; these stand in for your immutable archive:

    ```bash
    for c in 1001 1002; do
      vault write -f transit/keys/cust-$c
      vault write -field=ciphertext transit/encrypt/cust-$c \
        plaintext=$(echo -n "customer $c: IBAN and address" \
        | base64) > rec-$c.txt
    done
    cat rec-1001.txt
    ```

3. Decrypt customer 1001's record to confirm it works:

    ```bash
    vault write -field=plaintext transit/decrypt/cust-1001 \
      ciphertext=$(cat rec-1001.txt) | base64 -d; echo
    ```

4. Process an erasure request for customer 1001. Transit keys cannot be deleted until deletion is explicitly allowed, a deliberate safety catch:

    ```bash
    vault write transit/keys/cust-1001/config deletion_allowed=true
    vault delete transit/keys/cust-1001
    ```

5. Try to decrypt both records again. Customer 1001's ciphertext is still on disk but can no longer be decrypted; customer 1002's is unaffected.

6. Find the evidence in the audit log. Note that Vault hashes sensitive values in the audit log by default:

    ```bash
    grep '"path":"transit/keys/cust-1001"' /tmp/vault-audit.log \
      | head -n 2
    ```

7. Reflect: list every place a copy of the key could survive in a real deployment (Vault snapshots, exported keys, a replicated cluster) and how long each lives.

**Optional cloud extension.** In an AWS, Azure or Google Cloud free-tier account, create a customer-managed key, encrypt a small object with it, schedule the key for deletion with the shortest permitted waiting period, and find the deletion events in the provider's audit log. *Cost warning:* a customer-managed key incurs a monthly charge while it exists, including during the waiting period; delete the key and the bucket when you finish.

## 29.10 Failure story: the archive that could not forget

In October 2019 the Berlin data protection authority fined the property company Deutsche Wohnen 14.5 million euros. Inspectors had found in 2017 that tenants' personal data, including salary statements, bank statements and tax and health insurance records, was kept in an archive system that offered no way to delete data that was no longer needed. A follow-up check in 2019 found the problem still unfixed. The authority cited Article 25 (data protection by design) and Article 5 (the principles, including storage limitation).

The case went through the German courts and the Court of Justice of the European Union, which ruled in December 2023 (case C-807/21) that companies can be fined directly but only where an infringement was intentional or negligent. In June 2026 the Berlin Regional Court found the company liable in principle and reduced the fine to 900,000 euros, a judgment not yet final at the time of writing.

The engineering failure is simple: a long-term store was designed for keeping data, with no deletion path, and retrofitting one is slow and expensive. The lesson: **design the erasure path when you design the store**. Per-subject or per-period keys, with the key table on a short backup cycle, give even immutable archives a way to forget.

## 29.11 Chapter summary

- Data-centric protection encrypts the file and releases keys by policy on every open, so protection survives copying.
- Password and public-key file encryption is static: no revocation, audit or expiry.
- Rights management services combine labels, encryption and a key-releasing policy service; usage rights are enforced by clients, not cryptography.
- BYOK, DKE and external key services increase customer control at the cost of cloud features.
- Choose email encryption by recipient, and keep keys recoverable for retention.
- Retention and WORM storage conflict with erasure duties; crypto-shredding reconciles them.
- Crypto-shredding precision depends on key granularity, and the key store must have a much shorter backup cycle than the data.
- Proving deletion needs a documented design, a workflow, machine evidence and periodic restore tests.
