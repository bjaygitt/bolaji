## 11.1 Why definitions matter

For most of history, ciphers were judged by intuition: a design looked complicated, so people assumed it was strong. Many were broken. Modern cryptography, from the 1980s onward, insisted on two things before calling anything secure:

1. **A precise definition** of what "secure" means: what the attacker can do, and what counts as a win.
2. **Evidence** that no efficient attacker can win, usually a **proof** that winning would require solving a problem believed to be hard.

You will not write proofs as an architect. But you will constantly read claims like "IND-CCA2 secure", "EUF-CMA secure", "provably secure in the random oracle model" or "military-grade encryption" in papers, standards, vendor datasheets and procurement responses. Knowing what each promises, and what each leaves out, lets you ask the questions that separate a sound product from a risky one.

## 11.2 Security as a game

Cryptographers define security as a **game** between a **challenger**, who holds the secret key, and an **adversary**, who is any efficient algorithm. The game spells out exactly which questions the adversary may ask and what it must achieve to win. A scheme is secure if every efficient adversary wins with probability only negligibly better than guessing.

This framing does not list specific attacks. It covers **every** attack, including ones nobody has invented yet, as long as the attacker fits the model. That last clause is where most real failures hide.

## 11.3 Definitions for encryption

### IND-CPA: indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack

The game:

1. The adversary may ask for the encryption of any messages it likes.
2. It then picks two messages, m0 and m1, of equal length.
3. The challenger secretly flips a coin and encrypts one of them.
4. The adversary may ask for more encryptions, then guesses which message was encrypted.

The scheme is **IND-CPA secure** if no efficient adversary guesses correctly noticeably more than half the time. In plain words: **ciphertexts reveal nothing about which message was encrypted, even to an attacker who can encrypt messages of their choice.**

One immediate consequence: deterministic encryption can never be IND-CPA secure, because the adversary simply encrypts m0 itself and compares. This is the formal reason textbook RSA and ECB mode fail (Chapters 6 and 8), and why encryption needs IVs, nonces or randomness. The lab in section 11.11 lets you win this game against ECB.

### IND-CCA2: adding chosen-ciphertext attacks

**IND-CCA2** (often just "IND-CCA") adds a decryption oracle: the adversary may also ask for the decryption of any ciphertext except the challenge itself. This models attackers who submit modified ciphertexts and observe how the system reacts, which is exactly the padding oracle and Bleichenbacher attacks of Chapters 6 and 8. IND-CCA2 is the standard required for public-key encryption and key encapsulation in real protocols; ML-KEM is designed to meet it.

### Authenticated encryption: INT-CTXT

For AEAD (Chapter 6), confidentiality is paired with **integrity of ciphertexts (INT-CTXT)**: the adversary must not be able to produce any new ciphertext that decrypts successfully. A scheme that is IND-CPA and INT-CTXT secure is automatically IND-CCA secure, which is the formal reason Encrypt-then-MAC and AEAD work.

## 11.4 Definitions for signatures and MACs

**EUF-CMA** (existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack) is the standard for signatures and MACs:

1. The adversary may ask for signatures on any messages it chooses.
2. It wins if it outputs a valid signature on **any** message it did not ask about.

"Existential" means the forged message can be anything, even gibberish. This is deliberately strict: if an attacker can forge meaningless messages, a real attacker will find meaningful ones. A stronger variant, **SUF-CMA** (strong unforgeability), also forbids producing a new signature on a message already signed. Systems that use signatures as identifiers, such as some payment and ledger designs, need the stronger property.

## 11.5 Definitions for building blocks

| Primitive | Security notion | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Block cipher | PRP (pseudo-random permutation) | Indistinguishable from a random shuffle of blocks |
| MAC, PRF, KDF | PRF (pseudo-random function) | Outputs look random to anyone without the key |
| Hash function | Collision, preimage, second-preimage resistance | No efficient way to find these (Chapter 7) |
| Key exchange | Key indistinguishability | The agreed key looks random to an eavesdropper, even after other session keys leak |
| Random generator | Next-bit unpredictability | No efficient way to predict the next output bit |

## 11.6 Reductions: how proofs work

Security proofs are almost always **reductions**. A reduction says:

> If there were an efficient adversary that breaks scheme S, we could use it as a subroutine to build an efficient algorithm that solves hard problem P.

Since we believe P is hard, no such adversary should exist. For example, a proof might show that breaking a KEM implies solving the Module-LWE problem, or that forging HMAC implies breaking the underlying compression function as a PRF.

Three details of reductions matter in practice:

- **The assumption is the foundation.** A proof is only as good as the problem it reduces to. Proofs based on factoring or discrete logarithms become worthless against quantum computers, even though the proofs themselves are correct.
- **Tightness.** A reduction may lose a factor: "breaking S with probability ε implies solving P with probability ε / 2^40". A loose reduction means parameters must be larger than they look to keep the promised security.
- **The model.** The proof covers only attackers who behave as the game allows. An attacker who measures timing, injects faults, or exploits a parsing bug is outside the model.

### The random oracle model

Many efficient schemes, including RSA-OAEP, RSA-PSS, Ed25519 and parts of ML-KEM, are proven secure in the **random oracle model (ROM)**, which pretends that a hash function is a perfect random function. Real hash functions are not random oracles, and in 1998 researchers constructed (artificial) schemes that are secure in the ROM but insecure with any real hash function. In practice, no widely used natural scheme has been broken through that gap, so treat a ROM proof as strong evidence rather than a guarantee. Post-quantum schemes need proofs in the harder **quantum random oracle model (QROM)**, where attackers may query the hash in superposition.

## 11.7 How protocols fail

A **protocol** combines primitives into a sequence of messages: negotiate, authenticate, agree keys, protect data. Every primitive in TLS 1.2 had a respectable security argument, and TLS 1.2 still suffered a decade of serious attacks. The failures fall into recognisable families.

| Failure family | What goes wrong | Documented example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Downgrade | An attacker in the middle steers both sides to the weakest option they both still accept | FREAK and Logjam forced export-grade keys (Chapter 9) |
| Oracle | The system reveals, through errors or timing, whether a tampered ciphertext was valid | Bleichenbacher's 1998 RSA attack, reborn as ROBOT in 2017 |
| Cross-protocol | A key shared between an old protocol and a new one lets attacks on the old one reach the new one | DROWN used SSLv2 servers to decrypt TLS sessions |
| State machine | Implementations accept messages out of order or skip steps | The SMACK family of TLS state machine bugs |
| Composition | Two individually sound parts combine unsoundly | Triple handshake attack on TLS renegotiation and resumption |
| Model mismatch | The proof modelled a simplified protocol, not the real one | KRACK reinstalled WPA2 keys despite a proven handshake |
| Implementation | Correct design, broken code | Heartbleed leaked server memory, including private keys |
| Side channel | Time, power or cache behaviour leaks secrets | Lucky Thirteen (section 11.12) |

### Downgrade and legacy options

Negotiation is a weakness whenever weak options remain enabled. In 2015, the **FREAK** attack showed that a man in the middle could make some clients accept export-grade RSA keys that a server still supported, and Logjam did the same for DH. The root cause is that the negotiation itself was not protected well enough, and that nobody had removed options switched off decades earlier.

### Oracles never die

In 1998, Daniel Bleichenbacher showed that a server which reveals whether an RSA-encrypted message had valid PKCS#1 v1.5 padding lets an attacker decrypt data with many thousands of queries. TLS added countermeasures. In 2017, the **ROBOT** researchers found the same oracle still present in products from several major vendors, including load balancers and firewalls from F5, Citrix and Cisco. Fixes that rely on every implementer getting subtle error handling right tend not to last.

### Shared keys across protocols

The **DROWN** attack of 2016 showed that a server key also used by an SSLv2 service, even an old mail server nobody remembered, could be used to decrypt modern TLS sessions protected by the same key. The lesson for architects is about key reuse: one key in two protocols means the weaker protocol sets the security of both.

### State machines and composition

The 2015 "Messy State of the Union" research tested TLS libraries with unexpected message sequences and found clients that skipped authentication steps entirely when messages arrived out of order. The 2014 **triple handshake** attack combined session resumption and renegotiation, each fine alone, to impersonate a client; it led to the extended master secret fix in RFC 7627. In 2017, **KRACK** showed that the WPA2 four-way handshake, which had a security proof, could be tricked into reinstalling a key and reusing nonces, because the proof never modelled when the key was installed.

## 11.8 How good protocols are built now

Protocol designers learned from these failures, and TLS 1.3 shows the result:

- **Fewer options.** Only AEAD ciphers, only forward-secret key exchange, no renegotiation, no compression, no custom DH groups. Every removed option is a downgrade target that no longer exists.
- **Transcript binding.** Signatures and key derivation cover the whole handshake transcript, so tampering with negotiation breaks the handshake.
- **Downgrade sentinels.** A TLS 1.3 server that is forced to speak TLS 1.2 places a special value in its random field, which a 1.3 client detects.
- **Formal analysis before standardisation.** TLS 1.3 drafts were analysed with automated tools such as Tamarin and ProVerif, which found real problems before the standard was finished.
- **Verified implementations.** Projects such as HACL* produce formally verified cryptographic code, now shipped in mainstream browsers and libraries.

The enterprise lesson is direct: prefer standard, widely analysed protocols in their current versions, remove legacy options aggressively, and treat any proprietary protocol as unproven until shown otherwise.

## 11.9 Reading a security claim

When a vendor, paper or colleague says something is secure, ask:

- **Secure in what sense?** Which definition: IND-CPA, IND-CCA2, EUF-CMA, something else? Is it the right one for the use?
- **Under what assumption?** Factoring, discrete log, lattices, an idealised hash? Does it survive quantum attackers?
- **Proved, or argued?** Is there a published proof, ideally peer-reviewed or machine-checked?
- **Which protocol, which version?** A standard protocol in its current version, or a custom one? Which legacy options remain enabled?
- **What is outside the model?** Side channels, faults, misuse, key management.
- **What parameters?** Do the chosen key sizes give the claimed security?
- **Who has attacked it?** Years of public cryptanalysis count for more than any marketing claim.

Phrases such as "military-grade" or "bank-level encryption" fail almost every question on this list: they name a key length and nothing else.

> **At Meridian.** A vendor proposed a branch-office sensor that sends data to its cloud over a "proprietary AES-256 protocol". The PKI and Cryptography Services team ran the checklist with Network Engineering. The answers: a custom handshake with no published analysis, unauthenticated key exchange, and one device key shared across every unit. The team required standard TLS 1.3 with per-device certificates from Meridian's device CA (Chapter 15) as a condition of purchase, and GRC added "uses standard, current protocols" to the third-party security questionnaire.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 12.3.3 asks firms to document the cryptographic cipher suites and protocols in use and review them at least every 12 months, which is the natural place to record removed legacy options. NIST SP 800-131A Rev. 2 sets the transition rules for disallowed algorithms and key sizes. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control A.8.24 expects rules on cryptography, and control A.5.21 (ICT supply chain) is where vendor claims are assessed.

## 11.10 Where proven security still breaks

Proofs are about mathematical models. Real systems break outside the model:

- **Side channels.** Timing leaks of a few nonce bits broke ECDSA in smart cards (Chapter 10).
- **Misuse.** Nonce reuse destroys GCM's proven security (Chapter 6).
- **Wrong definition.** A scheme proven IND-CPA used where IND-CCA was needed.
- **Broken assumption.** Quantum computers break the problems behind RSA and ECC proofs (Chapter 44).
- **Operations.** A perfect protocol with an expired certificate, a leaked key or a disabled check protects nothing.

This is why mature engineering combines proofs with public scrutiny, formal verification, careful implementation, conservative parameters and disciplined operations.

## 11.11 Lab: win and lose the IND-CPA game, then hunt for legacy options

You need Python 3 with the `cryptography` package, OpenSSL 3.x and Docker.

**Step 1. Play the IND-CPA game against ECB and GCM.** The adversary encrypts m0 itself and compares.

```python
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers import (
    Cipher, algorithms, modes)
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import AESGCM
key = os.urandom(16)
def ecb(m):
    e = Cipher(algorithms.AES(key), modes.ECB()).encryptor()
    return e.update(m) + e.finalize()
def gcm(m):
    n = os.urandom(12)
    return n + AESGCM(key).encrypt(n, m, None)
def play(enc, rounds=1000):
    wins = 0
    for _ in range(rounds):
        m0, m1 = b"A" * 16, b"B" * 16
        b = os.urandom(1)[0] & 1
        c = enc([m0, m1][b])
        guess = 0 if enc(m0) == c else 1
        wins += guess == b
    return wins / rounds
print("ECB win rate:", play(ecb))   # 1.0: total break
print("GCM win rate:", play(gcm))   # about 0.5: guessing
```

**Step 2. Start a local TLS server that allows a legacy version.**

```bash
  -out l.crt -subj "/CN=localhost" -days 7
openssl s_server -accept 8443 -key l.key -cert l.crt \
  -tls1_2 -cipher "AES128-SHA" -www &
```

## 11.12 Failure story: when the proof assumed away the attack

TLS 1.2 and earlier used **MAC-then-encrypt**: compute a MAC over the plaintext, append it, add padding, then encrypt with CBC. Analyses in the early 2000s gave this composition security arguments under certain assumptions. Implementations, however, had to check padding and MAC in some order, and the time taken differed depending on where the check failed.

In 2013, Nadhem AlFardan and Kenny Paterson published **Lucky Thirteen**, a timing attack that recovered plaintext from TLS connections by measuring differences of about a microsecond in how long servers took to reject malformed records. The difference came from how many hash compression blocks were processed, which depended on the padding length. Every major TLS library was affected and had to be patched with painstaking constant-time code.

The mathematics had not been wrong. The model had not included time. TLS 1.3 removed the problem at its root by allowing only AEAD ciphers, whose security definitions cover tampering directly. For an enterprise, the practical lesson is that CBC suites lingering in an appliance's cipher list are not harmless leftovers: they are exactly the options attackers look for.

## 11.13 Chapter summary

- Modern cryptography defines security as a game against any efficient adversary, then proves no such adversary can win, assuming a hard problem.
- IND-CPA means ciphertexts reveal nothing; IND-CCA2 adds resistance to tampering and observing; EUF-CMA means no forgeries, even of meaningless messages.
- Proofs are reductions. Check the assumption, the tightness and the model; random oracle proofs are strong evidence, not guarantees.
- Protocols fail in recognisable families: downgrade, oracle, cross-protocol, state machine, composition, model mismatch, implementation and side channel.
- Shared keys across protocols let the weakest protocol set the security of all of them.
- Modern protocols remove options, bind the transcript, detect downgrades and are formally analysed; enterprises should follow the same instinct and remove legacy options.
- Ask precise questions of every security claim, and treat proprietary protocols as unproven.
