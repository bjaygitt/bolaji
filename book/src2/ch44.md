## 44.1 Why it matters

Most of this book rests on two public-key ideas. Key exchange lets two parties who have never met agree a secret over a hostile network. Digital signatures let anyone check who produced a message, a certificate or a piece of software. TLS, SSH, IPsec, OAuth tokens, code signing, the Web PKI and EMV chip authentication all depend on one or both. A large enough quantum computer breaks both at once. It does not erode them slowly, as faster classical hardware does; it removes the mathematical problem that makes them work.

Three facts make this a present-day concern:

- Adversaries can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it when the machine arrives.
- Replacing public-key cryptography in a large organisation is a programme measured in years, because much of it sits inside products, partners and devices you do not control.
- Governments and financial regulators have already set dates, and auditors have started asking for plans.

This chapter is the "why" for Part XI. Chapter 45 explains the replacement algorithms, and Chapter 46 turns both into a migration programme.

## 44.2 What a quantum computer is, briefly

A classical computer stores **bits**, each either 0 or 1. A quantum computer uses **qubits**. A qubit can be in a **superposition**, a weighted combination of 0 and 1 described by numbers called amplitudes. Several qubits can be **entangled**, meaning their states are linked so that the system has to be described as a whole rather than qubit by qubit. When you **measure** qubits you get ordinary 0s and 1s, with probabilities set by the amplitudes, and the superposition is gone.

A quantum algorithm is a recipe that arranges amplitudes so that, through **interference**, the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and the amplitudes of right answers reinforce. When you measure at the end, you are likely to see a right answer.

A quantum computer is **not** a faster classical computer that "tries every answer at once". Measurement returns one outcome, so the art is in the interference, and for most problems there is no useful speed-up. Quantum computers help with problems that have special mathematical structure. Unfortunately, the problems behind today's public-key cryptography have exactly that structure.

Real **physical qubits** are disturbed by heat, vibration and stray fields, so they make errors constantly. Long computations need **quantum error correction**, which spreads one reliable **logical qubit** across hundreds or thousands of physical qubits and continuously fixes errors. A machine able to break deployed cryptography is called a **cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC)**.

## 44.3 Shor's algorithm: the end of RSA and ECC

In 1994, Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could efficiently find the **period** of a function, the interval after which its outputs repeat. Several hard problems reduce to period finding:

- **Factoring.** The powers of a random number a modulo N repeat with some period r, and knowing r lets you compute the factors of N with ordinary arithmetic. That breaks RSA (Chapter 8).
- **Discrete logarithms.** Finding x from g^x mod p is also a period-finding problem in disguise. That breaks finite-field Diffie-Hellman and DSA.
- **Elliptic curve discrete logarithms.** The same idea works over the group of points on an elliptic curve (Chapter 9), breaking ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA.

A CRQC running **Shor's algorithm** could therefore recover a private key from its public key for every widely deployed public-key algorithm. Larger keys do not help: the quantum work grows only polynomially (roughly with the cube of the key length), so doubling an RSA key buys a small constant factor rather than safety.

The consequence is blunt: **every public-key algorithm taught in Parts II to IX, as deployed today, falls to Shor's algorithm.** That includes the key exchange in TLS, SSH, IPsec and messaging, the signatures on certificates, software, tokens and documents, RSA and ECC key wrapping in HSMs and KMS, and the RSA certificates used in EMV chip cards and remote key loading for payment terminals.

## 44.4 Grover's algorithm: a smaller threat to symmetric cryptography

**Grover's algorithm** (1996) speeds up unstructured search. Finding one key among 2^n possibilities takes about 2^(n/2) quantum steps instead of about 2^n classical guesses. On paper this halves the security level of symmetric keys and hash preimages: AES-128 drops to 64-bit security, which would be uncomfortable.

In practice the threat is much smaller, for two reasons:

- Grover's steps must run **one after another** on an error-corrected machine. Classical key searches split neatly across a million machines; Grover's algorithm does not parallelise well, because splitting the search space across k machines only gives a square-root-of-k improvement.
- Each step evaluates AES as a reversible quantum circuit, with full error correction. A single step is enormously expensive compared with one AES operation on a classical chip.

NIST's guidance reflects this: AES-128 remains acceptable, and AES-256 is comfortably secure against any foreseeable quantum attack. SHA-256, SHA-384 and SHA-3 remain secure for their uses. The response to Grover is simply to prefer 256-bit symmetric keys for data that must stay confidential for decades.

| Cryptography | Quantum attack | Effect | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RSA, DH, ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA | Shor | Completely broken | Replace with ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA (Chapter 45) |
| AES-128 | Grover | Weakened on paper, practically safe | Prefer AES-256 for long-lived data |
| AES-256 | Grover | Safe | None needed |
| SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-3 | Grover | Safe for practical purposes | None needed |
| HMAC, HKDF, CMAC | Grover | Safe | None needed |
| Triple DES (still in payments) | Grover and classical attacks | Already deprecated for other reasons | Move to AES (Chapter 38) |

This asymmetry matters for planning. Most bulk data in an enterprise is already encrypted with AES. What needs replacing is the public-key machinery that **agrees, transports or wraps** the AES keys, and the signatures that establish trust.

## 44.5 How close are we?

No one knows when a CRQC will exist. Three trends inform the estimate.

**Hardware is improving.** Companies and national programmes are building machines with more and better qubits. In December 2024, Google demonstrated error correction improving as the logical qubit grew, a key milestone toward practical fault tolerance: below a threshold error rate, adding more physical qubits made the logical qubit better rather than worse. Several companies have published roadmaps targeting fault-tolerant machines around the end of this decade.

**Resource estimates are falling.** In 2019, the best estimate for factoring 2048-bit RSA was about 20 million noisy qubits running for eight hours. In 2025, a revised analysis by Craig Gidney at Google estimated that fewer than one million noisy qubits could do it in under a week. Better algorithms and error-correction codes, not just bigger hardware, are shrinking the gap: the target is moving towards the hardware as the hardware moves towards the target.

**Expert opinion is uncertain but not reassuring.** Surveys of quantum researchers, such as those published annually by the Global Risk Institute, put a meaningful probability on a CRQC within about 10 to 15 years, with wide disagreement between respondents. Europol's Quantum Safe Financial Forum used the same 10-to-15-year window when it urged banks to act in 2025.

The honest summary is this: a CRQC is not imminent, its arrival date is uncertain, and that uncertainty is itself the risk, because migration takes years and some data must stay secret for decades.

> **Watch out.** Announcements that a quantum computer "broke RSA" usually involve tiny numbers or tricks that do not scale. Plan on published government dates, not on news cycles.

## 44.6 Mosca's inequality

Michele Mosca proposed a simple way to decide whether you are already late. Define three durations:

- **X:** how many years the data must stay confidential, its **shelf life**.
- **Y:** how many years it will take to migrate the systems that protect it to quantum-safe cryptography.
- **Z:** how many years until a CRQC exists.

> **In practice.** Mosca's inequality says: if X + Y is greater than Z, you have a problem. Some of your data will still need protection after attackers can read it.

A worked example: an insurer holds medical underwriting records that must stay confidential for 25 years (X = 25). Its realistic migration estimate, including vendors and partners, is 7 years (Y = 7). Even if a CRQC is 30 years away (Z = 30), X + Y = 32 is greater than Z, so records sent across today's networks with classical key exchange are already at risk.

Two points make the inequality sharper in practice:

- **Y is usually underestimated.** Large organisations typically estimate 5 to 15 years, because cryptography is embedded in so many products, protocols and partner links. The SHA-1 retirement in Section 44.12 took well over a decade.
- **X is per data set, not per system.** One API can carry payment data with a short shelf life alongside identity documents that matter for decades. Use the longest-lived data the system carries.

## 44.7 Harvest now, decrypt later, and the signature timeline

The most urgent threat is not a future attack on future traffic. It is **harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL)**: adversaries recording encrypted traffic and stealing encrypted data **today**, to decrypt once a CRQC exists. HNDL attacks **confidentiality**, through key exchange and public-key encryption. If a TLS session used ECDHE, a future CRQC can recover the ephemeral private key from the recorded key share, derive the session keys and read everything. Forward secrecy (Chapter 20) does not help here, because the attacker is not stealing a long-term key; it is solving the key exchange itself.

That is why the first post-quantum upgrades, deployed by browsers, messaging apps and SSH between 2023 and 2025, all protect key exchange: Signal's PQXDH, Apple's iMessage PQ3, hybrid X25519MLKEM768 in Chrome and other browsers, and the hybrid ML-KEM default in OpenSSH 10.

**Signatures** face a different timeline. A signature only needs to be unforgeable until it is verified, so a CRQC in 2035 cannot reach back and forge the server authentication in a TLS handshake from 2026. But signature keys that are still trusted when a CRQC arrives become dangerous immediately:

- **Root and issuing CA keys.** A CRQC that derives a root's private key from its public key can issue certificates for anything that trusts the root.
- **Firmware and code signing keys** whose public halves are burned into devices that will be in service for 10 to 20 years.
- **Long-term document signatures** that must remain valid evidence for decades.
- **Identity signing keys**, such as an identity provider's token signing key.

| Risk | Driven by | Urgency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recorded traffic decrypted later | Key exchange (ECDHE, RSA key transport) | Highest: affects data sent today |
| Stored data whose keys are wrapped with RSA or ECC decrypted | Public-key encryption and key wrapping | High for long-lived data |
| Forged signatures on new artefacts | Signature keys still trusted when a CRQC arrives | Must be complete before a CRQC; long lead times for roots and devices |
| Forged signatures on old artefacts | Long-term signatures without post-quantum timestamps | Specific to archives and legal records |

## 44.8 Where quantum risk lives in the enterprise

Chapter 2 counted more than 40 places where cryptography hides in a typical enterprise. Grouping the public-key ones by the risk in Section 44.7 is the first step towards a plan:

- **HNDL-exposed key exchange:** internet-facing TLS at the CDN and load balancers (Chapter 23), partner and open banking APIs (Chapter 39), site-to-site and remote-access VPNs (Chapter 22), SSH to production (Chapter 25), and links to card networks and payment partners.
- **Key wrapping and key transport:** RSA-wrapped data keys, key exchange between HSMs and with partners (including TR-34 in payments, Chapter 37), and BYOK imports into cloud KMS (Chapter 28).
- **Long-lived trust:** offline root CAs (Chapter 15), code and firmware signing (Chapter 36), TPM endorsement keys, smart cards and passkeys (Chapter 16), and EMV card authentication keys certified by card-network CAs.
- **Short-lived signatures:** TLS server certificates, mTLS workload certificates (Chapter 24) and OAuth tokens. These rotate often, so they can follow the ecosystem rather than lead it.
- **Symmetric-only systems:** DUKPT and PIN processing, AES disk and database encryption. These need little or no change beyond moving to AES-256 where data is long-lived.

> **At Meridian.** The PKI and Cryptography Services team ran a first-pass quantum exposure review in a two-week workshop with GRC and Payments Security. Card data turned out to have a short shelf life, because cards expire and are reissued every few years. The longest-lived secrets were insurance underwriting files and retail banking identity and mortgage records, which cross the internet edge, the open banking gateway and the inter-data-centre links. Payments Security flagged a separate signature concern: merchant payment terminals that verify firmware with RSA keys and stay in service for many years. Chapter 46 turns these findings into Meridian's migration plan.

## 44.9 Government and sector timelines

Governments have converted uncertainty into deadlines. Chapter 46 covers what each one requires of an enterprise in detail; the headline dates, as published by 2026, are these:

| Authority | Key dates |
| --- | --- |
| **US NIST (IR 8547, draft, 2024)** | Deprecate quantum-vulnerable algorithms at the 112-bit level after 2030; disallow RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, DH and ECDH entirely after 2035 |
| **US NSA (CNSA 2.0)** | Prefer quantum-resistant algorithms in software and firmware signing, browsers, servers and cloud services from 2025; new national security system acquisitions compliant from January 2027; exclusive use in most categories between 2030 and 2033; all national security systems by 2035 |
| **US federal policy** | National Security Memorandum 10 (2022) and the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act require agencies to inventory cryptography and plan migration, with 2035 as the overall goal |
| **UK NCSC (2025)** | By 2028: discovery and migration plan complete; by 2031: high-priority migrations complete; by 2035: migration complete |
| **European Union (coordinated roadmap, 2025)** | Begin transition by the end of 2026; protect high-risk use cases by the end of 2030; complete as far as feasible by 2035 |
| **G7 Cyber Expert Group (2026)** | Financial sector roadmap: prioritise critical systems within about 2030 to 2032; complete non-critical systems by about 2035 (non-binding) |
| **Others** | Australia's guidance targets ceasing traditional asymmetric cryptography by 2030; Canada, Germany, France, the Netherlands, Japan, Singapore and others have published plans with similar horizons |

> **Current development.** These dates converge on a common pattern: **inventory now, protect high-risk systems by about 2030, finish by 2035.** The G7 Cyber Expert Group's January 2026 roadmap gives banks a shared reference timeline. Treat 2030 as the practical deadline for anything important.

> **Compliance hook.** No framework yet tells a commercial bank to run ML-KEM by a given date. The obligations bite indirectly: PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 12.3.3 asks for a review of cipher suites and protocols in use at least every 12 months, including a plan to respond to anticipated changes in cryptographic vulnerabilities; DORA expects encryption policies to keep pace with the threat; ISO/IEC 27001 control A.8.24 asks for rules on the effective use of cryptography. A post-quantum risk assessment and roadmap is the evidence for all three.

## 44.10 What not to do

- **Do not wait for certainty** about the CRQC date. Migration time (Y) is the long pole, and you control it only by starting.
- **Do not buy unvetted "quantum-proof" products.** Use the NIST-standardised algorithms in Chapter 45, implemented in maintained, ideally validated, libraries and modules.
- **Do not rely on quantum key distribution (QKD) as a general solution.** QKD uses quantum physics to distribute keys over special optical links. It needs dedicated hardware, works point to point over limited distances, does not authenticate the endpoints on its own (so it still needs classical or post-quantum signatures), and is not recommended by the NSA, NCSC, ANSSI or BSI as a replacement for post-quantum cryptography in most uses.
- **Do not panic about symmetric cryptography.** Moving long-lived data to AES-256 and keeping SHA-256 or stronger is enough.

```bash

```

**3. Test a few public sites for hybrid key exchange.** The `-groups` option offers only the hybrid group, so the handshake fails if the server does not support it.

```bash
for h in www.google.com www.cloudflare.com example.com; do
  echo "== $h"
  openssl s_client -connect "$h:443" -servername "$h" \
    -groups X25519MLKEM768 -brief < /dev/null 2>&1 \
    | grep -E "Negotiated|group|error" | head -3
done
```

**4. Check your browser.** Visit the same sites and open the developer tools' Security panel (in Chromium-based browsers) to see the key exchange. Note which sites show X25519MLKEM768.

## 44.12 Failure story: the long tail of SHA-1

The retirement of SHA-1 is the best rehearsal we have for the post-quantum transition. Theoretical weaknesses were published in 2005. NIST deprecated SHA-1 for digital signatures in 2011. Browsers began warning about SHA-1 certificates in 2014 and stopped accepting them in early 2017, the same year the first real collision was produced.

Even with twelve years of warning, the transition was painful. Payment terminals, medical devices, industrial systems and older phones could not verify SHA-256 certificates, and some organisations asked for exemptions so that CAs could keep issuing SHA-1 certificates for payment processing endpoints that served those devices. Software signing systems and Git kept SHA-1 for years longer, and some legacy systems still use it.

What failed was not knowledge but inventory (nobody knew every place SHA-1 was used), ownership (devices belonged to merchants and partners) and agility (algorithms were hard-coded into firmware). The post-quantum transition is far larger: it replaces public-key algorithms, not one hash function, across protocols, certificates, hardware, firmware and partners, with larger keys and signatures that break size assumptions everywhere. If SHA-1 took about 15 years from first warning to near-retirement, starting post-quantum migration in the late 2020s with a 2035 deadline leaves little slack.

## 44.13 Chapter summary

- A CRQC running Shor's algorithm breaks RSA, Diffie-Hellman and all elliptic curve cryptography; larger keys do not help.
- Grover's algorithm only modestly weakens symmetric cryptography in practice; prefer AES-256 for long-lived data and keep SHA-256 or stronger.
- No CRQC exists, but error correction has passed key milestones and RSA-2048 resource estimates fell from about 20 million to under one million noisy qubits between 2019 and 2025.
- Mosca's inequality: if data shelf life plus migration time exceeds the time to a CRQC, you are already late.
- Harvest now, decrypt later makes key exchange the first priority; long-lived signature keys such as roots and firmware keys come next.
- Governments and the G7 financial roadmap converge on inventory now, high-risk systems by about 2030, and completion by 2035.
- QKD is not a general substitute for post-quantum cryptography.
