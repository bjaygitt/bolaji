## 1.1 What cryptography is for

Every system that handles information has to answer a few basic questions. Who can read this? Has anyone changed it? Who sent it? Can the sender later deny sending it? Cryptography gives mathematical answers to these questions, so that the answers hold even when the information travels over networks you do not control and sits on disks you cannot watch.

The answers are grouped into four **security goals**. Every tool in this book serves one or more of them.

| Goal | The question it answers | Everyday example | Main tools |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Confidentiality** | Can only the intended people read it? | Nobody on the café Wi-Fi can read your banking session | Encryption (AES, ChaCha20) |
| **Integrity** | Has it been changed, accidentally or on purpose? | A downloaded file is exactly what the publisher released | Hash functions, MACs |
| **Authentication** | Is it really from who it claims to be from? | Your browser knows it is talking to your bank, not an impostor | MACs, digital signatures, certificates |
| **Non-repudiation** | Can the sender later deny it? | A signed contract or a signed software release | Digital signatures |

Two points about this table trip people up.

First, **confidentiality does not give you integrity.** Encryption hides content, but on its own it does not stop an attacker from changing the encrypted bytes. With some encryption methods, an attacker who flips a bit in the encrypted data flips the same bit in the decrypted result, without ever knowing the key. In the lab at the end of this chapter you will turn an encrypted payment of 100 into a payment of 900 this way. Modern designs combine encryption with integrity protection for this reason (Chapter 6).

Second, **authentication is about the origin of data, not about people logging in.** When cryptographers say "authenticated", they mean "we can prove this came from the holder of a particular key". Whether that key belongs to the right person or server is a separate problem, solved by certificates and identity systems (Part III).

In an enterprise, these goals arrive disguised as business requirements: "payment instructions must not be altered" is integrity, and "only our mobile app may call this API" is authentication. Naming the goal tells you which tool to reach for.

## 1.2 Keys are the whole game

Almost every cryptographic tool works the same way: a public, well-known **algorithm** plus a secret **key**. The algorithm is like the design of a lock, which anyone can study. The key is like the physical key, which only the owner holds.

This leads to the most important principle in the field, stated by the Dutch cryptographer Auguste Kerckhoffs in 1883:

> **Kerckhoffs's principle.** A system should remain secure even if everything about it, except the key, is public knowledge.

People break this principle constantly by inventing private schemes. Secret designs get reverse-engineered, leaked or guessed, and then every copy is broken at once. A public algorithm that has survived years of expert attack is far safer, and if a key leaks, you replace one key instead of a whole design.

The consequence for your work is permanent: **protecting keys is the job.** The mathematics of a well-chosen algorithm is rarely what fails. Keys are generated badly, stored carelessly, copied too widely, never rotated, or stolen. Most of this book after Part II is about keys and the certificates that bind them to identities.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian Financial Group holds about 65,000 certificates, a few thousand vaulted secrets and keys in several HSM clusters, all using public algorithms. Its PKI and Cryptography Services team spends most of its time answering three questions for every key: where is it, who can use it, and when will it be replaced?

## 1.3 Symmetric and asymmetric: two families

Cryptographic tools come in two families.

**Symmetric cryptography** uses one shared secret key. The same key encrypts and decrypts, or creates and checks an integrity tag. It is fast, which makes it the workhorse for bulk data: every web page, every encrypted disk, every database backup. Its weakness is distribution: both sides need the same secret, and getting it to them safely is hard.

**Asymmetric cryptography**, also called **public-key cryptography**, uses a pair of linked keys. The **public key** can be shared with anyone. The **private key** is kept secret by one owner. What one key does, only the other can undo or check. This solves distribution: anyone can use your published public key to send you something only you can read, or to check something only you could have signed. Its weakness is speed: it is hundreds to thousands of times slower.

Real systems use both. This pattern, called **hybrid encryption**, appears everywhere:

1. Use asymmetric cryptography to agree on, or deliver, a fresh symmetric key.
2. Use that symmetric key to protect the actual data.

A TLS connection does exactly this: a few milliseconds of public-key work produce a shared session key, and everything after that is fast symmetric encryption. Cloud key management services use a related pattern called **envelope encryption**, where a protected master key encrypts many small data keys and each data key encrypts bulk data (Chapter 28).

## 1.4 Threat models: secure against whom?

"Is this secure?" has no answer on its own. The useful question is "secure against whom, doing what, for how long?" Writing that down is called a **threat model**, and a short one has four parts:

1. **Assets.** What are you protecting? Customer records, a signing key, a payment flow.
2. **Adversaries.** Who might attack it? A curious employee, a criminal group, a nation-state.
3. **Capabilities.** What can the attacker see or touch? Network traffic only? A stolen laptop? Your backups? Administrator rights on a hypervisor?
4. **Time.** How long must the protection last? A one-time login code needs seconds. Mortgage files need decades.

Time matters more every year. An attacker can record encrypted traffic today and keep it until a large quantum computer can break it. This is called **harvest now, decrypt later**, and for data that must stay secret for 10 years or more, the threat model already includes quantum attackers (Part XI). Enterprise threat models must also include insiders and suppliers: a backup administrator, a cloud operator or a compromised build server has capabilities that an outside attacker lacks.

Cryptographers also describe attackers by what they can do to the cryptography itself:

| Attack model | What the attacker can do | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ciphertext-only | Sees encrypted messages | The weakest attacker; any modern cipher must resist far more |
| Known-plaintext | Sees some messages and their encryptions | Common in reality: file headers and protocol messages are predictable |
| Chosen-plaintext | Can get messages of their choice encrypted | Realistic on the web, where attackers can trigger requests |
| Chosen-ciphertext | Can submit altered ciphertexts and learn from the reaction | Behind many real breaks, such as padding oracle attacks (Chapter 6) |

Modern algorithms resist all four. Real breaks usually come from surrounding software that leaks extra information.

> **In practice.** When you review a design, write its threat model in five lines before anyone mentions an algorithm. Most arguments between teams are really disagreements about the threat model.

## 1.5 How strong is strong enough? Security levels

Cryptographic strength is measured in **bits of security**. An algorithm with 128-bit security needs roughly 2^128 operations to break by the best known attack.

Different algorithms reach the same security level with very different key sizes:

| Security level | Symmetric key | Hash output | RSA key | Elliptic curve key |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 112 bits (minimum today) | 112 | 224 | 2048 | 224 |
| 128 bits (standard) | 128 | 256 | 3072 | 256 |
| 192 bits | 192 | 384 | 7680 | 384 |
| 256 bits | 256 | 512 | 15360 | 512 |

The values follow NIST guidance (SP 800-57). A 256-bit elliptic curve key is about as strong as a 3072-bit RSA key, and a 128-bit AES key is stronger than a 2048-bit RSA key. Comparing raw key lengths across algorithm families is meaningless; Chapter 3 explains why.

> **Current development.** NIST's post-quantum transition plan (IR 8547, first published as a draft for comment in November 2024) proposes deprecating RSA and elliptic-curve algorithms at the 112-bit level after 2030 and disallowing them after 2035, because quantum computers would break them regardless of key size. Symmetric algorithms survive with larger keys. Part XI explains the consequences for an enterprise.

## 1.6 What cryptography cannot do

Cryptography is powerful but narrow.

- **It cannot protect data while it is being used.** Data must normally be decrypted to be processed. If an attacker controls the server that decrypts it, encryption does not help. (Confidential computing, Chapter 30, narrows this gap.)
- **It cannot fix bad key handling.** A key stored next to the data it protects, or printed in a log file, gives no protection.
- **It cannot tell you who someone really is.** It proves a message came from a key holder. Linking that key to a real person or server is the job of certificates and identity processes.
- **It cannot stop authorised people from misusing access.** An administrator who can decrypt a database can copy it.
- **It cannot survive broken randomness.** Keys made from predictable random numbers are guessable, however strong the algorithm (Chapter 4).

When someone says "it's encrypted, so it's safe", ask: encrypted with what, where is the key, and who can use it?

## 1.7 Why cryptography fails in operations, not in mathematics

AES has been a standard since 2001 and remains unbroken in practice, yet cryptographic failures appear in incident reports every year.

The **key lifecycle**, the stages every key and certificate passes through from birth to destruction, shows where they happen.

| Lifecycle stage | What goes wrong | Typical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | Weak randomness; keys baked into images | Guessable or duplicated keys |
| Storage | Private keys in source code, file shares or plain configuration | Anyone who reads the file holds the key |
| Distribution | Keys emailed or shared between environments | One leak exposes production and test alike |
| Use | No control over who can use a key; validation that accepts the wrong key | Forged tokens or signatures |
| Rotation | No owner, no schedule, no automation | Old keys stay valid for years |
| Expiry | Certificates nobody tracks | Outages, or security tools that silently stop working |
| Retirement | Keys never revoked or destroyed | Old backups and devices remain a way in |

Three documented incidents show the pattern. In the Equifax breach of 2017, an expired certificate on a device that inspected encrypted traffic meant the device had stopped inspecting it, and the data theft went unnoticed for months; the congressional report found the company had let over 300 certificates expire. In 2023, an actor that Microsoft tracks as Storm-0558 used a stolen consumer signing key to forge tokens accepted for enterprise email accounts. The Cyber Safety Review Board's report describes a key created in 2016 that was still trusted years later, and validation that accepted it where it should not have. And in 2008, Debian found that a code change had crippled its OpenSSL random number generator, the failure story at the end of this chapter.

None of these attacked the mathematics. They were failures of expiry, rotation, use and generation. Cryptographic strength is mostly a property of **process and ownership**: a strong algorithm with no inventory, no owner and no rotation plan is a weak system.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian's GRC team once asked: "Which keys protect customer data, and who owns each one?" The answer took six weeks, because keys lived in load balancers owned by Network Engineering, Kubernetes clusters owned by Platform Engineering, payment HSMs owned by Payments Security, and configuration files owned by dozens of application teams. Chapter 2 draws that map.

## 1.8 The golden rules of applied cryptography

Experienced practitioners follow a short list of rules, each justified in later chapters.

1. **Do not invent your own algorithms or protocols.** Use well-studied standards.
2. **Do not implement primitives yourself in production.** Use mature libraries such as OpenSSL, BoringSSL, AWS-LC, libsodium or your platform's built-in cryptography. Tiny details, such as how long a comparison takes, can leak keys.
3. **Prefer high-level, hard-to-misuse interfaces.** A function `encrypt(key, message)` that handles nonces and integrity for you is safer than assembling AES, a mode and a MAC by hand.
4. **Always authenticate what you encrypt.** Use authenticated encryption (Chapter 6).
5. **Treat keys as the most valuable asset.** Generate them with good randomness, store them in protected places, give them owners and lifetimes, and plan their replacement from day one.
6. **Design for change.** Algorithms age. Systems that can switch algorithms and keys without a rewrite have **crypto agility**, now a regulatory expectation because of the post-quantum transition.
7. **Know what you have.** You cannot rotate, migrate or defend keys you have not found. Inventory comes before every other control (Chapter 43).

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requires documented key-management procedures and an inventory of trusted keys and certificates (requirements 3.6, 3.7 and 4.2.1.1), plus an annual review of cipher suites and protocols (12.3.3). ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control A.8.24 requires rules for the use of cryptography, including key management. Under DORA, EU financial entities must keep an encryption policy and a register of certificates. Chapter 41 maps these in detail.

## 1.9 Lab: see why encryption alone is not enough

This lab uses OpenSSL and Python on Linux or macOS to show confidentiality without integrity, integrity with a MAC, and hybrid encryption.

1. Create a working directory and three random values: an encryption key, a starting counter value and a separate MAC key.

```bash
mkdir crypto-lab1 && cd crypto-lab1
KEY=$(openssl rand -hex 32)
IV=$(openssl rand -hex 16)
MACKEY=$(openssl rand -hex 32)
```

2. Encrypt a payment instruction with AES in counter mode, which gives confidentiality only, and compute an HMAC over the ciphertext.

```bash
printf 'PAY 100 GBP TO ALICE' > msg.txt
openssl enc -aes-256-ctr -K "$KEY" -iv "$IV" -in msg.txt -out msg.enc
openssl dgst -sha256 -mac HMAC -macopt hexkey:"$MACKEY" msg.enc
```

3. Play the attacker. You do not know the key, but you guess the message format. Flip bits in byte 4 so that "1" becomes "9", then decrypt.

```bash
python3 -c "
d = bytearray(open('msg.enc', 'rb').read())
d[4] ^= ord('1') ^ ord('9')
open('msg.enc', 'wb').write(d)"
openssl enc -d -aes-256-ctr -K "$KEY" -iv "$IV" -in msg.enc; echo
```

The output reads `PAY 900 GBP TO ALICE`, with no error.

4. Recompute the HMAC from step 2. It no longer matches, so a receiver checking the tag would reject the message. Authenticated encryption combines both steps.

5. Try hybrid encryption. Generate an RSA key pair, create a random 256-bit session key, and wrap it with the public key.

```bash
openssl genpkey -algorithm RSA -pkeyopt rsa_keygen_bits:3072 \
  -out alice.key
openssl pkey -in alice.key -pubout -out alice.pub
openssl rand -out session.key 32
openssl pkeyutl -encrypt -pubin -inkey alice.pub \
  -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -in session.key -out session.wrapped
openssl pkeyutl -decrypt -inkey alice.key \
  -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -in session.wrapped -out session.out
cmp session.key session.out && echo "session key recovered"
```

The wrapped key is 384 bytes, the size of the RSA modulus, while the session key inside is 32 bytes.

6. Write a five-line threat model for the payment message, naming which goal from section 1.1 the attacker in step 3 violated.

**Optional cloud extension.** Repeat step 5 with AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault or Google Cloud KMS: create a key, wrap your session key with the service's encrypt operation, and compare the audit log entries. Cost warning: managed keys carry a small monthly charge and API calls are billed, so schedule the key for deletion afterwards.

## 1.10 Failure story: the debugging change that broke a million keys

In 2006, a maintainer of the Debian Linux distribution removed two lines from OpenSSL's random number generator. A code analysis tool had warned that the lines used uninitialised memory, and removing them silenced the warning. But one of those lines was how the generator collected most of its unpredictability. Afterwards, the only varying input was the process ID, a number usually below 32,768.

For almost two years, every SSH key, TLS key and other key generated on Debian and Ubuntu systems came from a pool of only about 32,000 possibilities per key type and size. An attacker could generate all of them and try each one. When the flaw was found in May 2008, administrators worldwide had to replace keys, revoke certificates and re-issue credentials.

RSA and DSA were never weak. The failure was a well-meant change to code the maintainer did not fully understand, in the most sensitive part of the system, without review by someone who did. Organisations that knew where their keys were recovered fastest. The lessons: do not modify cryptographic code casually, randomness is a single point of failure (Chapter 4), and plan for the day every key must be replaced.

## 1.11 Chapter summary

- Cryptography provides confidentiality, integrity, authentication and non-repudiation. Encryption alone provides only the first.
- Security rests on keys, not secret designs (Kerckhoffs's principle). Protecting and managing keys is the real work.
- Symmetric cryptography is fast but needs a shared secret; asymmetric cryptography solves distribution but is slow. Hybrid designs use both.
- A threat model names assets, adversaries, capabilities and time, and in an enterprise it includes insiders and suppliers.
- Strength is measured in bits of security; key sizes are not comparable across algorithm families.
- Cryptography cannot fix bad key handling, bad randomness or compromised endpoints.
- Real failures cluster in the key lifecycle. Ownership and inventory matter more than algorithm choice.
