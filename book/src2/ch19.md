## 19.1 Why operations decide whether automation works

A **certificate** binds a name (a website, a server, a device or a person) to a public key, and it is signed by a **certificate authority (CA)** that clients trust. Every certificate has an expiry date. When it passes, every client that checks the date refuses to connect, with no grace period. **Certificate lifecycle management (CLM)** is the discipline, and usually the platform, that discovers certificates, issues them according to policy, installs them, renews them and retires them. Chapter 17 explains the lifecycle, the protocols and the platforms. Chapter 18 explains how certificates are pushed or pulled into load balancers, CDNs, firewalls, Kubernetes, Windows and Java applications. This chapter assumes those pieces exist and asks a different question: how do you run them so that nothing expires, nothing breaks after a renewal, and you can prove both?

Three pressures make operations the deciding factor.

First, lifetimes are falling. The CA/Browser Forum, the body of CAs and browser makers that sets rules for publicly trusted certificates, approved a schedule in 2025 that cuts the maximum lifetime of a public TLS certificate to 200 days from 15 March 2026, 100 days from 15 March 2027 and 47 days from 15 March 2029. The same ballot cuts the time a CA may reuse a previous **domain control validation (DCV)**, its check that you control a domain name, to 10 days by 2029. At 47 days, an organisation with 4,000 public certificates performs roughly 47,000 renewals a year, about 130 every calendar day. Every one of those is a chance for an installation to fail.

Second, automation moves the failure point. When a person renews a certificate by hand, they usually notice if the website breaks. When a pipeline renews it at 03:00, a missing intermediate certificate or a service that was never reloaded can sit unnoticed until the old certificate expires, and then the outage looks like an expiry even though a valid certificate is sitting on disk.

Third, the dangerous failures are often silent. A web server with an expired certificate causes an outage that people report within minutes. A security appliance with an expired certificate may simply stop inspecting traffic, and nobody complains at all.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian Financial Group runs about 65,000 certificates, of which roughly 4,000 are publicly trusted and the rest come from its private CAs. The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the CLM platform, the CA relationships and the runbooks in this chapter. Application teams own their certificates in the sense that matters most: they are accountable for each service, approve its configuration and answer when it breaks. Platform Engineering owns cert-manager on the Kubernetes clusters, Network Engineering owns the load balancers and firewalls, and the SOC watches the alerts. The operating model in this chapter is how those teams share one service without anyone assuming someone else is watching.

## 19.2 The rollout programme

A CLM rollout is a programme, not a project with a go-live date. The platform can be installed in weeks. Bringing every certificate under automated, owned, policy-compliant management takes one to three years in a large organisation, because it depends on hundreds of application teams changing how they work.

### Phases

Most successful programmes follow a sequence like the one below. The phases overlap, and a large estate runs several at once for different technology groups.

| Phase | Goal | Typical exit criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Foundations | Platform built, CAs connected, policy written, roles agreed | Production platform with HA, two public CAs, private CA integration, RBAC and audit log streaming to the SIEM |
| 1. Discovery and inventory | Know what exists | Network scans of all internal ranges and DMZs, CT-log monitoring, CA account imports and cloud discovery running on a schedule; at least 90 percent of found certificates mapped to an owner |
| 2. Visibility and alerting | Nothing expires by surprise | Owners receive expiry alerts through the ticketing system, not personal email; zero unowned certificates within 30 days of expiry |
| 3. Pilot automation | Prove the delivery patterns | Two or three friendly teams per pattern (Kubernetes, load balancer, Windows, Java) fully automated, with validation after every install |
| 4. Scale-out by wave | Automate the bulk | Waves grouped by technology rather than business unit; each wave has an onboarding pack, a target date and a named lead |
| 5. Long tail and exceptions | Deal with what cannot be automated | Every remaining manual certificate has a documented exception, an owner, a compensating control and an expiry date for the exception itself |
| 6. Steady state | Run it as a service | KPIs reported monthly, runbooks tested, CA failover exercised at least yearly |

Group waves by technology because the work is technical: once the load balancer connector works for one application, it works for the next hundred with little extra effort. Grouping by business unit forces you to solve every integration at once.

### Onboarding application owners

An **application owner** is the person or team accountable for a service and its configuration. They do not want to learn PKI. They want three things: to know what they must do, to be confident that automation will not break their service, and to stop being woken up by expiry emails.

A good onboarding pack contains:

- a one-page explanation of what changes for them, with a named contact in the PKI team
- the list of certificates the inventory has attributed to their application, which they must confirm or correct
- the delivery pattern that fits their platform, with a worked example
- the validation checks that will run after each renewal, and where to see the results
- what happens when a renewal fails, who is alerted and when
- a short rollback procedure

Hold a 30-minute session per team or per group of similar teams, then automate one certificate together while they watch. Trust comes from seeing a renewal happen and the service stay up.

> **In practice.** Ownership data decays. People change teams and applications are decommissioned. Ask owners to reconfirm their certificates every six months, and treat an unconfirmed certificate as unowned. Route ownership to a team or a service, never to an individual's mailbox.

### Change management

**Change management** is the IT process that approves and records changes to production systems. If every renewal needs a change ticket and a weekly approval board, automation stops being automatic, and at 47-day lifetimes the board would review thousands of tickets a month.

The answer is the **standard change**: a pre-approved, low-risk, repeatable change that is logged but not individually reviewed. Agree with the change advisory board that an automated renewal is a standard change when all of these are true:

1. The subject names and SANs are unchanged.
2. The key type, issuing CA and certificate template are unchanged.
3. The target is a system that has completed a successful pilot renewal through the same connector.
4. Post-installation validation runs automatically and failure triggers rollback or an alert.

Anything else, such as a new name, a CA migration or a first installation on a new device type, is a normal change. The CLM platform or the pipeline should open and close the standard change record itself through the ITSM system's API (ServiceNow, BMC Helix ITSM and Jira Service Management all expose one). The record then becomes audit evidence for free.

### CMDB integration

A **configuration management database (CMDB)** records the organisation's configuration items (servers, applications, network devices) and how they relate. The CLM inventory and the CMDB need each other. The CMDB knows which application a server belongs to and who owns it. The CLM inventory knows which certificates are installed where.

Integrate them in both directions:

- **CMDB to CLM:** import applications, owners, support groups, environments and hostnames, so a newly discovered certificate on a known server is attributed to the right owner automatically.
- **CLM to CMDB:** publish certificates as configuration items (or as attributes of the endpoint), with relationships to the servers and services that use them. Incident and change processes can then see that a load balancer carries 40 certificates for 12 applications.

Keep the CLM inventory as the authoritative source for certificate data (serial, expiry, issuer, key type) and the CMDB as the authoritative source for ownership and topology. Two systems that both claim to own the same field will disagree within months.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian ran its rollout in waves: Kubernetes first (Platform Engineering was already using cert-manager), then the load balancers in DC-East and DC-West, then Windows servers through AD CS auto-enrolment, then the Java estate, with the mainframe and the payment HSM certificates last as managed exceptions. Ownership comes from the CMDB's application records. The PKI team publishes certificate data back as configuration items, so when the SOC opens an incident against a host, the responder sees the certificates on it and their expiry dates.

## 19.3 Post-installation validation

A renewal is complete when a client connecting to the service receives the new certificate and trusts it, not when the CA issues it. **Post-installation validation** is the automated check that proves this, run after every installation and on a schedule.

Check from the client's point of view, over the network, using the same name the clients use. Checking the file on disk proves nothing about what the service presents.

### The checks

1. **The served certificate is the new one.** Compare the serial number or SHA-256 fingerprint presented on the wire with the one the platform just issued. A mismatch usually means the service was not reloaded, or a different node or listener answered.
2. **The chain is complete and correct.** The server must send its leaf certificate and every intermediate certificate up to, but not normally including, the root. Validate the presented chain against the trust stores your clients actually use, not only against the one on your workstation.
3. **The names match.** Every hostname clients use must appear in the **Subject Alternative Name (SAN)** extension. Modern clients ignore the Common Name for hostname checking.
4. **The key matches.** The public key in the certificate must correspond to the private key the service holds. A mismatch normally prevents the service from starting at all, but on some appliances it causes a silent fallback to a default certificate.
5. **Revocation information is reachable.** If the certificate contains an **OCSP** (Online Certificate Status Protocol) responder URL or a **CRL** (certificate revocation list) distribution point, clients that check revocation must be able to reach it. This matters most for private PKI, where the responder or CRL host sits inside your network and may be blocked from a DMZ or a partner network. For public certificates, note that the CA/Browser Forum has made OCSP optional and some CAs have dropped it: Let's Encrypt ended its OCSP service in 2025 and relies on CRLs.
6. **Protocol and cipher policy is still met.** A renewal can change the key type (for example from RSA to ECDSA), and an appliance may then fall back to a different cipher suite list. Confirm that only approved protocol versions (TLS 1.2 and 1.3 in most policies) and approved cipher suites are offered.
7. **The expiry is what policy expects.** A certificate with a lifetime far longer or shorter than the template is a sign that the wrong template or CA was used.

### Where validation runs

Run validation from at least two vantage points: from inside the network segment the service lives in, and from where its real clients are (the internet for public services, a branch or partner network for internal ones). Many platforms can run checks after each push: Keyfactor Command, CyberArk Certificate Manager, AppViewX and DigiCert Trust Lifecycle Manager all offer discovery or scanning that can confirm what an endpoint presents (as of 2026, check each vendor's documentation for whether this runs automatically after provisioning). Open-source equivalents include the Prometheus blackbox exporter, which probes TLS endpoints and exposes the earliest certificate expiry as a metric, and simple scripts like the one in the lab in Section 19.12.

> **Watch out.** A load balancer or CDN may present different certificates depending on the **Server Name Indication (SNI)**, the hostname the client sends during the handshake. A check without SNI often returns a default certificate and reports success while the real virtual server is broken. Always send the real hostname.

## 19.4 Monitoring, alerting and KPIs

Monitoring answers "is anything about to go wrong?" Alerting decides who is told and when. KPIs (key performance indicators) answer "is the programme working?" for managers, auditors and the board.

### What to monitor

- **Expiry, measured as a fraction of lifetime.** A fixed "30 days" threshold is wrong for a 47-day certificate. Alert when less than about one third of the lifetime remains and renewal has not succeeded.
- **Renewal failures,** from the CLM platform, ACME clients and cert-manager. Each failed attempt is information; three consecutive failures are an incident in the making.
- **What endpoints present,** from scheduled validation scans, compared against the inventory.
- **Certificate Transparency.** Public CAs must log every certificate they issue to **Certificate Transparency (CT)** logs. Monitoring CT for your domains (using services such as Cert Spotter or crt.sh, or a CLM platform's built-in CT monitor) shows certificates issued outside your process.
- **Platform health:** connector and agent heartbeats, CA API errors, rate-limit responses, queue lengths.
- **Security events:** private key exports, CA credential changes, issuance outside policy, and new administrators. Stream these to the SIEM for the SOC.

### Escalation

Alerts must go to a team queue in the ticketing system, with escalation that scales with the remaining lifetime:

| Remaining lifetime | Situation | Who is told |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About one third | Renewal starts | Nobody, unless it fails |
| One third to one fifth | Automatic retries failing | Owning team, ticket raised |
| One fifth | Still failing | Team and its manager |
| One tenth | Still failing | PKI on-call takes over |
| Final few days | Still failing | Major incident process |

### KPIs

Report a small set of KPIs monthly, with trends, and always split public from private certificates.

| KPI | Definition | Example target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory coverage | Certificates in the inventory divided by certificates found by all discovery methods | Above 98 percent |
| Ownership coverage | Certificates with a confirmed owning team divided by all certificates | Above 98 percent |
| Automation rate | Certificates renewed and installed with no human action divided by all certificates | Public above 95 percent; private rising each quarter |
| Validation pass rate | Installations that passed post-installation validation first time divided by all installations | Above 99 percent |
| Time-to-renew | Median and 95th percentile time from renewal start to validated installation | Minutes for automated; days means a manual step is hiding |
| Renewal lead time | Remaining lifetime at the moment the new certificate went live | Never below one fifth of lifetime |
| Expiry incidents | Outages or control failures caused by an expired certificate | Zero |
| Expired in use | Expired certificates still presented by any endpoint | Zero |
| Out-of-band issuance | Public certificates for your domains seen in CT but not issued through the platform | Zero, each one investigated |
| Policy compliance | Certificates with no policy violations (key type, CA, validity, wildcard rules) | Above 98 percent |
| Mean time to replace | Time to replace all certificates from one CA or one intermediate in a drill | Hours for public, days for private |

"Expired in use" deserves its own line because it catches the silent failures. In the 2017 Equifax breach, a device that inspected encrypted traffic had an expired certificate for about 19 months, so it stopped inspecting traffic and the attackers went unnoticed; a later US House committee report found the company had let more than 300 certificates expire. An outage gets fixed. An expired certificate on a security control can sit there for a year and a half.

> **Compliance hook.** Ownership coverage and inventory coverage map directly to the PCI DSS v4.0.1 certificate inventory requirement (Section 19.9) and to ISO/IEC 27001 asset inventory. Expiry incidents and renewal lead time are good evidence for DORA ICT risk management and for operational resilience reporting.

## 19.5 Failure modes and fixes

Most certificate problems after a renewal fall into a short list of patterns. Learn to recognise them by symptom, because the error message a user reports rarely names the cause.

| Failure mode | Typical symptom | How to confirm | Fix and prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Missing intermediate certificate | Works in desktop browsers, fails in curl, Java, mobile apps and APIs with "unable to get local issuer certificate" | openssl s_client -showcerts shows only one certificate | Install the full chain (leaf plus intermediates); make chain completeness a validation check. Browsers often hide this by fetching or caching intermediates |
| Outdated roots in Java or appliance trust stores | New certificate from a new root or intermediate fails only for some clients: old JVMs, appliances, IoT, partner systems | keytool -list on the client trust store; vendor trust-store version | Update cacerts and appliance trust bundles on a schedule; test new CA hierarchies against an inventory of client trust stores before switching |
| Expired cross-signed root in the chain | Old clients report "certificate has expired" although the leaf is valid | openssl s_client shows an expired certificate in the served chain | Serve a chain that ends at a currently trusted root; remove expired roots from client stores |
| Certificate or key pinning | A mobile app or partner client rejects a perfectly valid renewed certificate | App logs show pin validation failure; the pinned hash matches the old key or intermediate | Pin to several keys including a backup, or to a CA you control; better, stop pinning public certificates; inventory every pin as a dependency |
| HA pair updated on one node | Intermittent failures, roughly half of connections, or failures after a failover | Connect to each node's own address; compare serials | Push to the active unit and sync the pair, or push to both; validate every node, not the floating address |
| CDN propagation delay | Some edge locations serve the old certificate for minutes to hours | Check from several regions; compare serials | Upload well before expiry; wait for the CDN's deployment status to report complete before considering the job done |
| HSM-backed key cannot be exported | Renewal job fails when it tries to copy the key to a second device | Platform log shows export denied; key attributes are non-exportable | Generate a new key on each device and issue per device, or use a network HSM shared by both; never weaken key attributes to make the pipeline work |
| Device API rate limits | Bulk push fails after the first few dozen devices with HTTP 429 or session errors | Device or management-plane logs show throttling | Batch and pace pushes; reuse sessions; push through the manager (such as Panorama or a central ADC manager) instead of every device |
| DCV failure | Public CA refuses issuance; order stays pending or invalid | CA order status; dig shows a missing or stale TXT or CNAME record | Automate DNS-01 through an API or CNAME delegation; check that split-horizon DNS is not answering the CA from the internal view; check CAA records |
| CA rate limits | Mass renewal stalls with "too many certificates" errors | ACME error type rateLimited | Spread renewals, use ARI windows, request higher limits from the CA in advance |
| Clock skew | "Certificate is not yet valid" just after renewal; failures only on some hosts | date on the client; NTP status | Fix NTP; most CAs backdate the start time slightly, but devices with bad clocks still fail |
| Wrong key type or size | Old clients or appliances cannot use an ECDSA certificate; policy scanner flags RSA 2048 where 3072 is required | openssl x509 -text shows the public key algorithm | Encode the key type in the template per target class; keep RSA available for legacy clients where policy allows |
| SAN mismatch | "Hostname mismatch" or "certificate is not valid for" errors on some names only | Compare the SAN list with every name clients use, including aliases and load-balanced names | Generate SANs from the inventory or CMDB, not from the old certificate; validate each name |
| Service not reloaded | New certificate on disk, old certificate on the wire, then an expiry outage | Serial on the wire differs from the file | Make reload part of the delivery job (nginx -s reload, systemctl reload, a rolling restart for Java); validate on the wire |
| Wrong certificate bound | Service presents a default or self-signed certificate | Issuer is the device's own name | Bind by name in the right profile or listener; check SNI routing |
| Revocation endpoint unreachable | Long handshake delays or hard failures in clients that require revocation checking | curl or certutil -urlfetch cannot reach the OCSP or CRL URL | Open the network path; publish CRLs to a reachable location; for private PKI, test from every client zone |

### A closer look at three of them

**Missing intermediates** are the most common post-renewal failure, and they are confusing because browsers hide them. A browser that has seen the intermediate before, or that can download it using the **Authority Information Access (AIA)** URL in the leaf, will build the chain anyway. Command-line tools, Java, Go programs, most API clients and many mobile platforms will not. The fix is always on the server: configure the full chain file (leaf first, then intermediates in order). The classic cause is a renewal script that writes only cert.pem and not fullchain.pem, or an appliance import that accepts a leaf without asking for its issuer.

**Outdated trust stores** are the mirror image: the server is right and the client is old. When the Let's Encrypt cross-signed root DST Root CA X3 expired on 30 September 2021, servers were still sending a chain that included a cross-signature from it. Modern clients found the alternative path to ISRG Root X1 and carried on. Clients built on OpenSSL 1.0.2, older Java releases and some appliances failed, because they either did not trust the new root or chose the expired path. Enterprises felt it most in machine-to-machine traffic, where the "client" was a ten-year-old appliance nobody owned. The lesson is that every CA change, including a CA's own planned root rollover, needs a test against an inventory of client trust stores, not just a browser.

**HA pairs updated on one node** produce the most frustrating tickets: "it fails sometimes". An F5 BIG-IP pair, a NetScaler HA pair or two NGINX nodes behind a virtual IP each hold their own copy of the certificate. If the push reaches the active unit and the configuration sync fails, everything works until a failover. Validate by connecting to each node's self address with the service's SNI, not only to the shared address.

> **Watch out.** Pinning is the failure mode that automation cannot fix on its own. If a mobile app or a partner's system pins your current key or intermediate, a perfectly correct renewal breaks it. Find pins before you change CA or key, by asking mobile and integration teams and by searching code for hashes and certificate files. Chapter 18 covers the integration patterns that avoid pinning.

## 19.6 The troubleshooting toolkit

You need a small set of tools and the habit of using them in the same order: what does the server send, does it verify, does it match the key, and what did the automation log say?

### OpenSSL

Show what a server presents, with SNI, including every certificate in the chain:

```bash
openssl s_client -connect www.example.com:443 \
  -servername www.example.com -showcerts </dev/null
```

Read the "Certificate chain" block (one line per certificate: s: is the subject, i: is the issuer) and the "Verify return code" at the end. Code 0 means the chain verified against the local trust store; code 20 or 21 usually means a missing intermediate.

Print the details of the leaf as served:

```bash
openssl s_client -connect www.example.com:443 \
  -servername www.example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -serial \
      -dates -ext subjectAltName
```

Verify a chain offline, with the intermediates as untrusted helpers and the root as the trust anchor:

```bash
openssl verify -CAfile root.pem -untrusted intermediate.pem leaf.pem
```

Check that a certificate and a private key belong together, for any key type, by comparing the hashes of their public keys:

```bash
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -pubkey | openssl sha256
openssl pkey -in leaf.key -pubout | openssl sha256
```

Check whether a certificate expires within 30 days (exit code 1 if it does):

```bash
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -checkend 2592000
```

Find the revocation endpoints and query OCSP directly:

```bash
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -ocsp_uri
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -ext crlDistributionPoints
openssl ocsp -issuer intermediate.pem -cert leaf.pem \
  -url http://ocsp.example-ca.com -resp_text
```

Confirm that old protocols are refused (this should fail):

```bash
openssl s_client -connect www.example.com:443 \
  -servername www.example.com -tls1_1 </dev/null
```

For a full protocol and cipher review, testssl.sh and the nmap ssl-enum-ciphers script produce reports suitable for evidence.

### curl

curl shows what a typical non-browser client experiences, including chain problems browsers hide:

```bash
curl -v https://www.example.com/ -o /dev/null
curl -v --resolve www.example.com:443:10.20.30.41 \
  https://www.example.com/ -o /dev/null
```

The --resolve form sends the real hostname to a specific node's address, which is how you test each member of an HA pair or each origin behind a CDN.

### keytool (Java)

Java applications use their own trust store (cacerts, or one set with javax.net.ssl.trustStore) and keystores in PKCS#12 or JKS format:

```bash
keytool -printcert -sslserver www.example.com:443
keytool -list -v -keystore app.p12 -storetype PKCS12
keytool -list -cacerts | grep -i isrg
keytool -importcert -alias corp-issuing-2026 -file issuing.pem \
  -keystore truststore.p12 -storetype PKCS12
```

The first command shows the chain a Java client receives. The third answers the most common Java question: does this JVM trust the root?

### certutil (Windows)

On Windows, certutil tests chain building and revocation exactly as Windows clients and services do, including AIA fetching:

```powershell
certutil -verify -urlfetch .\leaf.cer
certutil -store My
certutil -url .\leaf.cer
```

The -urlfetch output shows each AIA, CRL and OCSP URL it tried and whether it succeeded, which makes it the best tool for private PKI revocation problems. (Note that NSS also ships a tool called certutil, used for Firefox and some Linux services, with different syntax.)

### Kubernetes and cert-manager

In cert-manager, a Certificate resource creates a CertificateRequest; for ACME issuers that creates an Order, and the Order creates Challenges. A failure is visible at whichever stage stopped:

```bash
kubectl get certificate,certificaterequest,order,challenge -n shop
kubectl describe certificate shop-tls -n shop
kubectl describe challenge -n shop
cmctl status certificate shop-tls -n shop
cmctl renew shop-tls -n shop
cmctl inspect secret shop-tls -n shop
kubectl logs -n cert-manager deploy/cert-manager --since=1h
```

The Events section at the end of kubectl describe usually names the cause: a DNS provider credential error, a challenge that cannot be reached, or an issuer that is not ready. cmctl status shows the whole chain of resources in one view.

### Reading logs

Every layer keeps a log, and the cause of a failure is usually one layer below where the symptom appeared:

- **CLM platform:** the job or workflow history for the certificate, showing request, approval, issuance, delivery and validation, each with a status. Start here.
- **CA:** the order history in the CA portal or API, including DCV status and rate-limit errors.
- **Connector or agent:** the component that talked to the device. Authentication failures, timeouts and API errors from the target appear here.
- **Target system:** the web server error log, the appliance audit log, the Windows event log (CertificateServicesClient for auto-enrolment), or the Java application log at startup.
- **Client:** the browser developer tools, an app's TLS error, or a partner's error message. Ask for the exact text and the time.

> **In practice.** Write the first five commands into the on-call runbook with the real hostnames filled in. At 03:00, nobody remembers the -servername flag.

## 19.7 Runbooks

A **runbook** is a step-by-step procedure for a known situation, written in advance and tested. Three certificate situations deserve runbooks in every organisation.

### Runbook A: mass revocation or CA distrust

Triggers: a CA announces that certificates must be revoked (the CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements give CAs 24 hours for some reasons and five days for others), or a browser root programme announces that it will distrust a CA, as Chrome and Mozilla did with Entrust in 2024.

1. **Declare the incident** and name an incident lead from the PKI team. Open a bridge with Network Engineering, Platform Engineering, application on-call and the SOC.
2. **Scope it from the inventory.** Select certificates by issuing CA, intermediate, serial list from the CA, or validation date. Export the list with owners, endpoints and automation status.
3. **Check the deadline.** For a revocation, note the exact revocation time. For a distrust, note which certificates are affected (often those issued after a cut-off date) and from which client versions.
4. **Confirm the backup CA is ready:** account active, organisation and domains validated, CAA records allowing it, templates prepared.
5. **Prioritise:** internet-facing and customer-facing services first, then partner APIs, then internal services that use the public certificates, then everything else.
6. **Switch issuance** for the affected group to the backup CA in the CLM policy or ACME configuration, as one change.
7. **Renew automated certificates in batches,** paced to stay within CA and device rate limits, with validation after each batch.
8. **Assign manual certificates** to named engineers with a deadline; these are the long tail that causes outages.
9. **Check pins and client trust stores** for the new CA before cutting over clients that are known to pin or to run old trust stores.
10. **Track to zero** on a shared dashboard: affected, replaced, validated.
11. **Hold a review** within two weeks: which certificates were manual, which owners were wrong, how long it took, and what to automate next.

### Runbook B: private key compromise

Triggers: a private key is found in a code repository, a ticket, a backup outside its control boundary, or on a compromised host.

1. **Contain:** treat it as a security incident with the SOC. Preserve evidence of where the key was found.
2. **Identify every certificate** that uses the key's public key (one key may be reused across several certificates or servers; search the inventory by public key hash).
3. **Generate new keys** on each affected system. Never reuse or re-certify the compromised key.
4. **Issue and install replacements,** and validate them on the wire.
5. **Revoke the old certificates** with the reason keyCompromise once the replacements are live. For a public certificate, the CA must revoke within 24 hours of being told of key compromise, so coordinate timing, but never delay revocation to suit convenience.
6. **Search for misuse:** CT logs for unexpected certificates, access logs for impersonation, and network sensors for traffic signed or decrypted with the old key.
7. **Fix the cause:** why was the key exportable or copied? Move it into an HSM, a vault or on-device generation as policy requires.

### Runbook C: emergency replacement at scale

Triggers: a broken intermediate, a weak algorithm finding, a mis-issued batch, or a template error discovered after thousands of certificates were issued.

1. **Freeze** further issuance from the faulty template or intermediate.
2. **Scope** the affected certificates from the inventory and split them into automated and manual.
3. **Fix the template** and test with one certificate per delivery pattern (Kubernetes, ADC, Windows, Java, CDN).
4. **Release in waves** of increasing size (for example 10, 100, 1,000, then the rest), checking validation results between waves.
5. **Throttle** to respect device API limits and change windows for the most sensitive systems.
6. **Report progress** every few hours to incident command and, for regulated services, to GRC so they can judge whether regulators must be notified.
7. **Close** only when the inventory shows zero affected certificates in use, including those found by network scan rather than only those the platform issued.

> **At Meridian.** The PKI team rehearses Runbook A twice a year by replacing every public certificate from one CA with certificates from the second CA in the non-production environment, and quarterly by issuing and installing a production certificate from the backup CA. The result, measured as mean time to replace, is reported to the operational resilience committee as DORA testing evidence.

## 19.8 Getting ready for 47-day certificates and ARI

The schedule set by the CA/Browser Forum gives organisations until March 2029 to reach 47 days, but each step down roughly doubles renewal volume, and the 10-day DCV reuse window means that domain validation, not just issuance, must be automated.

### ARI in one paragraph

**ACME Renewal Information (ARI)**, standardised as RFC 9773 in 2025, lets an ACME CA tell each client when to renew each certificate. The client asks the CA's renewalInfo endpoint about a certificate and receives a suggested renewal window. Normally the window sits in the last third of the lifetime and is spread out to avoid load spikes. If the CA must revoke certificates, it moves the window into the past, and every ARI-aware client renews at its next check, with no human involvement. When the client places its new order it says which certificate it replaces, so the CA knows the renewal happened. ARI turns a mass revocation from a 24-hour scramble into something closer to routine. As of 2026, ARI is supported by Let's Encrypt and several other ACME CAs and by a growing set of clients (Caddy, lego and Certbot among them); check your client's and your CLM platform's release notes.

### A readiness checklist

- [ ] Every public certificate renews automatically, or has a dated exception.
- [ ] DCV is automated through DNS-01 with an API, or through CNAME delegation of the _acme-challenge label to a zone the automation controls.
- [ ] Renewal triggers are a fraction of lifetime, not a fixed number of days.
- [ ] ACME clients and the CLM platform honour ARI where the CA offers it, and poll at least daily.
- [ ] Post-installation validation runs after every renewal.
- [ ] Device and CA rate limits have been measured, and batch sizes set below them.
- [ ] Standard change approval covers automated renewals.
- [ ] Alerting thresholds have been scaled to the shorter lifetime.
- [ ] Systems that cannot automate (some appliances, mainframe interfaces, partner-installed certificates) have been moved behind something that can, such as a load balancer or CDN that terminates TLS, or onto a private CA with longer lifetimes where the use case allows.
- [ ] Certificates that use public CAs for private purposes (internal servers, client authentication, mTLS between companies) have been reviewed. Public TLS hierarchies are being restricted to server authentication only under the Chrome Root Program, so client-authentication uses belong on a private or dedicated hierarchy.

> **Current development.** In January 2026 Let's Encrypt made its six-day certificates (about 160 hours, requested through a "shortlived" ACME profile) generally available, along with certificates for IP addresses. Very short lifetimes reduce reliance on revocation, which many clients do not check reliably. They are optional, but they show where the industry is heading: if your automation can renew every few days, 47 days holds no fear.

## 19.9 Audit evidence and compliance mapping

Auditors and assessors ask for evidence, not intentions. A well-run CLM programme produces most of it as a by-product. The table maps common controls to the evidence a certificate automation service can produce.

| Framework and control | What it asks for | Evidence from the CLM programme |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PCI DSS v4.0.1 Req. 4.2.1 | Strong cryptography for PAN over open, public networks; only trusted keys and certificates accepted; certificates confirmed valid, not expired or revoked | Validation scan results for in-scope endpoints; chain and expiry reports; protocol and cipher scan output |
| PCI DSS v4.0.1 Req. 4.2.1.1 | An inventory of the entity's trusted keys and certificates used to protect PAN during transmission is maintained | Inventory export filtered to cardholder data environment endpoints, with owner, issuer and expiry; evidence of scheduled discovery |
| PCI DSS v4.0.1 Req. 12.3.3 | Cryptographic cipher suites and protocols in use are documented and reviewed at least once every 12 months | Protocol and cipher inventory from scans; annual review record |
| NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 SC-12, SC-17 | Cryptographic key establishment and management; issue public key certificates under an approved policy or obtain them from an approved provider | Certificate policy and templates; list of approved CAs; issuance audit log |
| NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 CM-8 | System component inventory | CLM inventory linked to the CMDB |
| NIST SP 1800-16 | Practice guide for TLS server certificate management | Mapping of programme processes to its recommendations (inventory, ownership, automation, monitoring) |
| ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A 8.24 | Rules for effective use of cryptography, including key management | Cryptography and key management policy; CLM policy and templates; key generation location records |

Note the PCI DSS numbering carefully: Requirement 4.2.1.1 is the certificate and key inventory, introduced in v4.0 as a future-dated requirement that became mandatory on 31 March 2025, while 4.2.1 holds the validity and trust rules. Assessors differ on whether 4.2.1.1 covers internal transmissions inside the cardholder data environment; most organisations simply include them.

### Producing evidence efficiently

- **Automate the reports.** Schedule monthly exports from the CLM platform: inventory for each scope (PCI, SOX systems, DORA critical functions), expiry status, policy violations and KPIs. Store them in the GRC system with timestamps.
- **Keep the audit trail.** Issuance, approval, revocation, key export and policy change events, retained for the period your policies require (often at least one year for PCI DSS logs, longer for financial regulation).
- **Show the exceptions.** An auditor trusts a programme that lists its 40 manual certificates with owners and compensating controls far more than one that claims full automation.
- **Sample validation.** Be ready to pick any certificate from the inventory and show, live, that the endpoint presents it and that it verifies.

> **Compliance hook.** Meridian Pay's QSA samples the PCI inventory each year. Because the inventory is generated from discovery scans of the cardholder data environment rather than maintained by hand, the PKI team can show that a newly deployed endpoint appeared in the inventory within one scan cycle, which answers the assessor's real question: would you know if something was missing?

## 19.10 PQC and hybrid certificates in CLM

**Post-quantum cryptography (PQC)** means public-key algorithms believed to resist attack by a large quantum computer. NIST published ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) as signature standards in 2024, and the IETF published RFC 9881 in 2025, defining how ML-DSA keys and signatures appear in X.509 certificates and CRLs. A **hybrid** or **composite certificate** carries both a classical and a post-quantum key or signature, so it stays secure if either algorithm holds; the IETF LAMPS working group's composite ML-DSA specification was close to publication in 2026. Chapter 13 covers the algorithms; here the question is operational.

For CLM, PQC is mostly a size and inventory problem. An ML-DSA-65 public key is 1,952 bytes and a signature 3,309 bytes, compared with 64 bytes for an ECDSA P-256 signature. Chains of PQC certificates are several times larger, which affects handshakes, constrained devices, appliance import limits and database fields. Private PKI will move first, because the organisation controls both ends; public WebPKI certificates will follow once browser root programmes accept them, and alternative designs to reduce handshake size are still being worked out in the IETF.

### What to ask vendors now

Ask your CLM vendor, your private CA vendor, your HSM vendor and the makers of your main TLS-terminating devices:

1. **Inventory:** can your discovery and inventory recognise ML-DSA, SLH-DSA and composite algorithm identifiers today, and report algorithm and key size for every certificate, so we can build a cryptographic inventory?
2. **Export:** can you export the inventory in a machine-readable format, such as a CycloneDX cryptography bill of materials (CBOM)?
3. **Policy:** can templates specify PQC and composite key types, and can we change a template's algorithm in one place for all its certificates?
4. **Issuance:** which of your supported CAs can issue ML-DSA or composite certificates, through which protocols (ACME, EST, CMP, REST), and on what roadmap?
5. **Key generation:** where are PQC keys generated, and which HSMs and FIPS 140-3 validated modules support them?
6. **Delivery:** which connectors (load balancers, CDNs, Kubernetes, Windows, Java keystores) can install PQC certificates, and what size limits apply?
7. **Validation:** can post-installation checks verify PQC and composite chains?
8. **Agility:** how long would it take to reissue every certificate under a new algorithm, and have you tested it?

Treat vague answers as a finding. The organisations that will migrate smoothly are those that can already answer "which certificates use which algorithm, where, owned by whom?" from their inventory.

> **Current development.** NIST's draft transition plan (IR 8547) proposes deprecating RSA and elliptic-curve signatures at 112-bit security after 2030 and disallowing them after 2035. Several national cyber agencies have published similar timelines. Note that these are drafts and guidance, and dates may change; plan your inventory and agility work now regardless.

## 19.11 Skills and career path for a certificate automation engineer

A **certificate automation engineer** builds and runs the systems in this Part. The role sits between PKI, platform engineering and operations, and demand for it is growing as lifetimes fall.

### Core skills

- **PKI and TLS fundamentals:** X.509 structure, chains and path validation, extensions (SAN, key usage, extended key usage, AIA, CRL distribution points), revocation, and the TLS handshake. You should be able to read openssl x509 -text output fluently.
- **Protocols:** ACME (including DNS-01, EAB and ARI), EST, SCEP, CMP and CA REST APIs.
- **Scripting and APIs:** Python, PowerShell and Bash; calling REST APIs; parsing JSON; handling retries and rate limits.
- **Infrastructure as code:** Ansible and Terraform, and how certificates and secrets flow through them safely.
- **Platforms:** Kubernetes and cert-manager, Windows and AD CS, Java keystores, at least one major ADC, one CDN and one cloud certificate service.
- **Key protection:** HSM concepts, PKCS#11, key attributes, cloud KMS.
- **Operations:** monitoring, alerting, incident response, ITSM and CMDB.
- **Communication:** explaining to an application team why their renewal failed, and to an auditor why the programme is under control.

### A typical progression

| Level | Focus | Typical responsibilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analyst or operator | Run the service | Respond to expiry alerts, onboard certificates, triage failures with the toolkit in Section 19.6 |
| Engineer | Build integrations | Write connectors and pipelines, extend validation, automate a technology wave |

Useful credentials include vendor training for the CLM platform and CA you use, a Kubernetes certification such as CKA, cloud platform certifications, and broader security certifications for those moving towards architecture. Experience counts for more: a portfolio of automated integrations and a well-run incident are better evidence than any certificate (of the paper kind).

## 19.12 Lab: build an expiry and chain checker, then break a chain

In this lab you build a small private PKI with OpenSSL, run three local TLS servers (one correct, one with a missing intermediate, one about to expire), and write a Python checker that reports expiry and chain problems over a list of endpoints. You need only OpenSSL 3 and Python 3.

1. **Create a root, an intermediate and two leaf certificates.** Save this as mkpki.sh and run it with bash in an empty directory:

    ```bash
    set -e
    openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
      -nodes -keyout root.key -out root.pem -days 3650 \
      -subj "/CN=Lab Root CA" \
      -addext "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE" \
      -addext "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign"
    openssl req -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
      -keyout int.key -out int.csr -subj "/CN=Lab Issuing CA 1"
    openssl x509 -req -in int.csr -CA root.pem -CAkey root.key \
      -CAcreateserial -out int.pem -days 1825 \
      -extfile <(printf "%s\n%s\n" \
        "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE,pathlen:0" \
        "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign")
    openssl req -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
      -keyout leaf.key -out leaf.csr -subj "/CN=localhost"
    openssl x509 -req -in leaf.csr -CA int.pem -CAkey int.key \
      -CAcreateserial -out leaf.pem -days 47 \
      -extfile <(printf "%s\n%s\n" \
        "subjectAltName=DNS:localhost,IP:127.0.0.1" \
        "extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth")
    openssl x509 -req -in leaf.csr -CA int.pem -CAkey int.key \
      -CAcreateserial -out soon.pem -days 5 \
      -extfile <(printf "%s\n" "subjectAltName=DNS:localhost")
    ```

2. **Verify the chain offline.** The first command fails because the intermediate is missing; the second succeeds:

    ```bash
    openssl verify -CAfile root.pem leaf.pem
    openssl verify -CAfile root.pem -untrusted int.pem leaf.pem
    ```

3. **Start three servers** in separate terminals (or with & at the end of each line). Port 8443 sends the full chain, 8444 sends only the leaf (the broken chain), and 8445 sends a certificate that expires in five days:

    ```bash
    openssl s_server -accept 8443 -cert leaf.pem -key leaf.key \
      -cert_chain int.pem -www -quiet
    openssl s_server -accept 8444 -cert leaf.pem -key leaf.key \
      -www -quiet
    openssl s_server -accept 8445 -cert soon.pem -key leaf.key \
      -cert_chain int.pem -www -quiet
    ```

4. **Write the checker.** Save this as certcheck.py. It connects to each endpoint with full verification against the trust anchors you give it. If verification fails, it records the reason, reconnects without verification to read the expiry anyway, and reports OK, WARN or FAIL:

    ```python
    #!/usr/bin/env python3
    """Check expiry and chain validity for a list of TLS endpoints."""
    import argparse, socket, ssl, subprocess, sys
    from datetime import datetime, timezone

    def fetch(host, port, sni, cafile, verify):
        ctx = ssl.create_default_context(cafile=cafile)
        if not verify:
            ctx.check_hostname = False
            ctx.verify_mode = ssl.CERT_NONE
        with socket.create_connection((host, port), timeout=5) as raw:
            with ctx.wrap_socket(raw, server_hostname=sni) as tls:
                return tls.getpeercert(binary_form=True), tls.version()

    def describe(der):
        pem = ssl.DER_cert_to_PEM_cert(der)
        out = subprocess.run(
            ["openssl", "x509", "-noout", "-enddate", "-issuer"],
            input=pem, capture_output=True, text=True,
            check=True).stdout
        fields = dict(l.split("=", 1) for l in out.splitlines())
        end = datetime.strptime(fields["notAfter"],
                                "%b %d %H:%M:%S %Y %Z")
        end = end.replace(tzinfo=timezone.utc)
        days = (end - datetime.now(timezone.utc)).days
        return days, fields["issuer"].strip()

    def check(line, cafile, warn_days):
        parts = line.split(":")
        host = parts[0]
        port = int(parts[1]) if len(parts) > 1 else 443
        sni = parts[2] if len(parts) > 2 else host
        try:
            der, proto = fetch(host, port, sni, cafile, verify=True)
            problem = ""
        except ssl.SSLCertVerificationError as e:
            problem = e.verify_message
            der, proto = fetch(host, port, sni, cafile, verify=False)
        except (OSError, ssl.SSLError) as e:
            return f"FAIL  {line:28} connect error: {e}"
        days, issuer = describe(der)
        status = "OK"
        if problem:
            status = "FAIL"
        elif days < warn_days:
            status = "WARN"
        note = problem or f"expires in {days} days"
        return f"{status:5} {line:28} {proto} {note} [{issuer}]"

    def main():
        p = argparse.ArgumentParser()
        p.add_argument("endpoints", help="file of host:port[:sni]")
        p.add_argument("--cafile", help="trust anchors (default: system)")
        p.add_argument("--warn-days", type=int, default=30)
        a = p.parse_args()
        bad = 0
        for line in open(a.endpoints):
            line = line.strip()
            if not line or line.startswith("#"):
                continue
            result = check(line, a.cafile, a.warn_days)
            bad += not result.startswith("OK")
            print(result)
        sys.exit(1 if bad else 0)

    if __name__ == "__main__":
        main()
    ```

5. **Create an endpoint list and run it.** The fourth line connects to a good server but sends the wrong SNI name, and the fifth points at a port with nothing listening:

    ```bash
    printf "%s\n" localhost:8443 localhost:8444 localhost:8445 \
      127.0.0.1:8443:wrong.example localhost:8449 > endpoints.txt
    python3 certcheck.py endpoints.txt --cafile root.pem
    echo "exit code: $?"
    ```

    You should see output like this:

    ```text
    OK    localhost:8443               TLSv1.3 expires in 46 days
    FAIL  localhost:8444               TLSv1.3 unable to get local
          issuer certificate
    WARN  localhost:8445               TLSv1.3 expires in 4 days
    FAIL  127.0.0.1:8443:wrong.example TLSv1.3 Hostname mismatch,
          certificate is not valid for 'wrong.example'.
    FAIL  localhost:8449               connect error: [Errno 111]
          Connection refused
    exit code: 1
    ```

    (Lines are wrapped here for the page; each result prints on one line with the issuer in square brackets.)

6. **Diagnose the broken chain with the toolkit.** Compare the number of certificates each server sends, and the verify result:

    ```bash
    openssl s_client -connect localhost:8444 -servername localhost \
      -CAfile root.pem -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
      | grep -c "BEGIN CERTIFICATE"
    openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 -servername localhost \
      -CAfile root.pem -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
      | grep -c "BEGIN CERTIFICATE"
    curl -v --cacert root.pem https://localhost:8444/ -o /dev/null
    ```

    Port 8444 sends one certificate and fails with "unable to verify the first certificate"; 8443 sends two and verifies.

7. **Fix it and re-run.** Restart the 8444 server with -cert_chain int.pem and run the checker again. All three local servers on 8443 to 8445 should verify, with 8445 still warning about expiry.

8. **Make the warning a fraction of lifetime.** Extend describe() to also read notBefore, and change the warning rule to "less than one third of lifetime remaining". Then 8445 warns and the 47-day leaf does not, which is the behaviour you want for short-lived certificates.

9. **Simulate a service that was not reloaded.** Issue a new 47-day leaf for the same key, overwrite leaf.pem, and run the checker without restarting the 8443 server. The expiry does not change because the running server still holds the old certificate. Restart it and see the new date. You have reproduced the most common "successful renewal" outage.

**Optional cloud extension.** Point the checker at a handful of your organisation's real public endpoints (with permission) using the system trust store (omit --cafile), and run it on a schedule from a small cloud function or container in two regions to compare what different edge locations serve. Cost warning: scheduled functions, container instances and outbound traffic are billed by the provider; use the free tier where available, set a budget alert, and delete the resources when you finish.

## 19.13 Failure story: DigiCert's 24-hour mass revocation, 2024

On 29 July 2024, DigiCert, one of the largest public CAs, announced that it had to revoke a large number of TLS certificates within 24 hours. The cause was a flaw in one of its domain control validation methods. In the CNAME-based method, the customer publishes a DNS record containing a random value supplied by the CA. The rules require that record to sit under a name beginning with an underscore, so it cannot collide with a real hostname. For several years, one code path had not added the underscore prefix, which meant the validations did not meet the Baseline Requirements. DigiCert found the problem after a customer asked a question about the random values. The affected validations were about 0.4 percent of the total, but they covered 83,267 certificates belonging to 6,807 customers.

The Baseline Requirements give a CA 24 hours to revoke certificates in this situation. Many customers could not replace certificates that fast, because the certificates were installed manually, on appliances, or in systems that needed change approvals and maintenance windows. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) issued an alert warning of possible disruption. DigiCert extended the deadline for some customers running critical infrastructure, citing exceptional circumstances. One healthcare benefits technology company obtained a temporary restraining order from a US court to stop the revocation of its certificates; the order was lifted a few days later after its certificates had been replaced. DigiCert reported that all affected certificates had been revoked by 3 August 2024. Later scans by Censys found that many affected certificates had been in use on public hosts when the incident began.

None of the affected customers had done anything wrong. The certificates were correctly requested and correctly installed. What separated the organisations that shrugged from those that went to court was operational readiness: an inventory that could answer "which of ours are affected and where are they?" within an hour, automation that could replace them without change board meetings, a second CA ready to issue, and clients that did not pin the old certificates.

The lessons map directly onto this chapter. Keep Runbook A ready and rehearsed (Section 19.7). Measure mean time to replace as a KPI (Section 19.4). Use ARI where your CA supports it, so that the next mass revocation arrives as a moved renewal window rather than an emergency (Section 19.8). And treat every manual certificate as a liability that will be called in, at short notice, sooner or later.

## 19.14 Chapter summary

- Running certificate automation is harder than installing it: falling lifetimes, silent failures and dependence on many teams make operations the deciding factor.
- Roll out in phases (foundations, discovery, visibility, pilot, waves by technology, long tail), onboard application owners with a clear pack and a live demonstration, make automated renewals standard changes, and integrate the CLM inventory with the CMDB in both directions.
- A renewal is finished only when post-installation validation over the network confirms the new serial, a complete chain, matching names and key, reachable revocation endpoints and compliant protocols.
- Alert on a fraction of lifetime, escalate by remaining lifetime, and report a small KPI set including automation rate, time-to-renew, expiry incidents and expired certificates still in use.
- Most failures are a short list of patterns: missing intermediates, old trust stores, pinning, partial HA updates, CDN delays, non-exportable keys, rate limits, DCV and SAN errors, and services not reloaded.
- A standard toolkit (openssl s_client, x509, verify; curl -v; keytool; certutil -urlfetch; kubectl and cmctl; the logs at each layer) diagnoses almost all of them.
- Written, rehearsed runbooks for mass revocation and CA distrust, key compromise and emergency replacement turn crises into procedures.
- Readiness for 47-day certificates means automated DCV, ARI support, lifetime-relative triggers and measured rate limits.
- PCI DSS v4.0.1 Requirement 4.2.1.1, NIST SP 800-53, ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and DORA all ask for evidence that a CLM programme produces naturally.
- For PQC, ask vendors now about inventory, algorithm agility, key generation and delivery support for ML-DSA and composite certificates.
