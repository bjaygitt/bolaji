## 13.1 Why the web PKI matters to an enterprise

When a customer opens your banking app or website, their device decides in a few milliseconds whether your server is really you. It makes that decision using a certificate issued by an organisation you chose, checked against a list of trusted organisations that neither you nor the customer controls. That shared system is the **web public key infrastructure (web PKI)**: the set of public certificate authorities, browser and operating system trust stores, rules, audits and logs that lets any client on the internet verify any server's certificate.

Chapter 12 explained what a certificate contains. This chapter explains the institutions behind public certificates, because for an enterprise they are a dependency like a cloud provider. Their rules decide how long your certificates last, how you prove you own your domains, which hostnames become public, and what happens to your services when a CA you rely on is punished.

## 13.2 The players

Five groups make the web PKI work:

| Player | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Certificate authorities (CAs)** | Verify applicants and issue certificates. Most are commercial companies; Let's Encrypt is a non-profit that issues a large share of the web's certificates for free |
| **Root programs** | Run by browser and operating system makers (Apple, Google Chrome, Microsoft, Mozilla). Decide which CA root certificates go into their trust stores, and remove them |
| **The CA/Browser Forum** | An industry body of CAs and browser makers that writes the **Baseline Requirements**, the common rulebook for publicly trusted TLS certificates |
| **Auditors** | Independent firms that audit CAs every year against WebTrust or ETSI criteria |
| **Subscribers and relying parties** | Organisations that request certificates and prove domain control, and the users and software that rely on them |

The central fact of the web PKI is uncomfortable: **any publicly trusted CA can issue a certificate for any domain.** Your bank's website is only as safe as the weakest CA in your customers' trust stores. Almost all the machinery in this chapter exists to manage that risk: rules to limit what CAs may do, audits to check they do it, logs to make their output visible, and the threat of removal to keep them honest.

## 13.3 Root programs hold the real power

A CA is "publicly trusted" only because root programs include its root certificates. Each program publishes its own policy, layered on top of the Baseline Requirements, and can remove a CA whose behaviour falls short. Most programs coordinate through the **Common CA Database (CCADB)**, where CAs disclose their roots, intermediates, audit reports and incident reports. Removal, called **distrust**, is the strongest sanction in the system, and it has been used repeatedly:

| Year | CA | Reason | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2011 | DigiNotar | Hacked; fraudulent certificates for major sites used to spy on users | Removed within days; the company went bankrupt |
| 2015 to 2016 | CNNIC, WoSign and StartCom | Unauthorised intermediate; backdated certificates and concealed ownership | Distrusted |
| 2017 to 2018 | Symantec (and its brands) | Years of mis-issuance and inadequate oversight of partners | Gradually distrusted; business sold |
| 2019 to 2020 | DarkMatter | Concerns about trustworthiness | Inclusion refused |
| 2024 | Entrust | A long pattern of compliance failures and slow incident responses | Chrome, Mozilla and others distrusted new certificates from November 2024 |
| 2025 | Chunghwa Telecom and Netlock | Compliance failures and unmet improvement commitments | Distrusted by Chrome from August 2025 |

Modern distrusts are usually **gradual**: browsers stop trusting certificates issued after a cut-off date, while existing certificates stay valid until they expire. This protects users while giving site operators months to switch CAs. The Entrust case shows the business consequence: Entrust sold its public certificate business to Sectigo in 2025, and enterprises that had installed certificates by hand spent months replacing them.

Root programs also shape what certificates may be used for. Chrome's root program now requires that hierarchies in its store serve **TLS server authentication only**, and public CAs have spent 2025 and 2026 removing the **client authentication** extended key usage from their TLS certificates. Let's Encrypt, for example, stopped including it in its default profile in February 2026 and closed its separate client-authentication profile in July 2026. Any system that used a public TLS certificate as a client certificate for mutual TLS, for instance towards a partner's API, needs a private or dedicated client-certificate CA instead (Chapter 15).

> **In practice.** Every organisation using public certificates should be able to answer: "If our CA were distrusted tomorrow, how long would it take to move every public certificate to another CA?" The honest answer depends on three things: an accurate inventory, automation that can target a different CA, and a pre-established, pre-validated account with that second CA.

## 13.4 The Baseline Requirements and validation

The Baseline Requirements set rules on key sizes, algorithms, certificate contents, validity periods, revocation, audits and, above all, **validation**: how a CA confirms an applicant should get a certificate.

| Level | What the CA verifies | What the certificate shows | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DV (Domain Validated)** | That the applicant controls the domain | Only the domain name | The large majority of web certificates |
| **OV (Organisation Validated)** | Domain control plus that the organisation legally exists | Organisation name and location in the subject | Common for businesses and regulated firms |
| **EV (Extended Validation)** | Domain control plus extensive organisation checks | Detailed organisation information | Browsers removed the special EV indicator around 2019, because studies found users did not notice it |

For an enterprise the choice between DV and OV is about policy, not security: browsers treat both the same way. OV adds organisation details that some partners or auditors expect, and a second validation process whose results also expire.

### Proving domain control

The approved **domain control validation (DCV)** methods fall into a few families:

- **DNS:** publish a random value in a DNS TXT record, or a CNAME that points to a CA-controlled name. The CNAME variant allows **delegated validation**: you point `_acme-challenge.www.example.com` at a zone your automation controls, and never give the automation write access to your main zone.
- **HTTP:** serve a random value at a specific path on the website.
- **TLS-ALPN:** answer a special TLS handshake on port 443.
- **Email and phone:** a link or code sent to a domain contact. These methods have been progressively restricted and suit automation poorly.

Two recent rules harden DCV. Since March 2025, CAs must check domain control from **multiple network perspectives** in different regions (Multi-Perspective Issuance Corroboration, adopted in Ballot SC-067). This defends against attackers who hijack internet routing (BGP) towards one CA's validation server for a few minutes, a technique demonstrated by researchers and used in real cryptocurrency thefts. Since March 2026, CAs must also **validate DNSSEC** when it is present for both CAA and DNS-based validation lookups (Ballot SC-085). That second change has an operational edge: if your zone publishes DNSSEC and its signatures are broken or expired, issuance and renewal now fail.

## 13.5 Shorter lifetimes: the 47-day schedule

Certificate lifetimes have shrunk steadily: from five years before 2015, to three, then two, then 398 days in 2020. In April 2025, the CA/Browser Forum approved **Ballot SC-081**, which shortens them further on a fixed schedule:

| Certificates issued on or after | Maximum validity | Maximum reuse of domain validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before 15 March 2026 | 398 days | 398 days |
| 15 March 2026 | 200 days | 200 days |
| 15 March 2027 | 100 days | 100 days |
| 15 March 2029 | 47 days | 10 days |

The same ballot cut the reuse period for **subject identity information** (the organisation details behind OV and EV certificates) from 825 days to 398 days from March 2026. So the organisation vetting for an OV certificate must now be refreshed roughly every year, and domain control must eventually be re-proven almost every time a certificate is issued.

Why shorten lifetimes? Because revocation works poorly (Chapter 14). A certificate whose key leaks, or that was mis-issued, stays dangerous until it expires. Shorter lifetimes cap that window. They also force automation, which makes it possible to replace every certificate quickly when a CA is distrusted or an algorithm must change, as it will in the post-quantum migration (Chapter 46).

Some CAs are moving faster than the ballot. Let's Encrypt made optional **six-day certificates** generally available in January 2026, which need almost no revocation infrastructure at all. It has also announced its own plan to move its default certificates from 90 days to 64 days in February 2027 and to 45 days in February 2028, with authorisation reuse falling to hours.

## 13.6 What 47 days means for enterprise operations

The schedule is easy to read and hard to absorb. An organisation with 4,000 public certificates that renews each one about 30 days before expiry performs roughly 4,000 renewals a year at 398 days. At 47 days, renewing about two-thirds of the way through each certificate's life, the same estate needs about 47,000 renewals a year: well over a hundred every working day, each with fresh domain validation. No ticket queue survives that. Six operational consequences follow.

**Installation must be automated end to end.** Requesting a certificate automatically is not enough if a person still copies it to a load balancer. Every endpoint needs a delivery path: an ACME client on the host, an API push from a certificate lifecycle management (CLM) platform, or a cloud or CDN service that manages the certificate itself (Chapters 17 to 19).

**Domain validation must be automated too.** With 10-day reuse, nearly every renewal requires a new DCV. The practical answer is DNS-based validation through an API, ideally with CNAME delegation so that the automation can write only challenge records. Email-based validation cannot keep up, and HTTP validation fails for wildcards and for servers that are not reachable from the internet.

**Change management must change.** A ticket and approval for every production certificate replacement produces thousands of change records with no real decision in them. The usual answer is to classify automated certificate renewal as a **standard change**, pre-approved because it follows a tested, monitored procedure, while keeping manual review for new names or new CAs.

**Hard-to-automate endpoints become the problem.** Appliances without APIs, legacy middleware, mainframe front ends and partner-hosted systems are where automation breaks. Each needs either a supported automation path, a move behind a device that can be automated (such as terminating TLS on a load balancer or CDN), or a decision that it should use a private certificate instead.

**Pinning becomes dangerous.** Any client or partner that pins your leaf certificate or key will break far more often. Pin, if at all, to CA keys with a backup.

**Monitoring shifts from expiry to failure.** With short lifetimes, an expiry alert 30 days out is meaningless. You need alerts when a renewal fails, when the served certificate differs from the issued one, and when renewal timing drifts. **ACME Renewal Information (ARI)** lets a CA tell clients when to renew, including telling them to renew early before a mass revocation.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian has about 4,000 public certificates: the customer website and mobile API behind its CDN, open banking partner endpoints, Meridian Pay merchant portals, and a long tail of marketing sites. The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the CA contracts and the CLM platform; Network Engineering owns the load balancers and the CDN configuration; application teams own the services. A "47-day readiness" review sorted every public certificate into three groups: automated end to end, automatable through the CLM platform's connectors, and blocked. The blocked group (about 300 certificates on appliances and partner-facing middleware) became a funded project due in 2027, so that 100-day certificates act as the rehearsal.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 4.2.1.1 asks for an inventory of trusted keys and certificates used to protect cardholder data in transit, and 4.2.1 requires that certificates on those paths are valid and not expired or revoked. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A control 8.24 (use of cryptography) expects a defined key management process.

## 13.7 Certificate Transparency

How would you know if a CA, through error or compromise, issued a certificate for your domain to someone else? Before 2013, you mostly would not. DigiNotar's fraudulent certificates were found only because one Chrome user's browser had a hard-coded expectation for Google's keys.

**Certificate Transparency (CT)**, created at Google and specified in RFC 6962, makes all public certificates visible:

1. Before issuing, the CA submits a **precertificate** to several public **CT logs**, independently operated append-only ledgers built as Merkle trees, so any tampering with history is detectable.
2. Each log returns a **Signed Certificate Timestamp (SCT)**, a signed promise to include the certificate within a set time.
3. The CA embeds the SCTs in the final certificate. Chrome and Safari reject public certificates without enough valid SCTs from independent logs.
4. **Monitors** watch the logs. Anyone can search them through public tools such as crt.sh, and domain owners can subscribe to alerts for new certificates that name their domains.

CT turned mis-issuance from invisible to detectable. For an enterprise it does three jobs: it detects certificates for your names that you did not request, it reveals **shadow IT** (teams buying certificates outside the approved process), and it helps build your inventory of public certificates.

It also has a side effect worth planning for: every hostname in a public certificate becomes public, permanently. Internal names such as `payroll-test.example.com` or `core-banking-dr.example.com` hand attackers a map of your estate. Use a private CA for internal names (Chapter 15), or use wildcards where policy allows and the key exposure is acceptable.

> **Current development.** The CT ecosystem is moving to a newer log design called **static CT** (the "Sunlight" architecture and the static-ct-api specification), which serves logs as static files that are cheaper and more reliable to operate at a volume of billions of certificates. Chrome began accepting these logs in 2025.

## 13.8 CAA: telling CAs who may issue

A **CAA** (Certification Authority Authorization) record is a DNS record that lists which CAs may issue certificates for a domain. CAs must check it before issuing.

```text
example.com.  CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  CAA 0 issue "ca-two.example"
example.com.  CAA 0 issuewild ";"
example.com.  CAA 0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"
```

This example allows two CAs, forbids wildcard certificates entirely, and asks CAs to report refused requests to a security mailbox. Newer CAA parameters can restrict issuance to a specific CA account (`accounturi`) or validation method (`validationmethods`), which makes it much harder for an attacker who controls a web server, but not your CA account, to obtain a certificate.

CAA is also a governance tool: it turns the approved-CA policy into a control that refuses issuance from unapproved CAs, provided CAA changes go through the PKI team.

> **Watch out.** If you keep two CAs so that one can take over when the other fails, CAA must list **both**. A CAA record that lists only your primary CA silently blocks your backup at the moment you need it. Test issuance from the backup CA at least once a year.

## 13.9 Design decisions for public certificates

An architect responsible for public certificates makes a small number of decisions that shape everything else:

- **How many public CAs?** One primary and one tested secondary is the common answer. The secondary needs a contract, pre-validated organisation details, DCV automation and CAA entries, or it is a backup in name only.
- **Where is the boundary between public and private?** Public certificates only where clients you do not control must trust you. Everything else, including service-to-service traffic and internal web applications, goes to a private CA.
- **Which edge services manage certificates themselves?** CDN and cloud-managed certificates remove work, but may sit outside your inventory unless you integrate them.
- **DV or OV?** Decide by policy and partner requirement, and record the reason. The validation level does not change browser behaviour.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian's GRC team owns the approved-CA standard. The PKI team turned it into CAA records on every registered domain, listing the primary CA, the secondary CA and the CDN's CA, each restricted with `accounturi` to Meridian's accounts. The SOC receives CT alerts for every new certificate naming a Meridian domain and checks each against the CLM platform's issuance records; anything without a matching request opens an incident.

## 13.10 Lab: watch the web PKI at work

These steps need only a shell with OpenSSL, `dig` and Python 3.

1. **Search Certificate Transparency.** Open `https://crt.sh` and search for a domain you own or a well-known one. Note how many certificates exist, which CAs issued them, and how often they are renewed. Search for `%.yourdomain.example` to find every subdomain that ever had a public certificate.

2. **Read the SCTs in a live certificate.**

    ```bash
    echo | openssl s_client -connect www.mozilla.org:443 \
      -servername www.mozilla.org 2>/dev/null \
      | openssl x509 -noout -text | grep -A12 "CT Precertificate SCTs"
    ```

3. **Check CAA records.** Compare a few large sites and note which use `accounturi` or `iodef`.

    ```bash
    dig +short CAA google.com
    dig +short CAA github.com
    ```

4. **Measure lifetimes.** For three sites you use, print the validity period and compare it with the SC-081 schedule.

    ```bash
    echo | openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 \
      -servername example.com 2>/dev/null | openssl x509 -noout -dates
    ```

5. **Model your renewal load.** Save this as `renewals.py` and run it with your own certificate count.

    ```python
    certs = 4000
    for life in (398, 200, 100, 47):
        renew_at = life * 2 / 3          # renew two-thirds through
        per_year = certs * 365 / renew_at
        print(f"{life:>3} days: {per_year:>7.0f} renewals/yr, "
              f"{per_year / 250:>5.0f} per working day")
    ```

**Optional cloud extension.** Write a small serverless function in any cloud that polls a CT search API daily for your domains and posts new certificates to a chat channel. Cost warning: function invocations and outbound requests are cheap but not free, and a misconfigured schedule can poll far more often than you intend. Set a budget alert before deploying.

## 13.11 Failure story: DigiNotar

In the summer of 2011, attackers broke into DigiNotar, a Dutch certificate authority whose roots were trusted by every major browser and by the Dutch government's own PKI. They issued over 500 fraudulent certificates, including one for `*.google.com`. That certificate was used to intercept the Gmail traffic of an estimated 300,000 users, mostly in Iran, by sitting in the middle of their connections with a certificate their browsers fully trusted.

The attack was discovered when Chrome, which had Google's expected keys hard-coded (an early form of pinning), refused the fraudulent certificate, and a user reported the error. DigiNotar's own logs had been tampered with, so the company could not even say which certificates had been issued. Within days, every major browser removed its roots. DigiNotar was declared bankrupt within weeks, and the Dutch government had to replace certificates across its own services.

DigiNotar is the reason Certificate Transparency exists and root programs enforce strict incident reporting. It was also the first time a CA's failure became its customers' emergency: every organisation using DigiNotar certificates had to replace them within days, with no automation and no warning.

## 13.12 Chapter summary

- Any publicly trusted CA can issue for any domain; root programs, the Baseline Requirements, audits and transparency manage that risk.
- Root programs can and do distrust CAs, usually with a cut-off date. Keep a tested second CA and the automation to switch.
- Public TLS hierarchies are becoming server-authentication only; client certificates for mTLS belong on a private or dedicated CA.
- DV dominates; domain control is checked from multiple network perspectives, and DNSSEC is validated where present.
- Under SC-081, maximum public lifetime is 200 days from March 2026, 100 days from March 2027 and 47 days from March 2029, with domain validation reuse falling to 10 days.
- At 47 days, renewal volume rises roughly tenfold, so installation, DCV, change management and monitoring must all be automated.
- Certificate Transparency makes every public certificate visible: monitor it for your domains, and keep internal names out of public certificates.
- CAA restricts which CAs, accounts and methods may issue for your domains, and must list your backup CA.
