## 23.1 Why the edge matters

Chapter 22 dealt with networks an enterprise owns or leases, where it chooses the routers, the addressing and the encryption. The public internet is different. A customer's packets cross a consumer ISP and one or more transit carriers, then arrive at a content delivery network that Meridian pays for but does not run. At least four independent systems must behave honestly:

- **DNS** must return the right address for `www.meridian.example`.
- **BGP**, the routing protocol between networks, must deliver packets for that address to the right network.
- The **edge provider** (the CDN and WAF) must terminate TLS with a key that really belongs to Meridian.
- The **origin connection** from the edge back to Meridian's data centre or cloud must itself be authenticated and encrypted.

TLS protects the content even if DNS or routing is subverted, because an attacker who diverts traffic cannot present a valid certificate for Meridian's name. The guarantee is not complete: a customer may click through a warning, and an attacker who controls routing can sometimes pass a public CA's domain-validation checks.

### Private and public networks: two trust models

| Property | Private network (Chapter 22) | Public internet (this chapter) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who runs the path | The enterprise or a contracted carrier | Many independent operators |
| Who names endpoints | Internal DNS, often split-horizon | Public DNS, with optional DNSSEC |
| Who decides routing | The enterprise's own routing policy | BGP between thousands of networks, with optional RPKI |
| Who vouches for server identity | The enterprise's private CA | Publicly trusted CAs, governed by browser root programmes (Chapter 13) |
| Who holds TLS keys | The enterprise's own load balancers | Often a third-party CDN or WAF |
| Typical encryption | IPsec, MACsec, private TLS | Public TLS 1.3, increasingly over QUIC |

On a private network the path is hostile only in limited ways, because you control it. On the public internet you must assume it is hostile everywhere, and decide how far to trust the edge providers you deliberately place in it.

## 23.2 How a request reaches the enterprise

Follow one request. A customer types `www.meridian.example` into a browser.

1. The browser asks a **recursive resolver** (usually run by the ISP, or a public resolver such as Google Public DNS, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 or Quad9) for the address. The resolver walks the DNS hierarchy from the root, to `.example`, to Meridian's authoritative name servers, which are typically run by a managed DNS provider or the CDN.
2. The answer is usually a CNAME pointing into the CDN's namespace, which resolves to an **anycast** address. Anycast means the same IP address is announced in BGP from hundreds of locations, so the customer's packets reach the nearest CDN **point of presence (PoP)**.
3. The browser opens a TLS 1.3 connection to the PoP. The PoP presents a certificate for `www.meridian.example` and proves possession of the matching private key.
4. The CDN's WAF inspects the decrypted HTTP request, applies bot and DDoS rules, and serves cached content if it can.
5. For anything dynamic, the PoP opens a second TLS connection to Meridian's **origin**, the load balancers in DC-East, DC-West or a cloud region, where Chapter 21's termination patterns take over.

> **At Meridian.** Meridian's public names (`www`, the mobile API host, the open banking API host and Meridian Pay's merchant portal) are served through its CDN and WAF provider. The PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the public certificates and decides which keys may sit at the edge. Application teams own the origin services. The SOC watches CDN logs, certificate transparency monitors and BGP alerts. Because no single team sees the whole path, Meridian keeps an edge register listing each public hostname with its DNS provider, CDN configuration, certificate, key custody model and origin.

## 23.3 CDN and WAF key custody

A CDN cannot cache or filter encrypted traffic it cannot read, so it must terminate TLS. To terminate TLS for `www.meridian.example`, it must complete a handshake that proves possession of the private key for a certificate naming that host. That is the core custody question: **where does the private key live, and who can use it?**

### The custody models

There are four common models, from most convenient to most controlled:

1. **Provider-managed certificate.** The CDN obtains a certificate for your name from a public CA (usually by ACME, Chapter 17) and generates and holds the key. You never see the key. Renewal is automatic. This is the default for most customers.
2. **Customer-uploaded certificate.** You obtain the certificate from your chosen CA, then upload the certificate and its private key to the CDN. The key now exists in at least two places, and you must re-upload at every renewal.
3. **Keyless or remote signing.** The certificate is at the edge, but the private key stays on a key server you run, often backed by an HSM. The edge sends a signing request to your key server during each handshake (section 23.4).
4. **Regionally restricted keys.** The provider holds the key but stores it only in PoPs in chosen jurisdictions, so a handshake landing elsewhere is relayed to an allowed region for signing.

Each step down the list reduces the provider's exposure and adds latency, cost and operational work for you.

### How the major providers do it

The table summarises public documentation as of 2026. Features and names change, so confirm with each provider before designing around them.

| Provider | Default model | Customer-supplied keys | Keys kept off the edge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Akamai | Akamai generates and holds keys in its key management infrastructure for its secure CDN | Akamai documentation describes keys generated inside its platform rather than imported | Platform-controlled; keys are not handed to customers |
| Cloudflare | Universal or advanced certificates, keys managed by Cloudflare | Custom certificate upload | Keyless SSL with a customer key server, which can use an HSM over PKCS#11; Geo Key Manager for regional restriction |
| Fastly | Fastly-managed certificates | Upload of customer keys and certificates through the web interface or API, including Platform TLS for large estates | Not a standard documented option; check current offerings |
| Amazon CloudFront | Certificates from AWS Certificate Manager, which must be requested or imported in the US East (N. Virginia) region | Import of certificate and private key into ACM | Not offered; the key is held by AWS |

Every model except keyless gives the provider a usable copy of the key. That is not automatically a problem: these providers are audited and many publish PCI DSS attestations. The decision is usually about **legal and regulatory control**: a bank's regulator or policy may require keys for banking hosts to stay in HSMs it controls, or within a jurisdiction.

### The second leg: edge to origin

The CDN-to-origin connection deserves as much care. Three controls matter:

- **Encrypt the origin leg with a properly validated certificate.** Some CDNs allow a "flexible" or "full but not strict" mode that encrypts to the origin without validating the origin's certificate, or does not encrypt at all. Never use those modes for anything sensitive.
- **Authenticate the CDN to the origin.** Configure the origin to require a client certificate that only the CDN presents (Cloudflare calls this authenticated origin pulls; other providers offer origin mTLS or signed headers). Otherwise an attacker who discovers the origin's address can bypass the WAF entirely.
- **Restrict origin reachability.** Allow inbound traffic only from the CDN's published address ranges, or use a private connection or tunnel from the provider.

> **Watch out.** An origin that accepts direct internet connections makes the WAF optional. Attackers search certificate transparency logs and historical DNS for origin hostnames, so enforce "reachable only by the CDN" both in the firewall and cryptographically.

## 23.4 Keyless SSL and edge HSMs

Keyless SSL, announced by Cloudflare in 2014, separates the two things a TLS server does with its certificate: present it, and prove possession of its key. The edge keeps doing the first. The second is delegated.

### How it works

In TLS 1.3 the server proves possession of its key by signing a hash of the handshake transcript in the **CertificateVerify** message (Chapter 20). With keyless SSL:

1. The client connects to the PoP and sends its ClientHello.
2. The PoP builds the server's side of the handshake, including the key exchange, and computes the transcript hash that needs signing.
3. The PoP sends that hash over a mutually authenticated TLS connection to the customer's **key server**.
4. The key server checks the request, signs the hash with the private key (in software or through PKCS#11 to an HSM), and returns the signature.
5. The PoP places the signature in CertificateVerify and finishes the handshake. Session keys are derived at the PoP, so the PoP can read and filter traffic as normal.

The key server never sees session keys or customer data, and the PoP never sees the private key. The CDN gets a signing **oracle**: revoke its client certificate at the key server and it can no longer impersonate you.

Cloudflare's key server reaches HSMs through PKCS#11 URIs (RFC 7512), and its documentation lists tested network and cloud HSMs.

### The costs

Keyless signing is not free:

- **Latency.** Each full handshake needs a round trip from the PoP to your key server.
- **Availability.** If the key server fails, new TLS connections fail globally, so you need key servers in several regions.
- **Capacity under attack.** A handshake flood consumes your HSM's signing capacity. Favour ECDSA P-256 keys, which are far cheaper to sign with than RSA 2048.
- **Operational ownership.** You now run a tier-zero service, including the key server's own mTLS certificates and HSM firmware.

### Edge HSMs and their alternatives

"Edge HSM" is used loosely: the customer's HSM used remotely, the provider's HSMs inside its key system, or hardware in the PoP servers. When a provider says keys are "protected by HSMs", ask which keys (the TLS keys, or only the keys that wrap them), whose HSMs, which FIPS 140-3 level, and whether the key is ever in server memory at the edge (Chapters 33 and 34).

> **In practice.** Use keyless or customer-held keys only where a requirement demands it, typically banking and payment hostnames. Elsewhere, provider-managed certificates with automatic renewal are safer overall, because renewal outages cause far more incidents than CDN key compromise.

## 23.5 DNSSEC: signing the phone book

DNS was designed without authentication. A resolver accepts any answer that matches the question and arrives on the expected port. **Cache poisoning** attacks, made famous by Dan Kaminsky's 2008 disclosure, inject forged answers so that a resolver sends every user of that resolver to an attacker's address. Source port randomisation made poisoning harder, but it did not make answers authentic.

**DNSSEC** (DNS Security Extensions) adds digital signatures to DNS data. It does not encrypt anything. It lets a validating resolver check that an answer really came from the zone's owner and was not altered.

### Signing: the records involved

DNSSEC adds a few record types, defined in RFC 4034:

- **RRSIG** holds a signature over a set of records of the same name and type (an **RRset**), with an inception and expiry time.
- **DNSKEY** holds the zone's public keys.
- **DS** (Delegation Signer) holds a hash of a child zone's key and is published in the **parent** zone. This is the link that builds the chain.
- **NSEC** and **NSEC3** prove that a name or type does not exist, by signing statements about the gaps between existing names. NSEC3 hashes the names to make zone walking harder.

### KSK and ZSK

Most zones use two kinds of key:

- The **zone signing key (ZSK)** signs the ordinary RRsets in the zone.
- The **key signing key (KSK)** signs only the DNSKEY RRset, which contains both keys.

The parent's DS record points at the KSK. The split exists for operational reasons. Changing the KSK requires a change at the parent (a new DS record through the registrar), which is slow and involves another organisation. Changing the ZSK is entirely internal. So the ZSK can be smaller and rotated often, while the KSK is rotated rarely and can be held in an HSM. Some operators use a single **combined signing key (CSK)** for simplicity, especially with ECDSA P-256 (algorithm 13), which is the most common modern choice. Ed25519 (algorithm 15) is standardised but less widely validated.

### Validation: following the chain of trust

A validating resolver starts with a **trust anchor**: the root zone's KSK, configured in the resolver software. To validate `www.meridian.example`:

1. It fetches the root DNSKEY set and checks it against the trust anchor.
2. It fetches the DS record for `example` from the root, signed by the root's ZSK, and checks that it matches `example`'s KSK.
3. It repeats the step from `example` to `meridian.example`.
4. It checks the RRSIG on the `www` answer with `meridian.example`'s ZSK.

If every link verifies, the resolver marks the answer **secure** and, if asked, sets the AD (authenticated data) flag. If a signature fails or has expired, the resolver returns SERVFAIL. The user sees the site as down. That is deliberate: failing closed is the point, but it means that DNSSEC mistakes are outages.

DNSSEC has a well-known gap: validation usually happens at the recursive resolver, not on the user's device. The link from the device to the resolver is unprotected unless it uses DNS over HTTPS or DNS over TLS. For an enterprise, the important resolvers are its own: internal resolvers that validate protect every employee and server behind them.

### Rollovers

Keys must change, and DNSSEC rollovers are a classic source of outages because cached data lingers for its TTL. The standard methods are described in RFC 6781 and their timing in RFC 7583:

- **ZSK pre-publish.** Publish the new ZSK in the DNSKEY set, wait until caches have seen it, switch signing to the new key, wait until signatures made with the old key have expired from caches, then remove the old key.
- **KSK double-DS or double-signature.** Introduce the new KSK, publish its DS at the parent, wait for the old DS to age out of caches, then retire the old KSK. Each wait is driven by TTLs, including the parent's TTL on the DS record, which the child does not control.
- **Algorithm rollovers** (for example from RSA to ECDSA) need extra care, because validators expect every RRset to be signed with every algorithm listed in the DNSKEY set.

The root zone's own KSK is the ultimate trust anchor, and changing it is an internet-wide event. The first root KSK rollover took place on 11 October 2018 after a one-year postponement. Resolvers that follow **RFC 5011** automated trust anchor updates learn a new key by seeing it published and signed by the old key for a hold-down period of 30 days.

> **Current development.** ICANN published the second root KSK, KSK-2024 (key tag 38696), in the root zone in January 2025, and scheduled the switch to signing with it alone for 11 October 2026. ICANN has advised resolver operators to check their trust anchor files rather than assume RFC 5011 updates worked. If you run validating resolvers, including appliances and resolvers embedded in firewalls, confirm that KSK-2024 is present.

### DNSSEC in products and in practice

Managed DNS providers make signing largely automatic: Amazon Route 53, Google Cloud DNS, Cloudflare DNS, Akamai Edge DNS and NS1 all offer DNSSEC signing, usually with the provider holding the ZSK and either the provider or the customer holding the KSK. Route 53, for example, uses a customer-managed KMS key as the KSK and manages the ZSK itself. On-premises, BIND, Knot DNS and PowerDNS support automated signing and key rollover policies, with the KSK optionally in an HSM through PKCS#11.

Two warnings from experience. First, CDNs often answer at the zone apex with synthesised records, and some signing setups handle CNAME flattening, wildcards and negative answers in subtle ways. Test these cases with validating resolvers before publishing the DS. Second, the DS record at the parent is the switch that turns validation on, and removing it does not turn validation off until the parent's DS TTL has passed. Plan the rollback before the rollout (see the failure story in section 23.11).

DNSSEC also enables other protocols. **DANE** uses DNSSEC-signed TLSA records to bind certificates to names, and is used mainly for email server authentication (Chapter 25). **HTTPS** and **SVCB** records (RFC 9460) carry connection hints, including ECH configuration (section 23.8), and benefit from being signed.

## 23.6 BGP security and RPKI

**BGP** (Border Gateway Protocol) is how tens of thousands of independent networks, each identified by an **autonomous system number (ASN)**, tell each other which IP prefixes they can reach. BGP was built on trust: a network announces "I originate 198.51.100.0/24", and its neighbours generally believe it.

That trust fails in two ways:

- A **route hijack**: a network announces a prefix it does not hold, accidentally or deliberately. Because routers prefer more specific prefixes, announcing a /24 inside someone's /22 pulls traffic away even where the legitimate route exists.
- A **route leak**: a network re-announces routes it learned from one provider to another, in violation of routing policy, so traffic takes an unintended path.

A well-documented example: in April 2018, attackers hijacked IP prefixes belonging to Amazon Route 53's DNS service for about two hours. Resolvers that followed the hijacked routes received forged answers for a cryptocurrency wallet site, and users who clicked through a certificate warning gave up their credentials and funds. TLS did its job: the attackers had no valid certificate. People overrode it.

### RPKI: certificates for address space

The **Resource Public Key Infrastructure (RPKI)**, described in RFC 6480, is a PKI whose certificates describe who holds which IP addresses and ASNs. Its roots are the five Regional Internet Registries (AFRINIC, APNIC, ARIN, LACNIC and RIPE NCC), which already allocate those resources. A holder of address space receives a **resource certificate** listing its prefixes, and uses it to sign objects.

The key object is the **Route Origin Authorisation (ROA)**, now profiled in RFC 9582. A ROA says: "the holder of this prefix authorises this ASN to originate it, up to this maximum prefix length." For example, a ROA might authorise AS64500 to originate 203.0.113.0/24 with a maximum length of /24.

The **maxLength** field matters. Setting it longer than you announce (for example authorising up to /32 when you only announce a /24) lets an attacker announce a more specific prefix with your ASN forged as origin, and the forged route still validates. Keep maxLength equal to what you actually announce.

Holders can use **hosted RPKI**, where the RIR runs the certificate authority and the holder creates ROAs in a web portal, or **delegated RPKI**, where the holder runs its own CA (for example with the open-source Krill) under the RIR's certificate. Hosted is right for almost every enterprise.

### Route origin validation

Signed ROAs are useless unless routers check them. **Route origin validation (ROV)**, defined in RFC 6811, works like this:

1. A **relying party** validator (such as Routinator, rpki-client or FORT) downloads all RPKI repositories, verifies every certificate and signature back to the RIR trust anchors, and produces a list of validated ROA payloads.
2. Routers fetch that list from the validator using the RPKI-to-Router protocol, which keeps the cryptography off the routers.
3. For each received route, the router assigns a state: **Valid** (a ROA matches prefix, length and origin), **Invalid** (a ROA covers the prefix but the origin or length does not match), or **NotFound** (no ROA covers it).
4. Routing policy drops Invalid routes.

Many large transit providers and cloud networks now drop Invalid routes, so a published ROA genuinely protects your prefixes against origin hijacks across a large share of the internet. ROV does not validate the path: an attacker can still forge a route that appears to originate from your ASN. **BGPsec** (RFC 8205) signs the whole path but has seen almost no deployment. **ASPA** (Autonomous System Provider Authorisation), still an IETF draft as of 2026 but already supported by several RIRs and validators, lets each network publish its upstream providers so that leaks and some forged paths can be detected.

> **At Meridian.** Network Engineering holds two provider-independent IPv4 prefixes and one IPv6 prefix, announced from both data centres through two transit providers. It has published ROAs in the RIR's hosted RPKI for each announced prefix, with maxLength matching the announcement, and uses a BGP monitoring service that alerts the SOC on any unexpected origin or more-specific announcement. Meridian's customer-facing services mostly sit behind the CDN's anycast addresses, so the CDN's own RPKI hygiene matters as much as Meridian's. That question is in the CDN contract review.

## 23.7 DDoS and TLS

Distributed denial of service attacks target availability, but TLS shapes both the attack and the defence.

### Why TLS is expensive to attack and to defend

A TLS handshake costs the server far more than the client. The client can send a ClientHello for almost nothing, while the server must perform a key exchange and a signature. Attackers exploit this asymmetry with **handshake floods**. Defences include:

- **Session resumption and 0-RTT** reduce repeat handshakes for legitimate clients (with the replay caveats from Chapter 20).
- **ECDSA certificates** cut the signing cost per handshake.
- **Terminating at the edge** spreads the cost across a CDN's global capacity instead of concentrating it on the origin.

### Layer 7 attacks need decryption

Volumetric attacks (floods of packets measured in terabits per second) can be absorbed by any large network without decrypting anything. **Application-layer attacks** look like ordinary HTTPS requests, and they can only be filtered by something that can read them. That forces a choice:

- **Always-on CDN protection** already terminates TLS, so it can filter layer 7 attacks immediately.
- **On-demand scrubbing services** reroute traffic during an attack, often by BGP announcement. To filter layer 7, they need your certificate and key in advance, or a keyless arrangement, or they can only forward encrypted traffic and filter on volume.

The HTTP/2 **Rapid Reset** attack, disclosed in October 2023, showed how protocol features become attack amplifiers: clients opened and immediately cancelled streams, generating record request rates. It was mitigated in CDNs and web servers within days, but the lesson for key custody is that your layer 7 defence lives wherever your TLS terminates.

> **Watch out.** If you use an on-demand DDoS scrubbing provider and keyless SSL, your key server must be reachable and sized for the attack itself. Test the failover path, including key access, before you need it, and keep the scrubbing provider's certificates and keys in your certificate inventory like any other deployment.

## 23.8 Encrypted Client Hello, QUIC and HTTP/3

Two protocol changes reshape what the network can see.

### QUIC and HTTP/3

**QUIC** (RFC 9000) is a transport protocol that runs over UDP and builds in TLS 1.3 (RFC 9001). **HTTP/3** (RFC 9114) is HTTP carried over QUIC. QUIC encrypts nearly all of its own transport headers, not only the payload, so middleboxes cannot see packet numbers or acknowledgements. Its benefits are faster connection setup, no head-of-line blocking between streams, and connections that survive a change of client address, such as a phone moving from Wi-Fi to mobile data.

For the enterprise, QUIC matters in two places:

- **At the edge**, the major CDNs support HTTP/3, and enabling it is usually a configuration switch. The certificates and keys are the same as for TLS over TCP.
- **Inside the corporate network**, TLS inspection proxies (Chapter 21) often cannot inspect QUIC. Most enterprises that inspect traffic block UDP port 443 outbound so browsers fall back to TCP, which they do automatically. That is a valid policy, but it should be a deliberate one.

### Encrypted Client Hello

In TLS 1.3, the server's certificate is encrypted, but the ClientHello, including the **Server Name Indication (SNI)** that names the site, is still in clear text. **Encrypted Client Hello (ECH)**, published as RFC 9849, closes that gap. It works like this:

1. The server operator (usually a CDN) publishes an **ECHConfig**, containing a public key, in the domain's DNS HTTPS record.
2. The client builds two ClientHellos: an **inner** one with the real SNI and sensitive extensions, encrypted with HPKE (RFC 9180) under that public key, and an **outer** one naming a shared public name, such as the CDN's generic hostname.
3. The CDN's front end decrypts the inner ClientHello and completes the handshake for the real site. An observer sees only the outer name.

ECH works best when many sites share one front end, because the outer name then reveals nothing about which site was chosen. That is why CDNs lead deployment.

ECH matters to an enterprise in both directions. As a publisher, enabling ECH on public sites improves customer privacy at little cost. As a network operator, ECH removes the SNI that secure web gateways and firewalls use for category filtering without full decryption. Options include running managed endpoints whose browsers have ECH disabled by policy, forcing traffic through an explicit or inspecting proxy, or controlling which DNS resolvers clients can use, because the client needs the HTTPS record to use ECH.

> **Current development.** Post-quantum hybrid key exchange (X25519MLKEM768, Chapter 46) is now enabled by default in major browsers, and the large CDNs accept it at the edge. A customer's first TLS leg is therefore often already quantum-safe for confidentiality, while the CDN-to-origin leg may not be. Check what your origin load balancers negotiate.

## 23.9 Design decisions, operations and compliance

The architect's decisions at the edge come down to a short list:

- **Which hostnames go through the CDN**, and which custody model each uses. Tie the model to data classification, not to habit.
- **How the origin is protected**: validated certificate, CDN client authentication and network restriction, all three.
- **Whether DNSSEC is signed and validated.** Signing public zones is now well supported by managed DNS. Validation on internal resolvers protects the workforce.
- **Whether ROAs exist** for every prefix you announce, with correct maxLength, and whether your providers drop Invalid routes.
- **What the inspection policy is** for QUIC and ECH on the corporate network.

Operationally, add edge certificates, origin certificates, keyless mTLS certificates and DNSSEC keys to the certificate and key inventory (Chapters 17 to 19). Monitor certificate transparency for your names, DNSSEC signature expiry, RPKI ROA expiry, and BGP announcements of your prefixes.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 4.2.1 requires strong cryptography for cardholder data over open, public networks and that certificates are valid and not expired; requirement 4.2.1.1 requires an inventory of trusted keys and certificates; requirement 12.8 covers service providers such as CDNs, including their PCI DSS status. DORA's ICT third-party risk requirements apply to CDN and DNS providers that support critical functions. NIST SP 800-81-2 gives DNSSEC deployment guidance, and NIST SP 800-189 covers BGP security and RPKI. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A controls 8.20 to 8.22 (networks security, security of network services, segregation) and 5.19 to 5.22 (supplier relationships) map to the custody and provider decisions in this chapter.

## 23.10 Lab: validate DNSSEC and RPKI from your laptop

You need `dig` and `delv` (from the BIND utilities) and Docker.

**1. Look at a signed zone.** Ask for DNSSEC records and see the RRSIG alongside the answer.

```bash
dig +dnssec www.ietf.org A
dig ietf.org DNSKEY +multi
dig ietf.org DS @a.gtld-servers.net +norec
```

The DNSKEY output labels keys as KSK (flag 257) or ZSK (flag 256). Note the key tags and match the DS record's key tag to the KSK.

**2. Validate the chain yourself.** `delv` performs full validation from the root trust anchor.

```bash
delv www.ietf.org A
delv dnssec-failed.org A
```

The first should report "fully validated". The second is a deliberately broken test domain and should fail validation.

**3. Sign a zone locally.** Run BIND in a container and create a KSK and ZSK.

```bash
mkdir lab23 && cd lab23
cat > db.lab.test <<'EOF'
$TTL 300
@   IN SOA ns1.lab.test. admin.lab.test. 1 3600 600 86400 300
    IN NS  ns1.lab.test.
ns1 IN A   192.0.2.53
www IN A   192.0.2.80
EOF
docker run --rm -v "$PWD":/w -w /w --entrypoint sh \
  internetsystemsconsortium/bind9:9.18 -c '
    dnssec-keygen -a ECDSAP256SHA256 -f KSK lab.test &&
    dnssec-keygen -a ECDSAP256SHA256 lab.test &&
    for k in Klab.test*.key; do cat "$k" >> db.lab.test; done &&
    dnssec-signzone -o lab.test -N INCREMENT db.lab.test'
grep -A2 RRSIG db.lab.test.signed | head -20
cat dsset-lab.test.
```

The `dsset` file holds the DS record you would give your registrar. Look at the RRSIG inception and expiry times: they are why signed zones must be re-signed on a schedule.

**4. Check route origin validation.** Run the Routinator validator, which downloads and verifies the global RPKI.

```bash
docker run --rm nlnetlabs/routinator:latest \
  validate --asn 13335 --prefix 1.1.1.0/24
docker run --rm nlnetlabs/routinator:latest \
  validate --asn 64500 --prefix 1.1.1.0/24
```

The first initial sync takes a few minutes. The first query should report Valid and the second Invalid, because a ROA covers the prefix but authorises a different ASN.

**5. See QUIC.** If your curl build supports HTTP/3, compare:

```bash
curl -sI --http3 https://cloudflare-quic.com | head -1
curl -sI --http2 https://cloudflare-quic.com | head -1
```

**Optional cloud extension.** Create a hosted zone in a cloud DNS service, enable DNSSEC signing, and inspect the KSK and DS records it generates. Use a test domain you own. Hosted zones and KMS keys carry small monthly charges, and a domain registration costs money. Delete the DS record at the registrar first, wait for its TTL, and only then disable signing, or the domain will stop resolving.

## 23.11 Failure story: Slack's DNSSEC rollout

In 2021 Slack set out to sign `slack.com` with DNSSEC, partly to meet a US government compliance requirement. Slack's engineering team later published a detailed account of what went wrong.

The first two attempts in September 2021 were rolled back for unrelated reasons and for a problem with a CNAME at the zone apex. On 30 September, the third attempt published the DS record for `slack.com` in the `.com` zone, which switched on validation for every DNSSEC-validating resolver in the world. Some users then began to fail to reach Slack. The cause turned out to be a bug in the managed DNS provider's handling of wildcard records: Slack's zone had a wildcard with A records but no AAAA records, and the signed negative answer for an AAAA query wrongly claimed that the name had no records of any type. Resolvers that cache negative proofs aggressively, Google Public DNS among them, then refused to return the A record as well.

Slack rolled back by removing the DS record and unsigning the zone. That made things worse for the affected resolvers. The `.com` zone's DS record had a TTL of a day, so validating resolvers that had cached it kept expecting signatures that no longer existed, and returned SERVFAIL. Slack could not undo the change from its side. The outage affected under 1% of its users but lasted about 24 hours, and recovery required asking large resolver operators to flush their caches. The provider later fixed the wildcard bug, and Slack signed the zone successfully in 2022.

The lessons are general:

- **The DS record is a one-way door for its TTL.** Plan rollback before rollout, and understand that "unsigning" while the DS is cached is itself an outage.
- **Test the hard cases** (wildcards, apex records, negative answers for missing types) against several validating resolvers before publishing the DS.
- **Know your provider's implementation.** A managed DNS service's signing behaviour is part of your DNSSEC design.
- **Short TTLs during change** reduce the window of harm, but you do not control the parent's TTL.

## 23.12 Chapter summary

- Public-internet traffic depends on DNS, BGP, an edge provider and the origin connection, each run by different parties; TLS protects content but not every diversion.
- CDNs must terminate TLS to cache and filter, so key custody is a design choice: provider-managed, uploaded, keyless or regionally restricted.
- Keyless SSL gives the edge a signing oracle rather than a key, at the cost of latency, availability and running a tier-zero key service.
- Protect the edge-to-origin leg with validated certificates, CDN client authentication and network restriction.
- DNSSEC signs DNS data with ZSKs and KSKs chained from the root trust anchor; validation fails closed, so rollovers and rollbacks must respect TTLs.
- RPKI ROAs, checked by route origin validation, protect prefixes against origin hijacks; maxLength must match what you announce, and path security is still emerging.
- DDoS defence at layer 7 lives wherever TLS terminates, which ties scrubbing decisions to key custody.
- QUIC and ECH improve performance and privacy but reduce what corporate inspection can see, so the enterprise needs an explicit policy for both.
