## 26.1 Why storage encryption matters

Data at rest outlives almost everything around it. Retired disks sit in returns cages long after their servers are gone, backup tapes are kept for ten years, and a cloud snapshot can be copied to another account in seconds. **Storage encryption** is the family of controls that make the bits on these media useless to anyone who obtains the media without the keys.

That last clause is the whole story. Storage encryption protects against people who get the *storage* but not the *system*. It does nothing against an attacker who logs in to a running server, because the operating system decrypts data transparently for anyone it considers authorised. Most breaches happen through running systems, which is why "encrypted at rest" on an audit report often means less than it appears to. The architect's job is to know exactly which threat each layer removes, and to stop paying for layers that remove the same threat twice.

There are still strong reasons to encrypt storage everywhere: laptops are lost and drives go back to vendors; disposal is far easier to prove when the key is destroyed; snapshots and backups travel to places with weaker access control; and breach-notification laws and frameworks from PCI DSS to HIPAA treat encrypted data differently.

Read the figure from the bottom. A **self-encrypting drive** protects the flash chips once powered off, but anyone reading the volume through the operating system sees plaintext. **Volume encryption** protects a copied disk image or a laptop stolen while shut down, not a logged-in session. **Database encryption** protects data files and backups, not a query from an authorised account. **Application-layer encryption** protects data even from database administrators, because the database stores only ciphertext.

Two rules follow from the stack:

1. **A layer only defends against attackers who reach the data below it without passing through it.** Ransomware running as a domain administrator passes through every layer from the volume down.
2. **Lower layers are cheap and broad; higher layers are expensive and targeted.** Turn on the bottom layers everywhere because they cost almost nothing, then add higher layers for the data that justifies them.

> **At Meridian.** The PKI and Cryptography Services team keeps a one-page "layer map" for each data class. Card data in Meridian Pay is encrypted at the device, volume and application layers; insurance claims documents at the device, volume and database layers; general file shares at the device and volume layers only. GRC uses the map to answer auditors' "is it encrypted?" questions with "at which layer, against which threat, with whose keys?"

## 26.3 Full-disk encryption: BitLocker, FileVault and LUKS

**Full-disk encryption (FDE)**, also called volume encryption, encrypts every sector of a volume with a symmetric key. Because a disk must be readable at any sector without reading its neighbours, FDE uses a tweakable block cipher mode, almost always **XTS-AES**, in which the sector number acts as a tweak so that identical plaintext in different sectors produces different ciphertext (Chapter 6). XTS provides confidentiality but not integrity: an attacker who can write to the raw disk can corrupt sectors undetected. That is an accepted trade-off for disks, because there is no room to store an authentication tag per sector.

Every mainstream FDE product uses the same two-level key hierarchy:

- A **volume master key** (the name varies) encrypts the data. It is generated once and rarely changes, because changing it means re-encrypting the whole disk.
- The volume master key is stored on the disk, wrapped by one or more **protectors**: a TPM-sealed key, a password, a smart card, a recovery key. Changing a password only re-wraps the master key.

This design is why the **recovery key** matters so much. It is just another protector, and an enterprise must escrow it centrally, or a failed TPM or forgotten password turns a laptop into a brick.

### BitLocker

**BitLocker**, built into Windows, encrypts volumes with XTS-AES (128-bit by default, 256-bit by policy). Its full volume encryption key is wrapped by a volume master key, which is protected by one or more of: the TPM alone, TPM plus PIN, TPM plus startup key, a password, or a 48-digit recovery password. The TPM protector seals the key to measurements of the boot chain (Chapter 36), so the disk unlocks automatically only on the original, unmodified machine. Enterprises escrow recovery passwords to Active Directory or Microsoft Entra ID and manage policy through Group Policy, Intune or another unified endpoint management tool. On Windows Server, BitLocker protects local and cluster volumes the same way.

TPM-only mode boots to the login screen without any user secret, leaving the key exposed to memory and bus-sniffing attacks on some hardware, so high-risk users should use TPM plus PIN.

### FileVault

**FileVault** is Apple's volume encryption for macOS. On Macs with Apple silicon or the T2 chip, internal storage is always encrypted by a hardware engine with keys held by the **Secure Enclave**; turning on FileVault adds the requirement that a user credential is needed to unwrap those keys. Recovery uses a personal recovery key, which an enterprise escrows to its mobile device management platform (Jamf Pro, Microsoft Intune, Kandji and others support this).

### LUKS

On Linux, **LUKS** (Linux Unified Key Setup) is the on-disk format used with the kernel's dm-crypt. A LUKS2 header holds up to 32 **key slots**, each containing the volume key wrapped under a different passphrase or token, with passphrases stretched by Argon2id by default. Servers rarely have a human to type a passphrase, so enterprises use one of:

- **TPM binding**, with `systemd-cryptenroll` or Clevis, which seals a key slot to the TPM.
- **Network-bound disk encryption** with **Clevis and Tang**: the server can unlock only while it can reach a Tang server on the internal network, so a server carried out of the data centre stays locked.
- **Key managers** that hand out passphrases at boot, often over KMIP.

The LUKS header is a single point of failure: if it is overwritten, the data is gone even with the correct passphrase. Back it up (the lab shows how), and protect the backup like a key.

## 26.4 Self-encrypting drives and TCG Opal

A **self-encrypting drive (SED)** performs encryption inside the drive controller. Every block written is encrypted with a **media encryption key (MEK)** that is generated inside the drive and never leaves it. Encryption is always on; the question is only whether the MEK is locked.

The **Trusted Computing Group (TCG)** publishes the specifications most SEDs follow. **Opal** (the Opal Security Subsystem Class) is the common one for client drives, and **Enterprise** for data-centre drives. In an Opal drive:

- The MEK is wrapped by a key derived from an authentication credential, sometimes called the key encryption key.
- When locking is enabled, the drive powers up locked and refuses reads until a host supplies the credential, often through a small pre-boot environment called the **shadow MBR**.
- The drive can define **locking ranges**, each with its own key and credential.
- **Cryptographic erase** replaces the MEK with a new random key. The old ciphertext remains on the media but is unreadable, and erasing a multi-terabyte drive takes seconds.

The attractions are no CPU cost, no agent, and fast sanitisation. NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2, published in September 2025, keeps cryptographic erase as a main purge technique and refers to IEEE 2883 for media-specific methods.

### What the 2018 research found

In November 2018, Carlo Meijer and Bernard van Gastel of Radboud University disclosed an analysis of hardware encryption on SSDs from Crucial and Samsung, later published as "Self-Encrypting Deception" at the 2019 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy. By reverse-engineering firmware, they found that many models had design and implementation flaws that allowed full data recovery without the password. Problems included:

- The password check happened in firmware, but the MEK was **not cryptographically derived from the password**, so patching the check unlocked the data.
- Some drives accepted an empty or default master password.
- A single key protected the whole disk even when several locking ranges were configured.
- Wear-levelling left copies of key material in unallocated flash.

The finding mattered far beyond those drives because **BitLocker at the time delegated encryption to the drive whenever it advertised SED support**. Organisations that believed they had BitLocker software encryption actually had the drive vendor's firmware encryption. Microsoft published advisory ADV180028 the same month, advising administrators to enforce software encryption by Group Policy, and in September 2019 changed Windows so that BitLocker uses software encryption by default on newly encrypted drives.

The lessons are general. Verify which layer is doing the work (on Windows, `manage-bde -status` reports "Hardware Encryption" when the drive's engine is in use). Prefer FIPS 140-3 validated or Common Criteria evaluated drives, whose key derivation has been reviewed. Layer software encryption over SEDs for high-risk endpoints, and treat SED cryptographic erase as a fast decommissioning step rather than the only confidentiality control.

## 26.5 SAN and NAS array encryption

Enterprise storage arrays offer encryption in two places, and the products differ mainly in which they use and how they hold keys.

- **Drive-level encryption** uses SEDs inside the array. The array controller holds the drive credentials and unlocks drives at boot. It protects drives removed from the array, for repair or theft.
- **Controller-level (software) encryption** encrypts data in the array's software before it reaches the drives. It works with ordinary drives, can encrypt per volume or per tenant, and can interact with compression and deduplication, which must happen before encryption because ciphertext does not compress.

Keys come either from a **built-in key manager** in the array or from an **external key manager** over **KMIP** (the Key Management Interoperability Protocol, an OASIS standard). The external option puts keys in a separate system, usually HSM-backed, with its own administrators, audit trail and backup. Chapter 34 covers enterprise key managers themselves; here the concern is how arrays use them.

| Vendor family (public docs, as of 2026) | Encryption options | Key management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **NetApp ONTAP** | NetApp Storage Encryption (SED drives); NetApp Volume Encryption (one XTS-AES-256 key per volume); NetApp Aggregate Encryption | Onboard Key Manager, or external KMIP key servers, which can be clustered and configured per storage VM |
| **Dell** (PowerMax, PowerStore, PowerScale) | Data at rest encryption with SEDs or controller-based encryption, depending on platform | Embedded key manager, with external KMIP key managers supported on several platforms |
| **Pure Storage FlashArray** | Always-on AES-256 encryption with internal layered keys, no option to disable | Internal by default; an external KMIP server can be required to unlock the array at power-on (Rapid Data Locking) |
| **HPE, IBM, Hitachi** | Comparable SED and controller-based options | Internal key managers plus KMIP to external managers |

Notice the different meanings of "external key manager". On some platforms the external server stores the volume keys themselves; on others it holds only an extra unlock key and the working keys stay inside the array. Both make a stolen array unreadable, but only the first lets you destroy a single volume's key centrally. Read the vendor's key hierarchy, not just the word "KMIP" on a datasheet.

### Design decisions for arrays

- **Put the key manager in a different failure domain.** A key manager running as a VM on the array it unlocks cannot start until the array is unlocked. NetApp's guidance is to host external key servers on a different storage system from the data. This "chicken and egg" outage is a common self-inflicted failure.
- **Test cold starts.** Arrays cache keys while running, so a key manager outage is harmless until the array reboots. Include a whole-site cold start in disaster recovery tests.
- **Plan key manager changes.** Moving between onboard and external key managers can require decrypting or moving volumes on some platforms.

> **At Meridian.** DC-East and DC-West each run a pair of HSM-backed enterprise key managers, clustered across both sites, serving KMIP to the storage arrays, the backup system and the hypervisors. Platform Engineering owns the arrays; the PKI and Cryptography Services team owns the key managers and issues each array a client certificate from the internal CA for KMIP authentication. The yearly site-recovery test includes a cold boot of one data centre's arrays with the local key managers deliberately offline.

## 26.6 Virtual machine encryption: vSphere and Hyper-V

Virtualisation creates a new copy problem: a virtual machine is a set of files that any storage or backup administrator can copy and start elsewhere. **VM encryption** encrypts those files (virtual disks, configuration, swap) so that a copied VM is useless without the hypervisor's keys.

### VMware vSphere

vSphere VM encryption uses a two-level hierarchy. Each VM's data is encrypted with a **data encryption key** generated by the ESXi host, and that DEK is wrapped by a **key encryption key** from a **key provider**. vSphere offers three kinds, as of 2026:

- **Standard Key Provider:** an external key server reached over KMIP. vCenter obtains keys and distributes them to hosts.
- **Trusted Key Provider:** part of vSphere Trust Authority, in which hosts get keys only after remote attestation of their boot state, and vCenter is not involved.
- **Native Key Provider:** introduced in vSphere 7.0 Update 2, it lets vCenter generate and distribute keys without any external server, ideally to hosts with TPM 2.0.

The same providers serve **vSAN encryption** and **virtual TPMs**. Since 7.0 Update 2, hosts can optionally persist keys so encrypted VMs restart while the key server is unavailable, a deliberate trade of security for availability.

### Microsoft Hyper-V

Hyper-V takes a different approach. Instead of the hypervisor encrypting VM files, a **virtual TPM** is given to each Generation 2 VM and the guest runs **BitLocker** (or LUKS) inside itself. **Shielded VMs** extended this with a Host Guardian Service that released keys only to attested hosts, but Microsoft stated with Windows Server 2022 that guarded fabric and shielded VMs would receive no further development, and its direction for isolation from the host is confidential computing (Chapter 30). Nutanix, Proxmox and cloud hypervisors offer similar variants.

The architectural choice is the same everywhere: **hypervisor-level encryption** is managed centrally and is invisible to guests, but every hypervisor administrator can still read the VMs; **guest-level encryption** keeps keys inside the guest, but must be managed per operating system.

## 26.7 Backup and tape

Backups concentrate an organisation's data into a few systems and keep it for years, which makes them an attractive target for theft and for ransomware operators, who now routinely try to destroy backups before encrypting production.

### Backup software encryption

Enterprise backup products (Veeam, Commvault, Rubrik, Cohesity, Veritas NetBackup and others) can encrypt at the source agent, in transit and at rest on the target. Two issues dominate. First, encryption defeats deduplication, so most products deduplicate and then encrypt unique blocks, which means the backup system sees plaintext. Second, key retention outlives everything: a backup kept seven years needs its key available and restorable for seven years, so the key manager's own backups join the recovery chain.

### Tape and LTO

**LTO** tape drives have included hardware encryption since LTO-4, using **AES-256 in GCM mode**, applied after compression so that capacity is unaffected. GCM gives tape something disks lack: each block carries an authentication tag, so tampering is detected on read. The drive needs a key for each tape, delivered by one of three methods:

| Method | Who supplies the key | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Application-managed** | The backup software, from its own key store or a KMS | Simple deployments; keys travel with the backup catalogue |
| **Library-managed** | The tape library, from an external key manager over KMIP or a vendor protocol | Enterprises separating key custody from backup administrators |
| **System-managed** | The host operating system or driver | Mainframe and some midrange environments |

Off-site tapes are the classic reason for this control, and in many lost-tape incidents the drive could encrypt but nobody had turned it on.

> **Watch out.** Encryption stops a thief reading backups, but not an administrator deleting them; that needs **immutability** (object lock, write-once tape, isolated vaults). And an attacker who can delete your backup encryption keys destroys every backup at once, so protect key deletion with the same quorum controls as backup deletion.

## 26.8 Cloud volume and object storage encryption

All three hyperscalers encrypt stored data by default. What you choose is **who controls the key**, and the options line up closely across providers:

| Option | AWS | Microsoft Azure | Google Cloud |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Provider-managed keys** (default) | SSE-S3 for S3; AWS-managed keys for EBS and others | Microsoft-managed keys for Storage and managed disks | Google default encryption |
| **Customer-managed keys in the provider KMS** | SSE-KMS, and DSSE-KMS (two layers) | Customer-managed keys in Key Vault or Managed HSM | CMEK in Cloud KMS |
| **Customer-provided key per request** | SSE-C (S3) | Customer-provided keys (Blob Storage) | CSEK (Cloud Storage, Compute Engine) |
| **Keys outside the provider** | KMS External Key Store | Managed HSM with your own key, or partner integrations | Cloud EKM |
| **Client-side encryption** | AWS Encryption SDK, S3 encryption client | Azure SDK client-side encryption | Tink, client libraries |

How these work underneath is envelope encryption (Chapter 32): the storage service encrypts each object or volume with its own DEK and asks the KMS to wrap the DEK under your key. Chapter 28 covers the KMS side, including external key options; this section concerns the storage choices.

Several details catch teams out:

- **Default encryption is not a control you own.** Since January 2023, Amazon S3 applies SSE-S3 to every new object, and Azure and Google Cloud have long encrypted by default. That satisfies "encrypted at rest" on paper but gives you no policy, no separate audit trail and no way to make data unreadable.
- **Customer-managed keys add real controls:** who may decrypt, a log of every use, and the ability to disable the key. For busy S3 buckets, enable **S3 Bucket Keys** with SSE-KMS to reduce KMS calls and cost.
- **Customer-provided keys are a sharp tool.** With SSE-C, CSEK or Azure customer-provided keys, you send the key with every request and the provider does not store it. Lose the key and the data is gone. AWS disabled SSE-C by default for new buckets from April 2026, and recommends keeping it disabled unless a workload specifically needs it.
- **Volumes need explicit settings.** Turn on EBS encryption by default per account and region, use customer-managed keys for managed disks in Azure, and set organisation policies in Google Cloud requiring CMEK for chosen services. In Azure, prefer server-side encryption with customer-managed keys, or encryption at host, over the older guest-based Azure Disk Encryption, which Microsoft has announced it will retire.

> **At Meridian.** Platform Engineering enforces customer-managed keys for all production storage in all three clouds through policy as code: S3 bucket policies that deny uploads without SSE-KMS, Azure Policy definitions that require customer-managed keys on storage accounts and disks, and Google Cloud organisation policies that restrict which services may run without CMEK. SSE-C and customer-supplied keys are blocked at the organisation level; an exception requires the PKI and Cryptography Services team's sign-off.

## 26.9 Design decisions, operations and compliance

For each class of data, an architect decides: which threats matter (lost media needs only the low layers; insiders need Chapter 27); where keys live (built-in managers are simpler, external ones give separation of duties and per-dataset destruction); what happens when the key system is down (every encryption design is also an availability design); how keys will be recovered in ten years; and how media will be sanitised (cryptographic erase works only if every copy of the key is destroyed).

Operational signals worth monitoring include devices without escrowed recovery keys, arrays or hosts that cannot reach their key manager, KMIP certificate expiry (a classic outage source, covered in Chapter 17), keys scheduled for deletion, and storage created without customer-managed keys.

> **Compliance hook.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 Requirement 3.5.1.2 limits disk-level and partition-level encryption: it is acceptable on its own for rendering stored PAN unreadable only on removable media, and on non-removable media PAN must also be protected by another method that meets Requirement 3.5.1. This is the standard's way of saying that FDE does not protect a running system. NIST SP 800-111 gives guidance on storage encryption for end-user devices, NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 covers sanitisation and cryptographic erase, ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control A.8.24 requires a managed use of cryptography, and GDPR Article 32 names encryption as an appropriate measure.

## 26.10 Lab: LUKS2 volume encryption on a loop device

This lab runs on any Linux machine, or a Linux VM or container with privileges, using `cryptsetup`. It creates an encrypted volume in a file, so it cannot damage your real disks.

1. Create a 64 MiB file and attach it as a loop device:

    ```bash
    truncate -s 64M lab.img
    LOOP=$(sudo losetup --find --show lab.img)
    echo "$LOOP"
    ```

2. Format it with LUKS2 and inspect the header. Note the cipher (`aes-xts-plain64`), the key size (512 bits, which is two AES-256 keys for XTS) and the Argon2id parameters on key slot 0:

    ```bash
    sudo cryptsetup luksFormat --type luks2 "$LOOP"
    sudo cryptsetup luksDump "$LOOP"
    ```

3. Open the volume, create a file system and write a recognisable string:

    ```bash
    sudo cryptsetup open "$LOOP" labvol
    sudo mkfs.ext4 -q /dev/mapper/labvol
    sudo mount /dev/mapper/labvol /mnt
    echo "MERIDIAN-SECRET" | sudo tee /mnt/note.txt
    sudo umount /mnt
    sudo cryptsetup close labvol
    ```

4. Search the raw image for the string. It should not be found:

    ```bash
    grep -c MERIDIAN-SECRET lab.img
    ```

5. Add a second passphrase as a "recovery key" in another slot, then back up the header:

    ```bash
    sudo cryptsetup luksAddKey "$LOOP"
    sudo cryptsetup luksHeaderBackup "$LOOP" \
      --header-backup-file lab-header.bin
    ```

6. Perform a cryptographic erase by destroying all key slots, then try to open the volume with either passphrase:

    ```bash
    sudo cryptsetup luksErase "$LOOP"
    sudo cryptsetup open "$LOOP" labvol
    ```

7. Restore the header from the backup and open the volume again. Reflect on what this means: a header backup is a copy of the wrapped key, so a cryptographic erase is only as complete as your control over every header backup.

    ```bash
    sudo cryptsetup luksHeaderRestore "$LOOP" \
      --header-backup-file lab-header.bin
    sudo cryptsetup open "$LOOP" labvol
    ```

8. Clean up with `sudo cryptsetup close labvol` and `sudo losetup -d "$LOOP"`.

**Optional cloud extension.** In an AWS, Azure or Google Cloud free-tier account, create a storage bucket, upload one object with provider-managed keys and one with a customer-managed key, and compare the metadata and the KMS audit log entries for each. Then disable the customer-managed key and try to read both objects. *Cost warning:* customer-managed keys carry a monthly charge per key and per-request charges; delete or schedule deletion of the key and remove the bucket when you finish.

## 26.11 Failure story: self-encrypting deception

The research in section 26.4 is the clearest documented case of storage encryption failing silently at scale. Organisations had enabled BitLocker on laptops with SED-capable SSDs and reported "full-disk encryption: 100 percent" to their boards. On many of those machines BitLocker had handed encryption to drive firmware whose password check could be bypassed, because the media key was not derived from the password.

Nothing in the management consoles showed a problem. The weakness was in a layer nobody had looked at, because a higher-layer product had delegated to it silently. The fix was configuration: a Group Policy forcing software encryption, then full decryption and re-encryption, since the policy applies only when BitLocker is first enabled. Organisations with good endpoint inventories found affected devices in hours; those without spent months.

The lesson: **know which component actually performs your encryption and derives your keys**, prefer components whose key handling has been independently validated, and treat "encryption enabled" as the start of assurance, not the end.

## 26.12 Chapter summary

- Storage encryption protects against people who obtain the media without the running system, not against authorised users of the system.
- Place every control on the five-layer stack and state which threat it removes.
- BitLocker, FileVault and LUKS share a two-level hierarchy: a volume master key wrapped by protectors. Escrow and measure recovery keys.
- Self-encrypting drives offer free encryption and instant cryptographic erase, but the 2018 research showed that unvalidated firmware can make them worthless; verify which layer is doing the work.
- Arrays encrypt at the drive or controller level, with keys from a built-in or external KMIP key manager; keep the key manager in a separate failure domain and test cold starts.
- VM encryption is either hypervisor-level (vSphere key providers) or guest-level (vTPM plus BitLocker or LUKS), trading central management against administrator exposure.
- Backup and LTO tape encryption depend on keys that must outlive long retention periods, and backup key deletion must be protected like backup deletion.
- Cloud storage is encrypted by default; the real choice is between provider-managed, customer-managed, customer-provided and external keys, enforced by policy as code.
