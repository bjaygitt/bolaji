## 42.1 Thinking in systems

A **cryptographic system** is more than its algorithms. It includes:

- the **keys**, and where they live (KMS, HSM, files, devices)
- the **identities** that may use each key (people, workloads, services)
- the **protocols** that carry protected data
- the **trust anchors** that decide what is believed (root certificates, JWKS endpoints, pinned keys)
- the **processes** around them: issuance, rotation, approval, backup, monitoring and recovery

Attackers look for the weakest link across all of these, and it is almost never the mathematics. The Storm-0558 signing key (Chapter 10) and the expired certificate that blinded Equifax's inspection device (Chapter 17) were failures of process and ownership, not of algorithms.

**Governance** is the set of decisions that stops such failures recurring: who decides which algorithms are allowed, who checks designs, who may break the rules and for how long, and who replaces everything when the rules change. **Architecture** is how those decisions show up in real systems. A policy that no system can follow is fiction, and an architecture with no policy behind it drifts.

## 42.2 The document hierarchy: policy, standards, patterns and procedures

Most organisations write cryptographic rules in four layers, kept separate because each changes at a different speed and needs a different approval.

| Layer | What it says | Who approves | How often it changes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Policy** | Principles: cryptography protects data by classification, keys are managed through their lifecycle, exceptions need approval | Board risk committee or CISO | Every one to three years |
| **Standard** | Testable rules: approved algorithms, key sizes, protocol versions, key storage tiers, maximum lifetimes | Crypto centre of excellence, with GRC sign-off | At least annually |
| **Pattern** | Reusable designs: encrypting a database field, service-to-service authentication, signing a release | Architecture review board | As needed |
| **Procedure** | Step-by-step runbooks: requesting a certificate, running a key ceremony | Owning team | When tooling changes |

The **policy** should be short and stable. If it names AES-256 or a TLS version, it will need board approval every time the industry moves, so leave specifics to the standard. The **standard** is where auditors test compliance, so every statement in it should be checkable: "TLS 1.2 or later, with forward-secret key exchange" can be tested with a scanner; "strong encryption" cannot. **Patterns** make the standard usable: a developer wants a library call and a design they know will pass review, not a treatise on key wrapping.

> **In practice.** Write the standard so that most of it can be enforced as code: a TLS baseline in load balancer templates, a cloud organisation policy that requires customer-managed keys, a CLM template that refuses RSA keys below 3072 bits. Rules in templates and pipelines are followed by default; rules in a document are followed by people who read documents.

## 42.3 The approved-algorithm list

The heart of the standard is the **approved-algorithm list**: a table of the algorithms, parameters and protocols that systems may use, with a status for each. A good list does three things. It tells new designs what to use. It tells existing systems what they must move away from and by when. And it gives auditors and scanners something precise to test against.

### Status categories

A simple scheme with four statuses works well:

- **Approved:** use freely in new and existing systems.
- **Approved with conditions:** allowed only in stated contexts, for example "RSA-2048 for certificates expiring before 2030" or "AES-CBC only with an HMAC under encrypt-then-MAC".
- **Deprecated:** existing use tolerated until a sunset date; no new use; an exception is needed after the date.
- **Prohibited:** must not be used; any discovery is an incident or an exception.

A short extract from Meridian's 2026 list shows the shape:

| Function | Approved | Approved with conditions | Deprecated | Prohibited |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetric encryption | AES-256-GCM, AES-128-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305 | AES-XTS (storage only), AES-CBC with HMAC (legacy formats) | 3DES (payment keys only, sunset per Payments Security plan) | DES, RC4, ECB for data |
| Hash | SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512, SHA3 | SHA-1 in HMAC or non-security checksums | | MD5, SHA-1 for signatures |
| Key exchange | X25519MLKEM768 (preferred), ECDHE P-256 and P-384, X25519 | DHE with groups of 3072 bits or more | RSA key transport | Static DH, export groups |
| Signatures | ECDSA P-256 and P-384, Ed25519, RSA-PSS 3072+, ML-DSA-65 for new internal signing | RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 3072+ for certificates | RSA-2048 (sunset 2030) | RSA below 2048, DSA |
| Protocols | TLS 1.3, TLS 1.2 with AEAD and forward secrecy, SSH with modern key exchange, IKEv2 | | TLS 1.2 with CBC suites | SSL, TLS 1.0, TLS 1.1, IKEv1 aggressive mode |

Notice what the list leaves out: product names. It names algorithms and parameters, so the same rule applies to the cloud KMS, the HSM clusters, the CLM platform and an application library. Chapter 45 explains the post-quantum entries, and Chapter 40 explains why "approved" should normally also mean "implemented in a validated module" for regulated workloads.

### Where the list comes from

Base the list on public authorities and record which one supports each entry: NIST SP 800-131A for transition dates, NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 for key sizes and crypto-periods, CNSA 2.0 where national security customers are involved, and national guidance (such as the UK NCSC's) where you operate. Payment cryptography follows PCI and ANSI X9 rules (Chapters 38 and 39). Where authorities disagree, pick the stricter one for new systems and record why.

> **Watch out.** An algorithm list with no sunset dates is a museum. Every deprecated entry needs a date, a migration owner and a measure of remaining use. Otherwise "deprecated" quietly becomes "permitted forever", which is how SHA-1 and TLS 1.0 survived inside enterprises long after the public internet dropped them.

## 42.4 The crypto centre of excellence

Someone has to own the policy, the standard and the list, and give teams the help they need to follow them. Many organisations create a **crypto centre of excellence (CCoE)**: a small group of cryptographic specialists who set standards, run shared services and advise everyone else. It is a role, not necessarily a separate department; at Meridian it is the PKI and Cryptography Services team plus named experts from Payments Security and IAM.

### What the CCoE does

- **Owns the standard and the approved-algorithm list**, reviewing them at least annually and whenever an event demands it.
- **Runs shared services**: private CAs, the CLM platform, general-purpose HSM clusters and key management integration. This is its strongest lever: a team that gets a compliant certificate in one API call has no reason to build its own CA.
- **Publishes patterns and libraries**, and **reviews designs** (section 42.5).
- **Advises on exceptions**, owns the cryptographic inventory and its metrics (Chapter 43), and leads the technical side of cryptographic incidents.
- **Tracks the outside world**: standards, CA/Browser Forum ballots, vulnerability disclosures and vendor roadmaps.

### Operating models

There are three common shapes. A **centralised** model, where one team owns every key and CA, is consistent and easy to audit but becomes a bottleneck. A **federated** model, where each business owns its cryptography under a common standard, is fast but drifts. A **hub-and-spoke** model has a central CCoE that sets standards and runs shared platforms, with named **crypto champions** in each team applying them; it scales well, provided champions have time and authority.

Meridian runs hub and spoke. Chapter 2 gave its operational RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed). Governance activities need their own, because the accountable party is usually a risk owner rather than an engineer:

| Governance activity | CCoE | GRC | CISO | Architecture board | System owner | Internal audit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cryptographic policy | R | C | A | C | I | I |
| Cryptographic standard and algorithm list | A, R | C | I | C | I | I |
| Design review of a new system | R | I | I | A | C | I |
| Exception request | C | R | I | I | A | I |
| Exception approval above high risk | C | R | A | I | C | I |
| Annual cipher and protocol review | R | A | I | I | C | I |
| Independent testing of controls | C | C | I | I | C | A, R |

Notice that the system owner, not the CCoE, is accountable for exceptions in their own system. The CCoE advises; it does not accept risk on behalf of the business.

> **At Meridian.** The CCoE has eleven engineers and a lead architect who reports to the CISO. Each of the roughly 60 application teams names a crypto champion, who attends a monthly forum and is the first reviewer for their team's designs. The champions are the main reason Meridian's TLS 1.0 removal took nine months rather than the three years first estimated: each owned the affected systems in their team, while the CCoE handled the shared load balancers and CDN.

## 42.5 Design reviews and exceptions

### Design review

A **cryptographic design review** checks a new or changed system against the standard before it is built. It should be proportionate: a static marketing site needs a checkbox; a payment authorisation service needs a meeting. Meridian triggers a full review when a system handles restricted data, holds its own keys, introduces a trust anchor or uses a protocol outside the published patterns. The review asks six fixed questions:

1. What data is protected, and at what classification?
2. Which keys exist, where are they generated and stored, who can use them, and how are they rotated?
3. Which algorithms and protocols are used, and are they all on the approved list?
4. Which trust anchors does the system accept, and who can change them?
5. How would the system change algorithm or key without a code release?
6. What happens if each key leaks, and how would we detect it?

Question 5 is the agility question, and it is the one teams most often cannot answer. Make it mandatory.

### Exceptions

No standard fits every system: a vendor appliance may only accept RSA-2048, or a payment scheme may still require 3DES for one key type. An **exception** is a documented, approved, time-limited permission to depart from the standard. Without a formal process, the same departures happen anyway, silently.

A workable exception record contains:

- the rule broken, the system affected and the reason
- compensating controls (network isolation, shorter key lifetime, extra monitoring)
- a GRC risk rating and a named **risk owner** in the business, not the engineer who asked
- an **expiry date**, normally within 12 months, and a remediation plan

Expired exceptions should escalate automatically, and renewal should require evidence of remediation progress, not just a new signature.

> **In practice.** Keep exceptions in the same system as the cryptographic inventory, linked to the affected assets. A scanner finding of "RSA-2048 on host X" is then matched to its exception automatically, the dashboard shows only unapproved deviations, and teams stop ignoring noisy results.

## 42.6 Threat modelling cryptographic systems

Design reviews need a method for finding weaknesses, and threat modelling provides it. A simple, effective method for cryptographic systems has five steps:

1. **Draw the data flow.** Show where sensitive data is created, stored, transmitted and processed. Mark each **trust boundary**: a point where data crosses between systems, networks or organisations with different levels of trust.
2. **List the keys.** For each protection on the diagram, identify the key, where it lives, who can use it and how it is rotated.
3. **Ask the key questions at every boundary:**
    - Who can decrypt this data in practice, including administrators, cloud providers and backups?
    - Who can sign or issue something that others will trust?
    - If this key leaks, how would we know, and how fast could we replace it?
    - What does this trust anchor accept, and who can change it?
4. **Apply a threat catalogue.** STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege) is a good checklist. Spoofing maps to authentication and certificates, tampering to integrity and signatures, repudiation to signing and audit logs, information disclosure to encryption and key access, denial of service to expiry and revocation infrastructure, and elevation of privilege to who controls the keys.
5. **Prioritise by blast radius.** A key that protects one record matters less than one that can mint every identity in the organisation.

### Blast radius as a design principle

**Blast radius** is how much is affected if one thing fails. Many recommendations in this book are simply ways of reducing it:

| Design choice | Blast radius reduced |
| --- | --- |
| Per-object DEKs under envelope encryption | One leaked key exposes one object |
| Per-tenant or per-application KEKs | One misconfigured policy exposes one tenant |
| Separate issuing CAs by purpose | One compromised CA affects one certificate type |
| Name constraints on CAs | A stolen CA key cannot issue for other domains |
| Short-lived credentials and certificates | A leaked credential works for hours, not years |
| Ephemeral key exchange | A stolen server key does not expose past sessions |
| Dual control on root and payment keys | One insider cannot act alone |

Blast radius also guides spending. Meridian's offline root CAs, payment HSM master keys and terminal firmware signing keys sit behind ceremonies, dual control and dedicated hardware, because one failure there would affect every device or transaction. A certificate on an internal dashboard gets automation and monitoring, but no ceremony.

> **At Meridian.** A threat model of the open banking API platform found that the partner mTLS trust store on the API gateway accepted any certificate issued by two public CAs, not just certificates registered to onboarded partners. Anyone who bought a certificate from either CA could complete the TLS handshake. Application-level checks still blocked access, but the trust boundary was far wider than intended. The fix pinned each partner to its registered certificate and moved trust store changes into the same change process as firewall rules.

## 42.7 Crypto agility

**Crypto agility** is the ability to change algorithms, keys, certificates or cryptographic providers quickly and safely, without redesigning systems. Every major cryptographic event of the past two decades has tested it: SHA-1's retirement, the removal of TLS 1.0 and 1.1, ROCA (Chapter 8), Debian's weak keys, mass revocations, CA distrusts and, now, the post-quantum transition, which replaces nearly all public-key cryptography (Chapter 46). Agility has three layers, and an organisation is only as agile as its weakest one.

### Layer 1: protocol and format agility

Protocols should negotiate algorithms, carry an algorithm identifier with every key, signature and ciphertext, and allow new options without breaking old clients. TLS 1.3 is the model: adding the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange needed only a new named group, with no change to the protocol itself, which is why browsers could deploy it widely within a year. X.509 certificates, JOSE tokens and CMS messages all carry algorithm identifiers for the same reason.

Stored data needs the same property: every encrypted object should record which algorithm and key version protected it, so it can be re-protected later. Cloud KMS envelope formats and libraries such as Tink do this by default; hand-built formats often do not.

Agility has a cost: every negotiable option is attack surface, as downgrade attacks such as FREAK and Logjam showed. IETF guidance on algorithm agility says the same: keep the ability to change, but keep the enabled set small. The modern balance is **agile, but with few strong choices at any time**, and deliberate retirement of old ones.

### Layer 2: application and platform agility

Applications become rigid when algorithm choices are hard-coded throughout the code. Make them agile by:

- calling cryptography through a small number of internal libraries, platform services or a KMS, never directly from business code
- keeping algorithm, key size and key location in configuration
- avoiding assumptions about sizes: ML-DSA-65 signatures are over 3,300 bytes, compared with 64 for Ed25519 (Chapter 45), so fixed-length database columns, protocol fields, headers and hardware buffers will break
- testing with alternative algorithms in continuous integration, so that a switch is a configuration change already proven to work

Platforms matter as much as code. A load balancer that cannot load a new algorithm, or an HSM with no firmware path to ML-DSA, blocks every system behind it, which is why agility belongs in procurement (Chapter 43).

### Layer 3: organisational agility

The hardest layer is organisational, because it depends on knowledge and habits rather than code:

- an accurate **inventory** of keys, certificates and cryptographic use, ideally in a machine-readable cryptography bill of materials (Chapter 43)
- an **owner** for every cryptographic asset
- **automation** for issuance, rotation and deployment (Chapters 17 to 19)
- **rehearsed procedures** for mass replacement
- **procurement rules** that require vendors to support algorithm upgrades and publish timelines
- **governance that can decide quickly**: a CCoE with the authority to change the algorithm list in days when an algorithm breaks, not at the next annual review

A useful single measure of organisational agility is **time to replace**: how long it would take to rotate every key or certificate of a given class, proven by rehearsal (Chapter 43).

> **Current development.** In 2025 NIST released a Cybersecurity White Paper for comment, *Considerations for Achieving Cryptographic Agility* (CSWP 39). It treats agility across protocols, applications, platforms and governance, much as this section does. Check its current status before citing it in a policy.

## 42.8 Compliance hooks

> **Compliance hook.** Auditors test the rules before the systems. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control 8.24, "Use of cryptography", expects a cryptography policy and key management rules: sections 42.2 and 42.3 are the evidence. PCI DSS v4.0.1 Requirement 12.3.3 asks for a documented review, at least every 12 months, of the cipher suites and protocols in use, with a strategy for responding to anticipated cryptographic vulnerabilities: the annual algorithm-list review and its sunset dates satisfy it. DORA's RTS on ICT risk management (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1774) requires an encryption and cryptographic controls policy and updates to cryptographic technology as cryptanalysis develops. NIST SP 800-53 controls SC-12 and SC-13 expect the organisation to define the cryptography it requires. Chapter 41 gives the full crosswalk.

Compliance is the floor. A system can pass an audit with keys in files readable by every administrator. Design to the threat model, then show compliance.

## 42.9 Lab: enforce an algorithm standard with OpenSSL and test it

In this lab you turn a line of an approved-algorithm list into enforced configuration, then prove the enforcement works, which is the same evidence an auditor would ask for. You need OpenSSL 3.x on Linux or macOS.

1. Create a working directory and a test certificate:

```bash
mkdir crypto-std && cd crypto-std
openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 \
  -nodes -keyout key.pem -out cert.pem -days 30 -subj "/CN=localhost"
```

2. Write a configuration that applies the standard: TLS 1.2 minimum, AEAD and forward-secret suites only.

```bash
cat > strict.cnf <<'EOF'
openssl_conf = openssl_init
[openssl_init]
ssl_conf = ssl_sect
[ssl_sect]
system_default = system_default_sect
[system_default_sect]
MinProtocol = TLSv1.2
CipherString = ECDHE+AESGCM:ECDHE+CHACHA20
Ciphersuites = TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384:TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256
EOF
```

3. Start a server that uses it, in a second terminal:

```bash
OPENSSL_CONF=./strict.cnf openssl s_server -accept 8443 \
  -cert cert.pem -key key.pem -www
```

4. Test a compliant client. This should succeed and show TLS 1.3:

```bash
openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 -brief < /dev/null
```

5. Test non-compliant clients. Each should fail the handshake:

```bash
openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 -tls1_1 < /dev/null
openssl s_client -connect localhost:8443 -tls1_2 \
  -cipher AES128-SHA < /dev/null
```

6. Write a short evidence note: the rule, the configuration file and where it is versioned, the commands and their results.
7. Now threat model the setup. Draw the data flow, list the key, apply STRIDE, and answer the agility question: what would you change to add a post-quantum hybrid group, and does your OpenSSL version support it? (OpenSSL 3.5 and later include ML-KEM; run `openssl list -kem-algorithms` to check.)
8. Write a mock exception for a legacy client that needs TLS 1.1.

**Optional cloud extension.** Apply the same rule as a TLS policy on a cloud load balancer (for example, a predefined security policy on AWS Application Load Balancer, an SSL policy on Azure Application Gateway or a Google Cloud SSL policy), then repeat steps 4 and 5 against it. Load balancers incur hourly charges; delete them when you finish.

## 42.10 Failure story: the update server everyone trusted

In June 2017, a destructive malware outbreak known as NotPetya spread across the world, causing an estimated 10 billion US dollars in damage. It began with the update server of M.E.Doc, a widely used Ukrainian accounting software package. Attackers had compromised the vendor's infrastructure and pushed a malicious update that customers' systems installed automatically, because it arrived through the trusted update channel.

Cryptography was not broken. It was bypassed by an architecture that concentrated trust in one weakly protected place. Once the attackers controlled the vendor's servers, every customer trusted what they delivered. Inside victim networks, the malware then spread using stolen administrator credentials, the second half of the blast radius problem.

The lessons are architectural. Sign updates with hardware-held keys kept apart from the distribution servers, and verify signatures on the client. Treat a supplier's update channel as a trust anchor in your threat model, with an owner. Segment networks, and protect administrative credentials as the high-value keys they are. A design review that had asked "who can sign something we will trust, and what happens if they are compromised?" would have flagged every one of these.

## 42.11 Chapter summary

- Attackers seek the weakest link across keys, identities, protocols, trust anchors and processes, and it is rarely the algorithm.
- Write cryptographic rules in four layers: a stable policy, a testable standard, reusable patterns and team procedures.
- The approved-algorithm list names algorithms and parameters, not products, gives each a status, and puts a sunset date and owner on everything deprecated.
- A crypto centre of excellence owns the standard, runs shared services and advises; in a hub-and-spoke model, champions in each team apply the rules.
- System owners, not the CCoE, are accountable for exceptions in their systems; every exception has compensating controls, a risk owner and an expiry date.
- Threat model by drawing data flows and trust boundaries, listing keys, asking who can decrypt or sign, applying STRIDE and prioritising by blast radius.
- Crypto agility has three layers (protocol and format, application and platform, organisational), and the organisational layer is usually the slowest.
